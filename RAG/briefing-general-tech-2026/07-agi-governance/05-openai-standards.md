# OpenAI: 'Building standards for the next phase of AI'

<a id="g08-5"></a>
### 8.5 OpenAI: "Building standards for the next phase of AI"

On **Monday, September 21, 2026**, OpenAI published a blog post titled
**"Building standards for the next phase of AI"**, timed for the margins
of the UN General Assembly's high-level week. It was covered the same day
by Reuters (Michelle Nichols) and AFP. The post is OpenAI's institutional
governance statement of the autumn — the company's answer, in its own
voice, to the summer of AGI proclamations, Amodei's pacing essay, and
the Hugging Face incident of July (see §8.6).

An attribution correction is required at the outset, because several
secondary accounts simplified it. The post is an **institutional
position from OpenAI's Global Affairs team** — it is not personally
attributed to Sam Altman, and no source attributes it to him. Formulations
like "Altman called for…" are an inaccurate simplification: the asks in
the document are the company's, expressed through its policy apparatus,
and the distinction matters because an institutional position binds the
company in a way a CEO's interview remark does not. Where the post's
language is quoted below, it is OpenAI speaking.

Two verified quotes carry the post's geopolitical argument. First: **"Leading
now will determine whether the United States shapes the global AI
framework or watches a fragmented, uneven, and conflict-ridden system
take hold around it."** Second: **"The United States is well positioned
to lead because its AI industry is at the technical frontier."** The
logic is explicit and sequential: American technical leadership is the
premise, and global framework-shaping is the prize for acting now; the
alternative is fragmentation, unevenness, and conflict. It is a bid for
US-led international governance, stated as a warning about what happens
without it.

The concrete asks are threefold: **complementary national and
international frontier standards, common measurements, and incident
reporting protocols.** Each term is doing deliberate work.
"Complementary" national *and* international standards rejects both a
purely domestic regime and a purely supranational one; "common
measurements" is the epistemic prerequisite — without agreed metrics,
standards are slogans; "incident reporting protocols" is the operational
plumbing, and it echoes directly the July Hugging Face incident that the
post itself invokes (see §8.6).

What the post does *not* ask for is as significant as what it does. The
standards it proposes are **explicitly non-binding**: there is no call
for licensing, no pre-market review, no enforcement mechanism. This is
the softest form of "standards" that still deserves the name — closer to
a coordination proposal than a regulatory one. The post also **rejects a
unilateral US regime** and describes the **US–China dialogue as a
"positive step"** — language that, published one day after the
Bessent–He Lifeng talks in New York (§8.8), reads as institutional
endorsement of the diplomatic track from the company most exposed to its
outcome.

The overall posture is therefore: American-led, internationally
complementary, non-binding, measurement-first, and diplomatically
engaged with Beijing. It is the maximal institutional ambition
expressible without asking anyone — including OpenAI itself — to submit
to a binding rule. Whether that is statesmanship or self-protection is
the question the 22-country declaration, adopted the same day (§8.7),
implicitly poses in return.

The post's two verified quotes repay a slower reading, because they
encode a theory of geopolitical order. "Leading now will determine
whether the United States shapes the global AI framework or watches a
fragmented, uneven, and conflict-ridden system take hold around it" is
a sentence built on a forced choice: shape or watch, order or
fragmentation, with no third option on offer. The adjectives attached
to the alternative — *fragmented, uneven, conflict-ridden* — do the
persuasive work: they describe not a neutral multipolar outcome but a
deterioration, which makes American leadership look like prevention
rather than ambition. The second quote — "The United States is well
positioned to lead because its AI industry is at the technical
frontier" — supplies the premise the first quote needs: leadership is
not claimed as a right but derived from a fact, the frontier position
of the American industry. The logic is circular only if you doubt the
premise; if you accept that the US industry is at the frontier, the
conclusion follows with the force of a syllogism. That is the sentence's
design: to make American-led governance feel like recognition of
reality rather than an exercise of power.

"Complementary national and international frontier standards" is the
post's most carefully engineered phrase, and it is engineered to
refuse two futures at once. Against the unilateralists — those who
would have Washington write the rules alone — it insists on the
international complement. Against the supranationalists — those who
would lodge authority in a single global body — it insists on the
national complement. What remains is a layered architecture in which
no single layer is sovereign: the American layer proposes, the
international layer harmonizes, and neither commands. It is, notably,
the exact inverse of the 22-country declaration's "explore a
verification institution" (§8.7): where the declaration reaches for a
body that could one day verify and therefore bind, OpenAI's
complementarity is designed to make bindingness structurally
difficult. The two documents, published the same day, propose
incompatible theories of how rules get authority — and they were
written, respectively, by the company being governed and the countries
doing the governing.

The non-binding character of the whole proposal should be stated
without euphemism, because it is the post's most consequential
feature. No licensing. No pre-market review. No enforcement. What is
proposed is measurement infrastructure (common measurements),
coordination infrastructure (complementary standards), and
information infrastructure (incident reporting) — the scaffolding of
governance with the governance left out. There are two ways to read
this, and a reference dossier should record both. The charitable
reading: binding regimes require the epistemic and institutional
preconditions the post proposes to build, and OpenAI is sequencing
correctly — measure first, standardize second, enforce later. The
skeptical reading: a frontier lab proposing non-binding standards for
itself is proposing to be governed in the only way that does not
constrain it, and the elaborate architecture is the price of the
proposal's acceptability to governments. The post itself does not
adjudicate between these readings. Neither, as of September 22, has
anyone else.
