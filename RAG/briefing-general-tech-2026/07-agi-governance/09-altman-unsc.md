# Altman at the UN Security Council (planned 23/09)

<a id="g08-9"></a>
### 8.9 Altman at the UN Security Council (planned 23/09)

The week's most symbolically charged governance event had, as of
September 22, **not yet happened** — and this chapter records it as
planned, not accomplished. On **September 18, 2026**, an **OpenAI
spokesperson** announced that **Sam Altman** would brief the **UN
Security Council** on **Wednesday, September 23, 2026**, as reported by
Reuters. The session is an **open meeting on "AI and international
security,"** convened by **France** in its capacity as holder of the
**rotating presidency** of the Council, and chaired by Foreign Minister
**Jean-Noël Barrot**.

The format matters. A Security Council briefing is not a General
Assembly side event: it places AI inside the UN's peace-and-security
architecture, the body charged with threats to international peace. The
announced theme — "AI and international security" — frames the
technology as a security question first and an economic or scientific
one second, which is a significant institutional choice by the French
presidency. An open meeting means the briefing is public, on the
record, and addressed to the fifteen Council members with the world
watching — including, notably, the United States and China, the two
absentees from the previous day's declaration (§8.7), both of them
permanent members of the body Altman is scheduled to address.

**Anthropic's participation was "hoped for but unconfirmed"** as of
September 22 — the phrasing in circulation indicates that an invitation
or expectation existed but no confirmation had been secured. Whether a
second lab CEO joins Altman at the Council table remains, at the time
of writing, an open question.

Two cautions attach to this section by rule. First, everything above
describes an announced event: **as of September 22, 2026, the briefing
has not taken place**, and no account of what Altman said — or whether
the session proceeded as announced — can be given here. Second, the
announcement came from an OpenAI spokesperson, not from the Council
itself; the convening authority is France's presidency, and the
chair is Barrot. The distinction between who announced the briefing
and who convenes the meeting is worth preserving, because it marks the
difference between a company's communications and a member state's
diplomacy.

The venue choice — the Security Council rather than the General
Assembly — is doing diplomatic work that deserves to be spelled out.
The General Assembly is where declarations are adopted (§8.7): it is
the UN's normative chamber, broad, deliberative, and unenforceable.
The Security Council is where threats to international peace are
managed: it is the UN's executive chamber, narrow, binding, and
backed — in principle — by enforcement powers. To brief the Council
on "AI and international security" is to ask, implicitly, whether AI
belongs in the second category rather than the first — whether the
technology is a matter for resolutions with teeth rather than
declarations with adjectives. France's presidency, in convening the
open meeting, is therefore making a classificatory claim about AI
itself: that it is a security object, cognizable by the body that
handles wars and weapons. That claim, if accepted by the Council's
other members, would be a more consequential governance event than
anything said in the briefing — because it would relocate AI, within
the UN system, from the development agenda to the security agenda.

The chair matters too. Jean-Noël Barrot, France's foreign minister,
presiding over an open meeting on AI and international security,
places a European middle power — a signatory-adjacent state, in the
spirit if not necessarily the letter of the 22-country declaration —
in the convening seat between the two superpowers. France's AI
diplomacy has, through 2026, consistently positioned Paris as the
broker between Washington's industry-led model and Beijing's
state-led model; the Council presidency gives that brokerage a
procedural form. An open meeting, finally, is the most public format
the Council has: no closed consultations, no restricted circulation —
the briefing is addressed to the Council but performed for the
world. Altman will be speaking to fifteen ambassadors and, through
them, to every government and lab watching the feed.

The unconfirmed Anthropic participation — "hoped for but unconfirmed"
— is a small detail that opens a larger question about representation.
If Altman briefs the Council alone, the session is, in effect, OpenAI
briefing the world's security organ: one company's perspective,
delivered by its CEO, on the international-security implications of
the technology it leads. If a second lab joins, the session becomes
something closer to an industry briefing. Neither format is a neutral
choice, and the fact that the second seat was hoped for but not
secured, as of September 22, suggests the conveners understood the
optics problem without having solved it. A reference dossier records
the hope as well as the absence: the empty chair, if it stays empty,
will be part of the event's meaning.
