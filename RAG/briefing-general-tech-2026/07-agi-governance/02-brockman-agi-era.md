# Brockman: 'the AGI era'

<a id="g08-2"></a>
### 8.2 Brockman: "the AGI era"

The second statement came from inside OpenAI itself, and it was as
carefully hedged as Huang's was categorical. On **September 3, 2026**, the
day of the GPT-6 Astra launch, OpenAI president **Greg Brockman** said in
a company briefing: **"It's not unreasonable to feel that we are now in
the AGI era."** The same day he posted on X: **"We're now moving into the
AGI era."**

The phrasing deserves close reading because it is engineered to do two
things at once. "Not unreasonable to *feel*" is a claim about the
reasonableness of a feeling, not a claim about the world; "moving into"
is a claim about direction, not destination. Between them they allow the
sentence to be heard as a declaration of arrival while retaining, on
inspection, the structure of an observation about trajectory. The X post
("We're now moving into the AGI era") is marginally bolder than the
briefing line, which is consistent with the different registers of the two
channels — a briefing is a record; X is a signal.

What makes Brockman's statements analytically interesting is their
coexistence with the company's official posture. As noted in §8.1, **OpenAI
does not say that Astra IS AGI.** There is an analysis circulating — cited
in secondary coverage — which claims that OpenAI's own launch documents
say the opposite of what Brockman implied, i.e., that the documents
actively disclaim AGI status for Astra. **This claim has not been
confirmed against a primary source**: the launch documents in question
have not been retrieved and quoted in verified reporting, and the
attribution remains unconfirmed. It is flagged here precisely because it
is the kind of claim that hardens into fact if left unmarked — a
secondary analysis, unconfirmed against the primary document, repeated
often enough to sound like OpenAI contradicting its own president.

What can be said with certainty is that there is a visible gradient inside
the September 2026 AGI conversation, running from Huang's categorical
"has arrived" through Brockman's hedged "not unreasonable to feel" to the
company's own refusal to apply the label to its flagship model. That
gradient is the real news of the week: the industry could not agree, in
the same seven days, on whether the era had arrived, was arriving, or
simply felt like it was arriving. The three positions are mutually
irreconcilable on their face, and they were all staked publicly by people
whose companies stand to be judged against them.

The communications strategy visible here is worth unpacking, because it
recurs across the industry and September 2026 is its clearest exhibit.
Brockman's two sentences occupy different positions on what might be
called the deniability gradient of corporate speech. The briefing line —
"it's not unreasonable to feel that we are now in the AGI era" — is
constructed so that every load-bearing word is a hedge: *not
unreasonable* (a double negative, weaker than "reasonable"), *to feel*
(the claim is relocated from the world to the observer's interior),
*now* (unspecified present), *AGI era* (an era, not the thing itself).
The X post — "we're now moving into the AGI era" — drops two of the
hedges and keeps the crucial one: *moving into* preserves direction
without arrival. Read as rhetoric, the pair is a masterclass in saying
the thing without saying it; read as epistemology, it is an admission
that the company cannot, or will not, specify the truth conditions of
its own era claim.

This matters for governance, not just for style. Regulatory and
diplomatic processes run on verifiable claims: a standard can be written
around "the model can do X," but not around "it is not unreasonable to
feel." The September 21 standards post (§8.5) accordingly avoids the AGI
word almost entirely and talks about measurements instead — the
institutional voice of the same company correcting, in effect, the
president's poetry with the Global Affairs team's prose. The two
registers are not contradictory; they are a division of labor. The
president signals to the market and the talent pool; the policy team
signals to governments. But the division of labor has a cost: external
observers — journalists, regulators, rival labs — must decide which
voice is the company's, and the answer changes what the company can be
held to.

On the unconfirmed analysis — the claim that OpenAI's launch documents
for Astra actively disclaim AGI status, contradicting Brockman — a
methodological note is in order, since this dossier's anti-fabrication
rule is doing visible work here. The claim is attractive precisely
because it would resolve the tension: the president oversells, the
documents correct, the company is internally consistent after all. But
attractiveness is not evidence, and the primary documents have not been
produced in verified reporting. There are three live possibilities: the
documents disclaim AGI (and Brockman was freelancing), the documents
are silent on AGI (and the analysis over-read them), or the documents
do not exist in the form described (and the analysis is confabulated).
This dossier cannot adjudicate between them, and it should not try. The
unconfirmed claim is recorded here as unconfirmed — which is itself a
finding about the September information environment: even the basic
question of what OpenAI's own paperwork says about its own flagship
model could not be settled from public sources within three weeks of
launch.
