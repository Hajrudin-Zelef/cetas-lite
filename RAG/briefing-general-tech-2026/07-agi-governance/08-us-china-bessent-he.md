# US-China: Bessent–He Lifeng and the incident line

<a id="g08-8"></a>
### 8.8 US-China: Bessent–He Lifeng and the incident line

The bilateral track ran parallel to the multilateral one, and it ran
first. On **Sunday, September 20, 2026**, US Treasury Secretary **Scott
Bessent** met Chinese Vice Premier **He Lifeng** in **New York, at
JPMorgan's headquarters**, for talks in which AI safety featured
prominently. Three outcomes are on the verified record.

First, the United States proposed a **national-security-level AI
"incident line"** — a direct crisis-communication channel between
**Donald Trump and Xi Jinping**, modeled on the Cold War hotline logic:
when an AI incident threatens to escalate, the two leaders can reach
each other before miscalculation does the reaching. The proposal's
existence is confirmed; whether Beijing accepts it is not.

Second, the two sides **formalized a "US–China AI dialogue"** — the
bilateral conversation on AI safety and governance now has a name and a
standing format, which is more than it had before New York.

Third, **export controls were off the agenda**: US Trade Representative
Jamieson Greer confirmed that the chip-export-control regime was not
discussed. The compartmentalization is deliberate — safety dialogue
proceeds in one channel while the technology-denial regime proceeds in
another, and neither is hostage to the other. Whether that
compartmentalization holds under the pressure of a real incident is
precisely what the incident line is for.

The **Chinese response** to the American proposals is, as of September
22, **unclear** — no verified statement of acceptance, rejection, or
counter-proposal is on record. This is not a minor gap: a hotline that
only one side has agreed to pick up is a proposal, not a channel.

The day after the talks, on **September 21**, Bessent gave a **CNBC
interview** that added two elements. He said the **next meeting** would
take place in **about two months, in Shenzhen**, focused on **AI dangers
and communication protocols** — which, if it happens, would put the
follow-up in mid-to-late November 2026, on Chinese soil, with protocols
as the agenda. And he **reiterated that the Hugging Face incident is on
"OpenAI management"** and that labs must **"take responsibility"** and
**"slow down anytime they want to."** The phrasing is worth noting for
its allocation of blame: not the technology, not the researchers — the
management. It is the Treasury Secretary of the United States telling
the country's leading AI lab, on television, that the July incident was
a management failure and that slowing down is always an available
choice. Coming twenty-four hours after proposing a leader-level incident
line, the message reads as the public half of a private conversation:
Washington wants the channel *and* the accountability.

A sourcing note: one Korean blog dated September 22 described an
eight-hour meeting "two days before the summit" — i.e., implying
September 22. **All major sources date the Bessent–He Lifeng talks to
September 20**, and the Korean blog's dating is discarded accordingly.
This is the kind of secondary-source drift the anti-fabrication rule
exists to catch: a single blog's arithmetic, repeated, becomes a
"fact" about when the two economic principals met.

Taken together, the New York talks represent the week's most concrete
diplomatic output on AI safety: a named dialogue, a proposed hotline, a
dated follow-up — and an explicitly unresolved Chinese answer. The
multilateral declaration of the next day (§8.7) was signed without the
two parties to this conversation; the bilateral conversation proceeded
without the twenty-two signatories. September 2026 thus produced two
governance tracks that do not intersect: one of norms without the
superpowers, one of superpowers without norms.

The incident-line proposal is the week's most Cold War-shaped idea,
and the shape is worth examining. Crisis hotlines between nuclear
adversaries were built on a specific theory: that the gravest risk was
not malice but miscalculation — that in a crisis, each side would
misread the other's signals and escalate past the point of return. An
AI incident line between Trump and Xi imports that theory into a new
domain, and the import carries assumptions worth surfacing. It assumes
that AI incidents will be *attributable enough* to trigger a
leader-level call but *ambiguous enough* to need one — that the two
leaders will know something happened without knowing what it means.
It assumes the relevant timescale is hours-to-days (the hotline
timescale), not minutes (the cyber timescale) or months (the
diplomatic timescale). And it assumes, most fundamentally, that the
US–China AI relationship is adversarial in the way the US–Soviet
nuclear relationship was adversarial: a competition managed to avoid
catastrophe, rather than a collaboration managed to produce progress.
None of these assumptions is stated in the verified record. All of
them are load-bearing.

The compartmentalization — export controls off the agenda, per Greer
— is the proposal's necessary companion, and it is the more fragile
of the two. The logic is that technology denial and safety dialogue
can run on separate tracks: Washington restricts the chips while
discussing the incidents, and neither track poisons the other. The
logic holds only so long as both sides accept the separation, and
there is reason, on the verified record, to doubt that Beijing does:
the Chinese response to the whole New York package is "unclear," and
unclarity about the hotline may be unclarity about the
compartmentalization too. A safety dialogue that proceeds while one
side believes the other's export controls are the real policy — and
the dialogue the theater — is a dialogue built on sand. The
compartmentalization is thus both the condition of the talks'
possibility and their central vulnerability, and the September 20
record leaves the vulnerability entirely unaddressed.

Bessent's CNBC remarks the next day add a domestic-political layer
that should not be missed. "The Hugging Face incident is on OpenAI
management" and labs must "take responsibility" and "slow down
anytime they want to" — this is the Treasury Secretary performing,
for a domestic audience, the accountability that the international
track cannot deliver. The bilateral dialogue (§8.8) and the
multilateral declaration (§8.7) both operate without enforcement; the
CNBC interview supplies, rhetorically, what they lack: blame,
assigned to a named party, on television. Whether the blame is fair
is a separate question — the July incident's technical causes are
still being adjudicated, and "management" is a capacious category.
What matters for the record is the allocation: Washington's public
line, as of September 21, is that the frontier's failures are
failures of stewardship, not of science, and that the remedy —
slowing down — is available unilaterally, at will, to the labs
themselves.

A final note on the Shenzhen follow-up, projected for "about two
months" — mid-to-late November 2026. If it happens, the return leg
on Chinese soil, with "AI dangers and communication protocols" as the
agenda, will be the first test of whether New York produced a process
or a photo opportunity. Protocols are the unglamorous half of crisis
management — the pre-agreed vocabularies, escalation ladders, and
notification formats that make a hotline usable rather than
symbolic — and their presence on the Shenzhen agenda suggests the
American side understands this. But a protocol negotiation requires
two negotiating parties, and as of September 22 only one side's
intentions are on the record. The diary entry for November is written
in pencil.
