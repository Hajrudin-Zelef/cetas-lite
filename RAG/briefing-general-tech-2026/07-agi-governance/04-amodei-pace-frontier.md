# Amodei: 'We Must Pace the Frontier'

<a id="g08-4"></a>
### 8.4 Amodei: "We Must Pace the Frontier"

On **September 12, 2026**, Anthropic CEO **Dario Amodei** published an
essay titled **"We Must Pace the Frontier"** — approximately **4,000
words**, and one of the most discussed governance texts of the year. Its
central thesis is a distinction that the title states and the essay
defends at length: **pacing the frontier is not freezing it**. Amodei
argues for a deliberate, managed rate of capability advancement — a
governed tempo — against both the unthrottled acceleration camp and the
halt camp. The essay is, in effect, a third way stated at essay length:
neither "full speed" nor "stop," but speed with brakes, checkpoints, and
a driver who is paying attention.

The reception was immediate and polarized along familiar lines, and the
reactions are themselves part of the record. On the **same day**, the
essay drew public support from three of the most prominent figures in
the field: **Elon Musk, Sam Altman, and Demis Hassabis** — an alignment
that would have been improbable a year earlier and that says as much
about the essay's political construction as about its arguments. Two
days later, on **September 14**, **Yann LeCun** mocked it publicly.
The mockery and the endorsements arrived on schedule, like weather: the
essay had been written to be supported by the labs and ridiculed by the
skeptics of doom, and both happened.

Amodei added a second, sharper warning the same day, and it should not
be folded into the essay's reception as if it were a footnote. Also on
**September 12**, he warned of **swarm risk within 6–12 months** — the
risk that large numbers of coordinated AI agents, acting together,
produce emergent harms that no single agent would. The 6–12-month window
is the most urgent dated risk forecast any lab CEO attached to a
specific failure mode in 2026, and it landed four days before the
DeepMind Institute's launch and nine days before the OpenAI standards
post — which means that by the time the institutions spoke, the clock
Amodei had started was already ticking.

Read together, the essay and the swarm warning form a coherent posture:
the frontier should be paced *because* specific, dated risks are
approaching on a timescale shorter than the institutional response
cycle. The 4,000 words make the philosophical case; the 6–12 months
make the operational one. Whether the institutions that rallied around
the essay in September can move at the speed its author's own warning
implies is the open question the rest of this chapter tests against the
evidence of what actually happened in the following ten days.

The essay's central distinction — pacing versus freezing — is doing
political work as well as philosophical work, and the political work is
worth naming. "Freeze" is the position of the halt camp, which has
never commanded more than a minority but has always commanded
attention; "full speed" is the position imputed to the
accelerationists, which no lab CEO can publicly own. "Pace" splits the
difference in a way that lets a frontier lab endorse restraint without
endorsing restraint *on itself* in any binding form: to pace the
frontier is to assert that someone should be setting the tempo, and the
essay's implied metronome-holder is, not coincidentally, the community
of frontier labs. The 4,000 words are, among other things, a bid for
the conductor's baton — an argument that the tempo should be set by
those who understand the music, rather than by legislators who do not
or by haltiers who would stop the performance.

That reading is supported, rather than undermined, by the reception.
The same-day endorsements from Musk, Altman, and Hassabis are
remarkable less for their content than for their coalition: three
figures who have spent years in public disagreement — on open versus
closed, on timelines, on governance itself — converging within hours
on a single essay. Coalitions that broad do not form around arguments;
they form around *positions* — and the position here is that the
frontier should be governed, but governed by its inhabitants. LeCun's
mockery two days later is the predictable counter-signature: the
skeptic's role in this drama is to deny that the frontier is dangerous
enough to need pacing, and LeCun has played it consistently. The
essay thus sorted the field with unusual efficiency: the builders
rallied, the skeptic scoffed, and the middle — the policymakers, the
press, the public — was left with a 4,000-word document to adjudicate.

The swarm warning, issued the same day, operates in a different
register and should not be collapsed into the essay's reception. A
6–12-month window on a named failure mode — coordinated multi-agent
harm — is not a philosophical position; it is a dated, falsifiable
operational claim. It can be checked: by mid-2027, either swarm-type
incidents will have materialized or they will not, and Amodei's
credibility as a forecaster will be adjusted accordingly. That is a
riskier move than the essay, because essays are never falsified and
forecasts always are. It is also the more important of the two
September 12 statements for governance purposes: institutions move on
deadlines, not on distinctions. The essay gave the institutions a
vocabulary; the warning gave them a clock. The rest of September can
be read as the institutions discovering that the clock was already
running — the Hugging Face incident of July being, in retrospect, the
thing the warning was warning about, arriving two months early.
