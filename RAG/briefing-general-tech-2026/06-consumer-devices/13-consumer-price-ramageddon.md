# Consumer price pressure and 'RAMageddon'

<a id="g07-13"></a>
### 7.13 Consumer price pressure and "RAMageddon"

If the foldable battle of September 2026 was the year's most visible hardware story, the
memory crisis was its most consequential — because it touched every device with a chip
in it, and because it is still unfolding. The industry's nickname for it, "RAMageddon,"
is doing the rounds in trade reporting, and it captures the mood: a memory-price
spike severe enough to reshape product pricing across categories, named with the
gallows humor of an industry that has seen cycles before but not quite like this one.

The numbers that define the crisis come from Gartner, published in February 2026 — early
enough in the year that the full-year consequences were still projections, late enough
that the direction was unmistakable. Gartner's figures: DRAM and SSD prices up 130%,
translating into PC prices up 17% and smartphone prices up 13%, with unit shipments
falling 10.4% for PCs and 8.4% for smartphones. Read that chain carefully, because it is
the whole story in one line: a component shock (+130% on memory and storage) becomes a
retail price shock (+17% / +13%), which becomes a demand shock (-10.4% / -8.4% in
units). Every link in the chain is verified; every link held through the year, with
IDC's Q2 and full-year PC figures (section 7.12) landing squarely in the territory
Gartner mapped in February.

The mechanism is worth spelling out for readers who do not follow the memory market.
DRAM and NAND flash are commodity components with violent pricing cycles: when demand
from data centers (AI buildout), smartphones, and PCs coincides with constrained fab
output, contract prices spike, and there is no substitute — every PC needs RAM, every
phone needs storage. Vendors have three options: absorb the cost (destroying margin),
raise prices (destroying volume), or de-spec machines (destroying the product). In 2026,
the industry mostly chose the second, partially the third. The result is visible in the
second data point of this section: on June 25, 2026, Apple raised prices on Mac, iPad,
and Vision Pro lines. When Apple — the industry's most margin-rich vendor, the one
best positioned to absorb a component shock — raises prices across three product
families in a single day, the crisis is not a forecast. It is the price list.

The consequences ripple outward from the price lists. For PCs, the memory crisis is the
primary drag on the unit figures in section 7.12: a buyer facing a 17% price increase
defers the purchase, and deferred purchases compound into IDC's -11.3% full-year
projection. For smartphones, the 13% increase lands hardest in the midrange, where
margins are thinnest and price elasticity highest. For the foldables in sections 7.1–7.8, the crisis is a headwind nobody mentions
on stage: the iPhone Duo's $1,999 entry price was set in a world where every gigabyte
of DRAM costs dramatically more than it did eighteen months ago, and TrendForce's
5-million-unit forecast implicitly assumes consumers will pay it anyway.

There is a final irony worth recording. The AI boom is both the cause of the memory
crisis and its supposed justification: data-center demand for high-bandwidth memory is
one of the forces pulling DRAM supply away from consumer devices and pushing prices
up. The consumer holding a more expensive phone in 2026 is, in a real sense, paying
for someone else's training run. "RAMageddon" is a joke about RAM prices; the
underlying story is about who gets the memory — the data center or the device — and
in 2026, the data center won. The device, and its buyer, paid the difference.

The crisis chain, as Gartner mapped it in February 2026:

| Link in the chain | Figure | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Component shock | DRAM + SSD prices +130% | The input cost spike |
| Retail price shock | PCs +17%, smartphones +13% | What vendors passed through |
| Demand shock | PC shipments -10.4%, smartphone shipments -8.4% | What buyers did in response |

Every link held through the year. IDC's Q2 and full-year PC figures (-4.9%
in Q2, -11.3% expected for the full year) landed in the territory Gartner
sketched in February; the direction never reversed, only the magnitude came
into focus. February's projection becoming September's reality is the
crisis's signature: it was visible early, it was quantified early, and it
still could not be avoided — because there is no substitute for memory and
no short-term substitute for the fabs that make it.

The year's milestones, in order:

| Date | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| February 2026 | Gartner publishes the +130% / +17% / +13% figures | The crisis gets its numbers |
| 25/06/2026 | Apple raises Mac, iPad, and Vision Pro prices | The industry's most margin-rich vendor passes costs through — the crisis reaches the price list |
| 9/09/2026 | iPhone Duo announced at $1,999–$3,199 | A flagship priced inside the crisis |
| 21/09/2026 | Five Googlebooks announced at $899–$1,299 | A volume play priced inside the same crisis |

The June 25 Apple price increase is the section's hinge event. Apple
absorbs component shocks when it can — its margins allow it — so a
same-day increase across Mac, iPad, and Vision Pro reads as a signal that
absorption was no longer tenable. When the vendor best positioned to eat
the cost declines to eat it, the rest of the industry's pricing decisions
are already made. Every PC and phone price tag in the second half of 2026
carries the memory crisis in it, including the two flagship launches of
September: the Duo's $1,999 entry and the Googlebook's $899–$1,299 band
were both set in a world where every gigabyte costs dramatically more than
it did eighteen months earlier.

The per-category impact follows the elasticity. PCs, with a 17% price
increase and double-digit unit declines, show the textbook response: buyers
defer, and deferred purchases compound. Smartphones, at +13%, bend hardest
in the midrange — the volume tier where margins are thinnest and buyers
most price-sensitive. Foldables, the chapter's headliners, face the crisis
as an unmentioned headwind: TrendForce's 5-million-unit Duo forecast
implicitly assumes consumers will pay $1,999 and up despite it, and
Samsung's 30% first-month Fold 8 growth happened in spite of it. Nobody put
"RAMageddon" on a keynote slide in September. Everyone priced around it.

The final irony is structural: the AI boom is both the crisis's cause and
its supposed justification, with data-center demand for high-bandwidth
memory pulling DRAM supply toward servers — one of the forces behind the
+130%. "RAMageddon" jokes about RAM prices; the underlying story is
allocative — who gets the memory, the data center or the device — and in
2026, the data center won. The buyer paid the difference.
