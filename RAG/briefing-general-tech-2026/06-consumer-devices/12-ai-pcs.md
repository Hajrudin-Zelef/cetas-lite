# AI PCs: 55% of shipments, but units fall

<a id="g07-12"></a>
### 7.12 AI PCs: 55% of shipments, but units fall

The AI PC is 2026's great paradox: a category that is winning the share battle and
losing the unit war at the same time. By the broadest industry measure, the transition
is nearly complete — Omdia's 2026 figures put AI-capable PCs at 55% of shipments, and
Intel has spoken of 100 million AI PCs shipped over two years. The NPU, the Copilot key,
the on-device model: what was a premium differentiator in 2024 is standard equipment in
2026. Gartner, notably, has pushed its expectation of 50% AI PC penetration out to 2028
— a more conservative timeline that reflects the difference between "shipped with an
NPU" and "bought for AI," but still describes a market more than halfway converted.

And yet global PC unit shipments are falling. IDC's figures: down 4.9% year over year in
Q2 2026, with a full-year 2026 decline of 11.3% expected. The industry is shipping a
higher proportion of AI PCs out of a shrinking total — the mix improves while the market
contracts. This is not the pattern of a technology transition pulling demand forward;
it is the pattern of a transition happening inside a downturn, where the new
capability is not sufficient to make people buy sooner.

The cause of the downturn is not mysterious, and it is the subject of the next section:
the memory crisis. But the AI PC numbers deserve their own analysis, because they reveal
something about what "AI PC" means in 2026. The 55% shipment share counts hardware
capability — an NPU present in the silicon. It does not measure whether buyers chose
the machine for its AI features, whether they use them, or whether the AI features
influenced the purchase at all. In a year when DRAM and SSD prices rose 130% (section
7.13), a large fraction of "AI PC" shipments are simply the PCs that were available to
buy: vendors put NPUs in everything because the silicon generation turned over, not
because consumers demanded neural processing. The share figure is real; its meaning is
thinner than it looks.

Intel's "100 million AI PCs in two years" belongs to the same category of statement:
a cumulative shipment claim that describes silicon deployment, not user adoption. It is
useful as a measure of how completely the hardware transition has propagated through
the supply chain, and misleading as a measure of the AI PC as a consumer proposition.
Nobody has published credible figures on what fraction of those 100 million machines'
owners use on-device AI features weekly — because, as of September 2026, no vendor has volunteered that number as of September 2026.

The forward read is Gartner's: 50% penetration pushed to 2028. That revision encodes a
judgment — that the AI PC's adoption curve is slower than the shipment curve, and that
the memory crisis (which makes every PC more expensive regardless of its NPU) is
dragging the whole market down faster than AI features can lift it. The Googlebook
launch of September 2026 (sections 7.9–7.11) can be read as a bet against this
pessimism: that system-level AI, done properly in the OS rather than as a hardware
checklist, is the feature that finally makes the AI PC a buying reason rather than a
specification footnote. It is a bet placed at the bottom of a unit downturn. The next
four quarters will show whether it was well timed.

The paradox, stated as a table:

| Metric | Figure | Source | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI PC share of shipments, 2026 | 55% | Omdia | Up — the transition is more than half done |
| Intel cumulative AI PC shipments | 100M over two years | Intel | Up — silicon deployment at scale |
| Global PC units, Q2 2026 | -4.9% YoY | IDC | Down |
| Global PC units, full-year 2026 (expected) | -11.3% | IDC | Down sharply |
| 50% AI penetration timeline | Pushed to 2028 | Gartner | Delayed |

The table's tension is the section's whole argument: the mix improves while
the market contracts. The industry is shipping a higher proportion of
AI-capable machines out of a shrinking total — a transition happening
inside a downturn, where the new capability is not pulling demand forward
fast enough to offset the forces pushing it back.

What the 55% actually measures deserves emphasis, because the number is
widely quoted and narrowly meaningful. It counts hardware capability — an
NPU present in the silicon — not buyer intent. It does not record whether
the purchaser chose the machine for its AI features, whether they use them,
or whether the features influenced the purchase at any point. In a year
when DRAM and SSD prices rose 130% (section 7.13), a large share of "AI
PC" shipments are simply the PCs that were available to buy: vendors put
NPUs in every generation because the silicon turned over, not because
consumers demanded neural processing. The share figure is real; its meaning
as a measure of AI adoption is thinner than its citation count suggests.
Intel's 100-million figure belongs to the same category — cumulative
silicon deployment, not weekly active use of on-device AI, a number no
vendor has published.

Gartner's revision — 50% penetration pushed to 2028 — encodes the judgment
that the adoption curve trails the shipment curve, with the memory crisis
as the named cause. The Googlebook launch reads, in this context, as a bet
against the pessimism: system-level AI as the implementation that finally
makes the AI PC a buying reason instead of a footnote — placed at the
bottom of a unit downturn. The next four quarters grade the bet.
