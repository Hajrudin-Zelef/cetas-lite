# FP8: the safe production standard

<a id="g05-8"></a>
### 5.8 FP8: the safe production standard

Every quantization story in 2026 has two tiers: the safe one you deploy
without a second thought, and the aggressive one you benchmark carefully
before committing. FP8 is the safe tier — and in 2026, "safe" acquired a
precise operational meaning: the precision you fall back to when an aggressive
format fails a quality gate, the format whose tooling is assumed present in
every serving stack, the tier where "production standard" means nobody gets
fired for shipping it.

Eight-bit floating point became the datacenter's mainstream low-precision
standard from 2025 onward. The industry's working figure is roughly 99%
quality retained versus FP16 — close enough that for most production workloads
the switch from FP16 to FP8 is a configuration change, not a research project:
recompile or reconfigure the serving stack, run the evals, watch them pass. A
widely used practitioner guide, updated March 6, 2026, documents the state of
play as of early 2026: FP8 with dedicated hardware acceleration on Nvidia's
Hopper and Blackwell architectures, mature tooling across the serving
runtimes, and quality retention that makes FP8 the default first step down
from FP16.

The 2026 significance of FP8 is not that it is new — it isn't — but that it
stopped being a decision. In 2024, serving stacks defaulted to FP16 and
debated FP8 as an optimization worth evaluating; in 2026, stacks default to
FP8-adjacent thinking and debate *4-bit*. FP8 became the floor of the
conversation: the assumed starting point from which the aggressive tier
(Section 5.9) is measured. That normalization is itself an economic event — it
means the industry's *baseline* per-token cost already embeds an 8-bit
assumption, and further gains must come from going lower.

Two honest boundaries on the FP8 story, both verified by what this dossier
could and could not confirm. First, hardware support is Nvidia-led: FP8
acceleration is confirmed on Hopper and Blackwell. FP8 native support on AMD's
MI300+ was *not* verified by direct reading for this dossier and is therefore
not asserted — the AMD quantization story in 2026 runs more through INT4/AWQ-
class tooling in vLLM and SGLang (Sections 5.10, 5.14) than through FP8-first
claims, and asserting unverified hardware support would violate the dossier's
method. Second, FP8 is a floor, not a ceiling: the emerging aggressive tier is
4-bit (FP4-family) on Blackwell, where the additional halving of memory
traffic buys the next step down in cost per token. The measured datapoint
bridging the two tiers is DeepSeek-R1-0528: post-training quantization from
FP8 to NVFP4 cost at most 1% of quality — evidence that the FP8→FP4 step, done
with the right method, is nearly as safe as the FP16→FP8 step was, which is
exactly why operators started taking it in 2026.

| FP8 at a glance | Detail |
|---|---|
| Status | Datacenter 8-bit mainstream from 2025 onward |
| Quality | ~99% retained vs FP16 (industry working figure) |
| Hardware acceleration | Confirmed on Nvidia Hopper and Blackwell (practitioner guide updated 6/03/2026) |
| AMD MI300+ native FP8 | Not verified by direct reading — not asserted |
| 2026 role | The "safe" tier and the industry's baseline assumption; aggressive frontier moved to 4-bit (FP4) on Blackwell |
| Bridge datapoint | DeepSeek-R1-0528: ≤1% quality loss FP8→NVFP4 via PTQ |

There is a psychology to the safe tier that matters operationally. "Safe"
doesn't mean "free" — FP8 still requires evals, still changes numerics, still
needs the hardware acceleration to be present. It means the failure mode is
bounded and familiar: teams have run the FP16→FP8 conversion enough times that
the eval-gate procedure is routine, the rollback is a config change, and the
post-mortem template already exists. That operational maturity is what makes a
tier "safe" rather than any property of the bits. It also explains the tier
dynamics of 2026: INT4 spent the year *becoming* safe — accumulating the eval-
gate procedures, the kernel support, the incident post-mortems — while
FP4-family formats were still in the "benchmark carefully" phase. Tiers are
not technical categories; they are organizational ones. A format graduates
when the industry's runbooks cover it.
