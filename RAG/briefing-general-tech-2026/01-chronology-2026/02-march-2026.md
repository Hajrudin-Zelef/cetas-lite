# March 2026

<a id="g02-4"></a>
### 2.4 March 2026

If February was the month the constraints were named, March was the month the
industry's biggest players showed their answers. Nvidia used GTC to reveal its
full next-generation platform; Intel announced its datacenter CPU refresh at
MWC; and SK Group's chairman told Reuters what the memory executives were saying
privately — the shortage was structural and long. March is the hinge of this
chapter: everything before it was pressure, everything after it was response. It
is also the month when the year's two dominant vendors (Nvidia in accelerators,
Intel in host CPUs) both put their next-generation cards on the table, three
months before either would ship.

GTC, on March 16, was the Vera Rubin reveal — the full unveiling of the platform
succeeding Blackwell. The details of the Rubin architecture belong to the
silicon chapters of this dossier's companion sections, but GTC's strategic
content is worth stating here: Nvidia was presenting not a chip but a stack —
compute, networking, and the Dynamo software layer as one deployable unit.
Dynamo reaching general availability around March 2026 is the software half of
that story: the inference-serving software that lets operators actually extract
the platform's performance, productized and supported rather than left as a
reference implementation. The industry had learned from the Blackwell ramp that
software readiness paces hardware adoption; Nvidia was determined not to repeat
the lesson with Rubin.

The AI-RAN announcement with T-Mobile and Nokia, folded into the same GTC
window, extended that stack thinking into telecom: AI workloads running on radio
access networks, with two major operators and a major equipment vendor attached.
The message to the market was that Nvidia intended to own the full
inference-serving path from rack to radio, and that telecom infrastructure was
being reframed as AI infrastructure. The proof point for the datacenter half
would come in June, when the first production NVL72 rack came online at
CoreWeave — but March was when the roadmap became public and the industry's
planning assumptions reset around it. Every datacenter build decision made from
March onward had a Rubin-shaped alternative on the table.

At MWC in the same month, Intel announced Xeon 6+ "Clearwater Forest" — the
E-core-heavy datacenter CPU line that would launch formally at Computex in June.
The announcement timing matters: Intel was positioning Clearwater Forest, with
up to 288 E-cores (vendor claim, from Intel's materials), as the efficiency
complement to accelerator-heavy AI racks — the host CPU for a world where the
GPU does the math and the CPU does everything else at minimum power. The E-core
bet is a philosophical one: in an AI rack, the host processor's job is
orchestration, I/O and efficiency, not raw single-thread performance, so Intel
was maximizing core count and performance-per-watt rather than per-core speed.
March's announcement and June's launch form a single arc (see 2.7), and the MWC
venue was the tell: Intel was pitching efficiency computing to the telecom and
edge crowd first, the cloud crowd second — the same audience, not
coincidentally, that Nvidia was courting with AI-RAN.

The month's third signal was the least technical and the most consequential. SK
Group chairman Chey Tae-won told Reuters in March that the wafer shortage would
extend into 2030. This was not a quarterly-guidance hedge; it was the head of
one of the world's largest memory and semiconductor conglomerates describing a
structural deficit with a four-year horizon. Wafers are the substrate of
everything — no wafers, no DRAM, no NAND, no logic — so a wafer shortage is a
shortage of the entire industry's raw material, not just one component. Read
against Gartner's February pricing data (+130% DRAM+SSD), Chey's warning
converted a price spike into a planning assumption: memory would not "normalize"
on any horizon relevant to current product cycles. Every subsequent memory
decision in this chapter — Micron's June guidance ("considerable time," gradual
improvement in 2028), Apple's June price increases, the HBM-free Taalas bet from
February — traces back to the world Chey described in March. It is the single
most load-bearing sentence in the chapter's first half, and it came from an
interview, not a product launch.
