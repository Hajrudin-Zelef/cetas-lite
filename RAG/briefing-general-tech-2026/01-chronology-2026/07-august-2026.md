# August 2026

<a id="g02-9"></a>
### 2.9 August 2026

August was an infrastructure month in the most literal sense: power delivery,
optical interconnect, and the silicon-design community's annual technical
reckoning. Nothing in August was as headline-grabbing as Rubin's June production
start or AMD's July event, but the month's announcements describe the physical
layer on which the next two years of datacenter builds will run — and that makes
it load-bearing for any forward-looking reading of this dossier. August is the
month to re-read when the 2027–28 deployment wave arrives, because it is when
the industry agreed on what that wave would be built from.

The headline item came on August 12: Nvidia announced 800V DC power delivery for
AI datacenters, with Google and Microsoft as named partners and 80+ partners
participating through the Open Compute Project. The technical content: rack-
compatible MGX designs (H2 2026) and 2-megawatt rack power centers (2027), with
mass deployment expected in 2027–28. The physics behind it is straightforward —
AI racks are outgrowing the power envelopes that 48V/54V distribution was
designed for, and moving megawatts at low voltage means either impossibly thick
copper or unacceptable conversion losses; 800V DC is the industry's answer to
getting megawatts to silicon efficiently. But the partner list is the real news:
Google and Microsoft co-announcing a power standard with Nvidia, inside OCP,
means the hyperscalers have converged on a shared physical layer rather than
fragmenting into proprietary power designs. Power delivery is the least
glamorous and most binding constraint in datacenter design — you can swap
accelerators, but you cannot easily rewire a building — so a shared 800V DC
standard is a commitment device for the whole industry's 2027–28 builds. (All
deployment dates here are announced plans, not accomplishments — the mass-
deployment window of 2027–28 falls after this dossier's snapshot, and the
"Planned" discipline of 2.10 applies.)

The same week as Hot Chips 2026 (the week of August 24), Intel detailed
"Crescent Island" — its next datacenter silicon disclosure to the industry's
most technical audience. Intel choosing it for Crescent Island signaled
that the part was past the marketing stage and into the engineering-detail stage
— the point in a product's life when NDAs start lifting and the ecosystem begins
real planning. The disclosure sits in a direct line with the June Clearwater
Forest launch: Intel's datacenter roadmap was now a visible multi-generation
sequence, not a single product, and the company was using the industry's
technical venues to keep the sequence credible quarter by quarter.

Optical interconnect had its own August, and it came with financials. FS.com
announced the D7070 800G muxponder via BusinessWire on August 24 — an
800-gigabit multiplexing transponder for datacenter interconnect, the kind of
product that turns raw fiber into usable AI-cluster bandwidth. And around August
25, the company reported H1 2026 results: revenue up 25.9% to RMB 1.76 billion,
net profit up 65.1% to RMB 451.1 million. The pairing is instructive — a product
announcement and a financial result in the same week, both pointing the same
direction: optical transport for AI datacenters was growing fast and profitably,
with profit growing more than twice as fast as revenue (a margin story, not just
a volume story). FS.com would return in September with a Wi-Fi 7 announcement
(see 2.10), making the company one of the dossier's recurring infrastructure
names — the kind of second-tier supplier whose results are a better barometer of
the buildout than any single hyperscaler's capex guidance.

Finally, August 24 brought the second PCC M5 hardware leak — the follow-up to
February's 9to5Mac report on Apple's Private Cloud Compute transition. Two
leaks, six months apart, both pointing at M5-based PCC hardware (J226C): the
February report described the plan, the August leak suggested execution. Apple's
inference-cloud migration was proceeding on the cadence the February report
implied, and the M3 Ultra/M4 skip was holding — no interim generation had
appeared in the PCC hardware trail. In a year of gigawatt announcements and
trillion-dollar valuations, the quietest through-line in the chapter may be this
one: Apple, methodically, building its own inference cloud out of its own
silicon, one hardware reference at a time.
