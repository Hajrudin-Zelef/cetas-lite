# May 2026

<a id="g02-6"></a>
### 2.6 May 2026

May belonged to Google, with an interconnect footnote. On May 12, at "The
Android Show: I/O Edition," Google announced "Googlebook" — AI-first laptops
built around Gemini, and the clearest signal yet of the long-rumored
Android/ChromeOS convergence (the effort carried the internal codename
"Aluminium"). The strategic content is worth unpacking, because Google was not
announcing a laptop; it was announcing a category. An AI-first laptop is
designed from the start around an assistant rather than being a conventional PC
with an assistant bolted on: Gemini as the continuity layer across tasks, local
AI acceleration in the silicon, and the operating system organized around
agentic workflows rather than app grids. Whether the first generation delivers
on that vision is a product question for reviewers; the announcement's
significance is the strategic commitment it represents — Google declaring that
the laptop's next paradigm shift is AI-native, and that it intends to define it.

The Android/ChromeOS convergence is the operating-system expression of the same
idea, and the "Aluminium" codename is the tell that this was a long-running
internal effort, not a reactive announcement. For years, Google maintained two
operating systems with an awkward division of labor — Android for touch,
ChromeOS for keyboards — while Apple unified its platforms around shared silicon
and Microsoft pushed Windows everywhere. Convergence means one platform spanning
phone, tablet and laptop, with Gemini as the continuity layer and a single
developer target. The May announcement was the vision; the OEM reveals would
come in September (see 2.10), with five manufacturers and ASUS Canada preorders
on September 21. May to September is the full arc of a platform launch: announce
the strategy at I/O season, ship the partner hardware in the autumn, open US
sales on October 4 (planned, after the snapshot).

The month's second item was a forward-looking analyst note: Zacks, on May 14,
placed UALink volume deployment in 2027. Taken alone it is a single data point;
taken with the April 7 publication of the UALink 2.0 specification, it completes
the standard's near-term timeline — spec in 2026, volume in 2027. The note's
value is as an independent read on the consortium's execution: a sell-side
analyst, with no seat at the consortium table, looking at the 1.0-to-2.0 cadence
and concluding that volume production a year later was credible. For the open-
interconnect camp, external validation of the timeline mattered almost as much
as the spec itself, because the decision hyperscalers face is whether to bet
2027 rack designs on it. May's contribution to the chapter is therefore a small
one but a fitting one: the month's big story was a consumer platform
announcement, and its footnote was the infrastructure timeline quietly becoming
real.
