# April 2026

<a id="g02-5"></a>
### 2.5 April 2026

April was a quiet month on the surface and a busy one underneath — the kind of
month where standards get published, unannounced products surface on vendor spec
pages, and pilot workloads run without press releases. Three items, each modest
on its own, each load-bearing for what followed. April rewards the reader who
understands that infrastructure news is often boring on purpose: the most
consequential events are the ones that make future events possible, and they
rarely trend.

On April 7, the UALink Consortium published the UALink 2.0 specification,
exactly one year after UALink 1.0 was ratified on April 8, 2025. The cadence
itself is the story: a one-year spec cycle for an open accelerator interconnect,
in an industry where the alternative is a proprietary fabric controlled by a
single vendor. UALink is the industry's collective answer to NVLink — an open
standard letting accelerators from different vendors communicate at scale inside
and across racks — and the 2.0 publication kept the standard moving at the pace
the hardware required. The forward look came a month later (Zacks,
May 14), placing UALink volume deployment in 2027 — spec in 2026, volume in
2027, a twelve-month pipeline from paper to product. For datacenter planners
choosing between proprietary and open interconnect fabrics for 2027–2028 rack
builds, that cadence was actionable information, and it is why the note belongs
in the chronology rather than in a footnote. April was when the open-
interconnect roadmap stopped being aspirational and became something you could
design against.

Also in April, Intel's Core Ultra X9 378H appeared on Intel ARK — the company's
official product specification database — without a formal launch announcement.
The X9 378H designation places it at the top of the Core Ultra stack — the X9
tier, the 378 numbering,
the H suffix for high-performance mobile — signaling that Intel's next mobile
refresh was further along than its marketing calendar suggested. It is a small
item, but it belongs in the record for two reasons: it foreshadowed the June
Computex cadence, and it was a reminder, in a year dominated by datacenter news,
that the client-silicon roadmap was still moving. The AI PC story of 2025 had
not ended; it had just been drowned out by the datacenter story, and ARK was
quietly keeping score.

The third April item was the least public and arguably the most significant for
the accelerator-competition narrative: OpenAI had been running GPT-class pilot
workloads on AMD Helios racks since approximately April 2026. "GPT-class pilot
workloads" is doing careful work in that sentence — these were real frontier-
model workloads, not synthetic benchmarks, running on AMD's rack-scale platform
months before Helios hardware was formally detailed at Advancing AI in July. The
"~April" dating reflects the sourcing: pilot programs are rarely announced with
a press release, and the timeline is reconstructed rather than declared. But the
pilot's existence reframes the July announcements (see 2.8) completely: when
OpenAI's Sachin Katti appeared on stage on July 23 to say the company would
deploy Helios "at massive scale" at the end of 2026, he was describing the
scaling of a deployment that had already been running for three months, not a
cold start. April is when AMD's datacenter comeback stopped being a roadmap and
started being a workload — and it is why July's on-stage commitments read as
confirmations rather than promises. The most important April event was the one
nobody announced.
