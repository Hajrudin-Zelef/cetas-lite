# June 2026

<a id="g02-7"></a>
### 2.7 June 2026

June was the month roadmaps became hardware. The first production Vera Rubin
NVL72 rack came online on June 1 at CoreWeave's Livingston facility, built with
Dell — the moment Nvidia's March GTC reveal turned into a revenue-generating
deployment. "First rack in production" is a precise milestone, and precision
matters here: not a demo unit on a keynote stage, not a press-release render,
but a customer-operated rack serving real workloads, with power, cooling,
networking and software all working. CoreWeave, the neocloud that had ridden
the GPU-shortage
era to prominence, was the fitting first customer: the company whose entire
business model is converting accelerator supply into rentable capacity, and
therefore the operator with the strongest incentive to be first. The Dell
partnership is equally load-bearing: Rubin at rack scale is a systems-
integration problem — power delivery, liquid cooling, networking fabric,
physical installation — and Dell's presence signaled that the supply chain for
NVL72-class deployments was operational, not experimental. A chip launch is a
vendor event; a first production rack with a systems integrator is an industry
event.

The same day, June 1, Intel formally launched Xeon 6+ at Computex — the
Clearwater Forest line announced at MWC in March, now shipping with up to 288
E-cores (vendor claim, from Intel's launch materials). The March-to-June arc
closed exactly as announced: Intel had promised an efficiency-core datacenter
CPU for the AI-rack era and delivered it on the Computex stage, the industry's
traditional launch venue for exactly this kind of product. The 288-core figure
positions Clearwater Forest as the density play — maximum throughput per socket
per watt for the host and inference-adjacent workloads that surround
accelerators. The strategic framing is worth restating: in an AI rack, the
expensive silicon is the accelerator; the host CPU's job is to stay out of the
way while doing everything else — orchestration, I/O, data movement — at minimum
power and maximum density. Clearwater Forest is Intel's answer to that job
description, and its June 1 launch put it in the market on the same day Rubin's
first rack went live — a coincidence of calendar that nicely captures the year's
two-track silicon story.

Late June brought the memory story back with executive authority. On June 24,
Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra told the Q3 FY2026 earnings call that shortages would
take "considerable time" to improve, with only gradual improvement expected in
2028. This was the CEO of one of the three major DRAM manufacturers telling
investors, on the record, that relief was two years out. The statement
validated SK Group chairman Chey's March warning of a
wafer shortage into 2030 and converted it from a conglomerate chairman's caution
into an industry consensus — when two of the industry's most senior voices,
three months apart, describe the same multi-year deficit, it stops being a
warning and becomes the planning baseline.

The very next day, June 25, Apple raised prices on Mac, iPad and Vision Pro,
citing memory costs — the first time in this chronology that a consumer-device
price increase was explicitly attributed to the memory squeeze rather than to
tariffs, currency or margin. The attribution matters: Apple rarely names a
component cost as the reason for a price increase, preferring to let pricing
speak for itself. Naming memory was both an explanation and a signal — to
customers (this is why), to suppliers (we are paying it), and to the market (the
squeeze has reached the price tag). Gartner's February +130% figure had been the
data; Mehrotra's guidance was the forecast; Apple's price increase was the
receipt. The causal chain that began in February was now complete and visible on
a price tag, and it would not be the last consumer consequence: the iPhone Duo's
$1,999 September price (see 2.10) was set in a market where memory had been
expensive all year.

June also produced one of the verification pass's more satisfying confirmations.
McKinsey's "Frontiers of compute" research, published in June 2026, included the
piece "The technology shifts reducing AI inference costs," which found that
optimization work had cut cost per token by 85–95%. The figure is striking
enough that it was flagged as a suspected hallucination during the mid-wave web
outage — and then confirmed real on re-verification, with the primary source
located (see 2.2). It belongs in the June narrative because it is the
counterweight to the memory-squeeze story: while memory prices were rising,
inference *efficiency* was improving dramatically — better kernels, better
quantization, better serving stacks — and the net economics of serving AI were
being pulled in both directions at once. That tension, scarcity at the input and
efficiency at the output, is the defining economic fact of 2026 infrastructure.
It also explains why the industry could simultaneously panic about memory prices
and keep building: the per-token economics were improving fast enough to justify
the buildout even as the per-gigabyte economics deteriorated. Both things were
true; June was when both became documented.
