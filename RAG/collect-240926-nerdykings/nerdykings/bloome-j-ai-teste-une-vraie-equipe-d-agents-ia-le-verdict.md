# Bloome: I Tested a Real Team of AI Agents (The Verdict)

Most of the time, you open a chatbot, write a big prompt, and hope it spits out the right result directly. Sometimes it works by sheer luck. But on a real multi-step project, this technique quickly shows its limits. I tested **Bloome**, a platform that makes several AI agents work together in the same conversation — one that builds, one that critiques, one that improves, and me keeping the final say. Here's how it plays out on a real project.

## The problem with a single prompt

In a real project, generating the final page is almost the last step. Before that, you have to understand what you're building, who you're talking to, what message to get across, what objections people might have, and how to turn a raw idea into something clear and credible. A single AI can generate quickly, but it doesn't always step back the way a real team would: it can accept a bad angle, write something that sounds good but sells nothing, or rush into execution before the problem has even been framed.

The whole idea behind Bloome is precisely to see what happens when several agents work in the same conversation with different roles, rather than betting everything on a single prompt.

## How Bloome works

After creating an account, Bloome downloads as an app on **macOS, Windows, Google Play, and iOS**. You then connect the agents you want to work together: **Codex, OpenCode, Hermes Agent, Claude Code, Gemini CLI, Grok**, or Bloome's native agent. For this test, I connected Codex, then created a small custom team: a *growth specialist*, a *brand strategist*, and a *product marketing* person, plus Codex for execution.

Each agent has its own name, profile picture, and above all **collaboration rules** that you define yourself in the group settings: who speaks first, what each one should challenge, and the ban on directly producing a final version without going through the intermediate steps.

## The test: a product team for the Nerdy Pocket

To test this under real conditions, I set up a group around a fake project: a landing page for the **Nerdy Pocket**, a fictional retrofuturistic handheld console that I regularly use to test AI tools. Rule of the game set in the collaboration rules: product marketing first defines the targets, positioning, and models; the brand strategist then establishes the brand universe, premium tone, and visual direction; the growth specialist comes in last to challenge anything too vague or too marketing-heavy, before final approval from me.

Result: instead of immediately firing off a prompt to generate the site, the team first turned the raw idea into a solid brief. The agents called on each other, waited their turn, and the growth specialist even sent back a critique listing point by point what was missing — *"zero conversion strategy"* — before I approved and we moved to execution.

## The critique loop: V1 → V2 → V3

Once the brief was set, Codex generated a first prototype based on the team's work. **V1** followed the requested structure but remained too simple, almost a placeholder. I asked for a visually more premium version, without touching the positioning or the copywriting: **V2** came back noticeably improved. Then I had the three other agents review the result, each giving their opinion on the premium direction, the conversion path, and priority fixes — leading to a **V3** incorporating their feedback.

What stands out in this process is that I'm not the one doing the back-and-forth between each agent: they review and critique each other, and I only step in to make the call.

## The final twist: replacing the agent with Claude Opus 4.8

Even after V3, I still wasn't satisfied with the result. So I made a more radical decision: replace the Codex agent with an agent running on **Claude Opus 4.8**, asking it to pick up all the other agents' work, respect the validated structure, and deliver a much more elaborate version with animations and a wow effect.

The final result — the *Nerdy Kings Ultimate Landing* — looks nothing like V1: premium retro style respected, a playable mini-game embedded directly in the page, two editions with their prices (€299 and €499), a materials section detailing brushed aluminum and gold alloy buttons, and a final call-to-action with email capture. Quite a gap from the starting prototype.

## My take

What struck me most wasn't the quality of the final render — any good code model can produce a beautiful landing page. It's the fact that the agents **call on each other, critique each other, and adjust among themselves** without me having to act as the intermediary at every step. It genuinely changes the nature of the work: you go from "I prompt and I fix" to "I supervise a team and I make the call."

Let's be honest: if your goal is to send a single prompt and wait for the result, Bloome is useless — you might as well open a classic chatbot. But if you have a real workflow, with multiple steps and the need to build progressively on a good foundation, it's clearly a tool worth testing. Free credits are added every day, which leaves plenty of room to form an opinion before pulling out your credit card.
