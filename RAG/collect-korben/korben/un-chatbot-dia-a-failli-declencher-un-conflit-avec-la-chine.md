## Full summary

The article, based on a CNN investigation, reports that an AI chatbot nearly triggered a military incident between the United States and China. According to the account, US military aircraft were already airborne when officials discovered that the intelligence justifying the interception of a Chinese ship had been produced by an AI that had misidentified the vessel's cargo.

The incident dates back to spring 2026, during the war against Iran, and concerns a ship in the Middle East supposedly carrying components for a nuclear weapons program. A special operations analyst had queried a chatbot about information concerning the ship's manifest, the document describing its cargo. This intelligence came from the US Special Operations Command in the Pacific, based in Hawaii, and the chatbot reportedly combined it with public information and secret intelligence from intercepted signals.

The response allegedly attributed nuclear material to the ship that no reliable evidence could identify there. This is described as a hallucination, where the AI produces false information presented as fact, and in this case the error was compounded by a disastrous presentation. The analyst reportedly asked the AI a second time to format the conclusions into the usual intelligence report layout before disseminating it, so recipients received a document conforming to their working habits, lending it the credibility normally granted to military analysis.

Armed soldiers were preparing to board when verification of the report stopped the operation. The testimonies describe an intervention that was prepared and then cancelled, with a risk of confrontation between Washington and Beijing, though the article cautions it cannot be asserted that a boarding would necessarily have triggered a war. The published investigation cites four sources close to the matter but does not reveal the chatbot's name or the true nature of the cargo, making it impossible to identify a vendor or even to know whether the analyst used a commercial product or a government tool.

The Pentagon is nonetheless pushing hard to accelerate adoption of such software. In January, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth presented a strategy aimed at giving three million military and civilian personnel access to AI models, while several officials describe differing verification practices depending on the services and tools used.
