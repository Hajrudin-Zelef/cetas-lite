# Bedrock and Graviton5: model catalog and in-house chip

<a id="s07-11"></a>
### 7.10 Bedrock and Graviton5: the model catalog and the in-house chip

On Bedrock, AWS's managed-model platform, 2026 was the year of abundance.
Kimi K3 went GA there on September 18, 2026, reinforcing the offering days before quarter's end.
Grok 4.6 was added on August 19, cross-region, with listed pricing of $2/$6 according to available sources.
Claude Fable 5.1 joined the catalog around September 8, classified "Covered Model."
This classification comes with a possible 30-day data retention.
GPT-6 Astra was available from launch on the platform.
And Gemma 4 is offered in three variants: 31b, 26b-a4b, and e2b.

This profusion is no accident, it is a strategy.
Bedrock does not bet on a champion model, but on the broadest catalog on the market.
Every lab finds its place there, from the largest to the most specialized.
The customer chooses its model as it chooses an instance: according to need and budget.
It is the natural extension of AgentCore's provider-agnostic positioning.
AWS sells the supermarket of models, not an exclusive brand.
And in a market where rankings change fast, the supermarket is a safe value.

Grok 4.6's cross-region illustrates another dimension of the offering.
Cross-region inference distributes requests across several regions to absorb peaks.
It is an answer to agents' fluctuating demand, which gives no warning before consuming.
The listed pricing of $2/$6, according to available sources, gives a cost benchmark.
In the token economics dominating 2026, every price benchmark counts for buyers.
Bedrock thus turns multi-region complexity into a simple pricing option.
It is the industrialization of inference, sold as a banal service.

The "Covered Model" status granted to Claude Fable 5.1 deserves decoding.
This classification comes with a possible 30-day data retention.
For regulated enterprises, these contractual parameters are as important as performance.
They determine what can be audited, retained, and proven in case of inspection.
AWS plays here the compliance card, its historical terrain of predilection.
A powerful but uncovered model will stay out of large enterprises' critical systems.
The "Covered Model" is therefore a sesame for the most demanding deployments.

Gemma 4's variants, 31b, 26b-a4b, and e2b, illustrate the offering's granularity.
Offering several sizes of the same model allows fine-tuning of the cost-performance tradeoff.
A simple agent does not need the biggest model to accomplish its task.
This granularity is at the heart of agentic economics: each call must be billed at the right price.
Bedrock thus becomes a catalog where one composes one's agent architecture piece by piece.
It is an engineer's approach, not a marketer's, and it seduces technical teams.
Variant diversity is a direct answer to usage diversity.

Meanwhile, AWS was also advancing on silicon with Graviton5.
The chip went GA on June 10, 2026, a few days before the New York Summit.
Etched in 3 nm, it packs 192 cores, a considerable figure for a cloud ARM processor.
It is the fifth generation of AWS's in-house chip, and the most ambitious to date.
The timing is no accident: announcing the chip just before the Summit sets the tone.
AWS reminds that it is also a foundry, not just an assembler of services.
Cloud verticalization goes through silicon, and Graviton5 is the proof.

Graviton5's adoption gives the measure of the stake.
Meta deploys tens of millions of Graviton cores, alongside Uber and Snowflake.
Tens of millions of cores is a mind-boggling scale.
It means AWS's in-house chip has become critical infrastructure for the biggest players.
Bloomberg even mentions hundreds of thousands of rented chips, according to available sources.
Whether this figure is exact or approximate, the order of magnitude is staggering.
It shows the in-house-silicon bet, launched years ago, finally paid off.

The Graviton5 lesson goes beyond AWS.
It shows that in 2026, cloud differentiation goes through silicon.
When compute is the raw material, whoever manufactures its raw material has an advantage.
The tens of millions of cores deployed at Meta validate the economic model.
They prove an in-house chip can rival traditional foundries at large scale.
It is a warning to Intel and AMD: their biggest customers are also becoming their competitors.
Verticalization spares no one, not even chipmakers.

The timing of Bedrock additions is not innocent.
Kimi K3 in GA on September 18, days before quarter close.
Grok 4.6 on August 19, Claude Fable 5.1 around September 8: a sustained pace.
Each addition is an announcement, each announcement a signal sent to the market.
AWS thus keeps up a media drumbeat that constantly recalls the catalog's richness.
It is a strategy of attrition: through additions, the catalog becomes unmissable.
Competitors must follow this pace or appear to lag.

The catalog war has become a front in its own right between clouds.
Bedrock bets on breadth: all labs, all sizes, all usages.
It is the opposite of an exclusivity strategy, where a cloud would attach itself to a single star model.
Breadth protects against reversals: if one model disappoints, the catalog offers ten others.
It also turns the cloud into a marketplace, taking its margin on each transaction.
In this logic, the model is a commodity and the platform is the asset.
It is a complete reversal of 2023's hierarchy of values.

The Bloomberg figure, hundreds of thousands of rented chips, opens another reading.
It suggests an economy of compute rental, not just purchase.
Renting chips by the hour turns an investment into an operational charge.
It is the cloud's historical model, applied down to silicon itself.
For customers, it is flexibility; for AWS, recurring revenue.
The in-house chip thus becomes a financial asset as much as a technical one.
And that is perhaps where Graviton's real profitability plays out.

Graviton5 finally raises the question of generalized verticalization.
If AWS can design chips adopted by tens of millions of cores, why not others?
The temptation is great for any hyperscaler to follow this path.
But designing a chip is a trade, with its long cycles and industrial risks.
AWS took years and five generations to reach this level of maturity.
Followers will have to invest massively to hope to catch up.
Graviton5 is not just a chip, it is a barrier to entry.
