## 7. Infrastructure & compute in depth

In 2026, compute became the strategic infrastructure par excellence, on a par with energy or telecommunications.
Every advance in models translates into a demand for capacity that grows faster than available supply.
The major players are locking up gigawatts, entire racks, and chip generations years in advance.
The battle is no longer just about the fastest chip, but about the cost per token, open standards, and real availability.
It is in this context that one must read Nvidia's persistent dominance, AMD's counteroffensive, Intel's comeback, and the rise of the clouds.
This section dissects the 2026 of compute, from racks to deals, through the historic September 21 trading session.

The year was marked by a shift: inference, and singularly agentic inference, became the main driver of demand.
Agents demand significant compute capacity for inference, and they run continuously, not in bursts.
This operating regime changes the economics of the industry: cost per token becomes the determining line item in AI budgets.
All of 2026's announcements read through this lens, from AMD's Helios racks to AWS's in-house chips.
Even September's stock market moves are explained by this agentic demand, as shown by the "trillion-dollar day."
Compute is no longer just an equipment line item; it is the raw material of the agent economy.

Three dynamics structure the 2026 landscape.
First, concentration: Nvidia remains the reference, and every competitor defines itself against it.
Second, diversification: hyperscalers and labs multiply suppliers to secure their supply.
Finally, financialization: warrants, cross-investments, and multi-year gigawatt contracts now bind foundries and model developers.
These three dynamics reinforce one another and sketch a market that is at once more competitive and more locked in.

### 7.1 Rubin NVL72: the 2026 reference for dense AI compute

In 2026, the Rubin NVL72 established itself as the reference for dense AI compute.
It is the yardstick everyone measures themselves against, including the most aggressive competitors.
When AMD presents its Helios racks, it is against the Rubin NVL72 that it chooses to position itself.
This choice is not insignificant: one only compares one's product to the recognized market leader.
The NVL72 thus embodies the state of the art of the dense rack for AI, the one buyers know and sellers must beat.
Its reference position makes it the obligatory passage point of all the industry's commercial rhetoric.
It is the sign of a dominance no longer debated in principle, only in its modalities.

AMD's claim — "30% more tokens per dollar" against the Rubin NVL72 — says a lot about this status.
It is not about raw power, but about the economics of inference.
Tokens per dollar has become the metric that counts for large-scale deployments.
By choosing this angle of attack, AMD implicitly acknowledges that Nvidia sets the standard.
The battle thus shifts from peak performance to cost of use.
It is an admission of market maturity: density is no longer enough, profitability is required.
And it is precisely because the NVL72 is the reference that this comparison carries argumentative force.

"Dense" compute answers a simple physical constraint: concentrate maximum compute per rack and per watt.
Models, and especially agents, multiply inference calls, and every millisecond of latency counts.
A dense rack shortens network distances between accelerators and simplifies operation at scale.
It is this logic that pushes all players toward systems designed at rack scale rather than the single server.
The NVL72 set the vocabulary of this era: we no longer talk in isolated chips, we talk in racks.
Anyone wanting to exist in this market must therefore offer a credible answer to the rack format.
Density has become the common language of tenders and announcements.

Dominance of the reference has a structuring effect on the whole software ecosystem.
Developers, frameworks, and operators optimize first for the dominant platform.
This creates a soft lock-in effect: switching providers costs more than just the price of chips.
It is precisely this lock-in that AMD attacks with its open standards and price argument.
But as long as the NVL72 remains the reference, every competitor must prove it is "just as good, for less."
Nvidia's position is therefore both a commercial asset and a designated target.
Being the reference means absorbing everyone's comparisons, constantly.

2026 saw inference demand explode with the generalization of autonomous agents.
Agents demand significant compute capacity for inference, and they operate continuously.
In this regime, cost per token becomes the determining line item in enterprise AI budgets.
The NVL72 reference then serves as the calculation basis for all buyers costing out a deployment.
It is what makes it possible to compare offers, negotiate contracts, and plan capacity.
Its centrality in 2026's discourse is therefore no accident, but the reflection of the industry's real economics.
When everyone talks in tokens per dollar, it means inference has taken power.

Nothing in 2026 indicates this reference is challenged on the terrain of pure density.
The challenge is about price, openness of standards, and availability, not the top of the pyramid.
It is an enviable position: being attacked on margins rather than technological leadership.
For buyers, this competition is good news, as it puts pressure on prices.
For Nvidia, the challenge is to defend token economics without eroding its own margins.
The NVL72 will remain the 2026 yardstick, and keeping that status is already a victory in itself.
The real question for 2027 will be whether the reference shifts toward cost rather than density.

In 2026's negotiations, the Rubin NVL72 also serves as an implicit unit of account.
When a buyer evaluates a competing offer, they mentally convert it into NVL72 equivalents.
That is what references do: they become the currency of comparisons.
AMD's salespeople know this, which is why they attack this reference head-on.
Not comparing oneself to the NVL72 would be admitting one plays in another category.
The comparison is therefore a tribute as much as an attack.
It installs Helios in the same conversation as the leader, which is already a positioning victory.

A single reference's dominance also carries systemic risk.
When the whole ecosystem optimizes for the same platform, technological diversity impoverishes.
A monoculture is efficient in the short term, but fragile in the long term.
This is one of the implicit arguments of open-standards advocates.
Supplier diversity is not just a price question, but a resilience one.
The most sophisticated buyers understood this, hence their multi-supplier strategy.
The NVL72 benefits from monoculture while being threatened by the desire for diversity.

For CIOs, having a reference simplifies decisions.
It provides a stable comparison point in a market that moves very fast.
Enterprises' internal benchmarks align with the dominant platform's performance.
This paradoxically reduces the cost of evaluating alternatives, to the challengers' benefit.
For once the reference is established, one need only show one beats it on a given criterion.
AMD understood this well with its tokens-per-dollar argument.
The reference is therefore a double-edged sword for whoever holds it.

Being the reference also confers normative power over innovation.
It is the dominant platform that defines the de facto formats, precisions, and interfaces.
Others must adapt or convince the market to change norms.
AMD's bet on open Ethernet is precisely an attempt to move the norm.
If open standards prevail, the reference loses part of its lock-in power.
It is a long-term battle, fought as much in standards committees as in the market.
In 2026, the NVL72 keeps the advantage, but the challenge is organizing.
