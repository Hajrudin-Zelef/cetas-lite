# The US–China dialogue on AI safety

<a id="s10-6"></a>
### 10.5 The US–China dialogue on AI safety

On September 5, 2026, the Reuters news agency reveals that a bilateral dialogue between the United States and China, devoted exclusively to artificial intelligence, is planned for mid-September.
The information is considerable for a simple reason: this would be the first bilateral discussion devoted exclusively to AI since Donald Trump's return to the presidency.
Since that return, the two powers have clashed over trade, chips, Taiwan, and many other fronts, but never had they sat at a table dedicated solely to AI.
The mere fact of announcing such a meeting is already a signal of partial de-escalation: AI moves from the status of a trade-war weapon to that of a negotiating object in its own right.
On the American side, the delegation is led by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent — a choice that deserves attention.
It is neither the Secretary of State, nor the National Security Advisor, nor a special envoy for technology who leads: it is the head of the Treasury.
The message is that AI is now treated as a subject of political economy and economic security as much as a diplomatic or military one.
The Treasury, which administers sanctions and export controls, is also the instrument of American technological coercion: the same man who holds the export-controls stick holds the word of dialogue.

The dialogue's agenda, as reported, has three parts, and each is a world unto itself.
First part: cooperation on AI-driven cyberattacks.
Both countries endure — and, in all likelihood, conduct — cyber operations in which AI plays a growing role: large-scale phishing, automated vulnerability exploitation, synthetic disinformation.
Talking about "cooperation" on this subject between the world's two leading cyber powers is an exercise of extreme difficulty, since each suspects the other of being both the firefighter and the arsonist.
But the simple fact of putting the subject on the agenda implicitly recognizes that AI-augmented cyberattacks constitute a shared systemic risk, including for their presumed authors: an uncontrolled escalation of AI-versus-AI operations would ill serve the interests of both capitals.
Second part: the "self-policing" of laboratories — in English in the reported facts, "self-policing" of labs.
The expression denotes the idea that AI laboratories themselves, American and Chinese, should equip themselves with mechanisms of self-discipline and mutual oversight, beyond state regulations.
It is a striking idea: it shifts part of governance toward the actors themselves, betting that the labs of both countries have a common interest in avoiding catastrophes — much as central banks cooperate despite the rivalry of their states.
What "self-policing" concretely covers remains to be seen: common charters, incident-information exchanges, shared evaluation standards?
The verified facts do not detail it, and it must be flagged as a blind spot rather than speculated upon.
Third part: Chinese distillation.
"Distillation" denotes the practice of training a model by relying on the outputs of a more powerful model — typically, a Chinese laboratory distilling the capabilities of an American frontier model.
This is one of the most sensitive American grievances: the United States invests hundreds of millions in training frontier models, and distillation would allow Chinese competition to capture part of those capabilities at lower cost.
Putting distillation on the agenda of a bilateral dialogue is elevating it to the rank of an official diplomatic issue, on par with cyberattacks.
It is also recognizing, implicitly, that export controls on chips are not enough to protect the American lead if model capabilities can be transferred by simply querying APIs.

On September 20, 2026, the announced dialogue takes shape: Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng meet in New York, for a conversation of about eight hours.
Eight hours face-to-face between the American Treasury Secretary and the Chinese Vice Premier: this is a serious negotiation format, not a protocol handshake.
From this meeting emerges a precise American proposal: the creation of an AI incident notification mechanism at the national-security level.
The idea is modeled on Cold War mechanisms — the "hotline" between Washington and Moscow — transposed to the age of models: if an AI-related incident threatens the national security of either country, the other must be notified without delay through a dedicated channel.
This is a proposal of considerable scope: it assumes the two countries agree on what an "AI incident at national-security level" is, that they accept sharing potentially sensitive information about their own vulnerabilities, and that they trust the channel not to be instrumentalized.
That the proposal comes from the United States is consistent with the initial agenda: after "cooperation" on cyberattacks, here is the operational instrument that would make it concrete.
In parallel, both parties agree to formalize a "US-China AI dialogue" — a lasting discussion framework, not just a one-off meeting.
Formalization is a classic but essential diplomatic step: it turns an event into a process, with appointments, working groups, and institutional memory.
Finally, the sequence projects into the future: a next meeting between Trump and Xi is planned for September 24 in Washington.
Eight days after New York, the two presidents meet in the American capital: the technical Bessent–He Lifeng dialogue thus appears as the preparation for a political summit.
This is the complete diplomatic sequence: announcement (September 5), technical negotiation (September 20, eight hours), leaders' summit (September 24).

The analysis of this sequence must remain cautious about what the facts do not say.
The verified facts do not say whether the incident-notification mechanism was accepted by China: it is an American proposal, not a concluded agreement.
They do not say whether the formalized "US-China AI dialogue" has a calendar, a written mandate, or deliverables.
They do not say what Trump and Xi will decide on September 24 in Washington.
What they do say — and this is already much — is that the United States and China chose, in September 2026, to talk about AI face-to-face, at length, at the highest technical level, with an agenda mixing cyberattacks, laboratory discipline, and distillation.
In a context where, simultaneously, Washington suspends models for export (see 10.6) and Beijing blocks American acquisitions (see 10.6), this dialogue is not the end of rivalry: it is its channeling.
Both countries seem to converge toward an implicit doctrine: fierce competition on means (chips, models, talent), but open communication on accidents.
This is the grammar of deterrence transposed to AI: one fights for superiority, one talks to avoid catastrophe.
If the notification mechanism saw the light of day, it would be the first mutual-trust infrastructure of the frontier-models era — modest, technical, but historic.

**Deep dive — chronology, files, and blind spots**

Let us retake the sequence in order, for its density over three weeks is itself diplomatic information:

- 05/09/2026: Reuters reveals that a bilateral dialogue devoted exclusively to AI is planned for mid-September — a first since Trump's return.
- Mid-September 2026: planned holding of the dialogue (the announcement precedes the event).
- 20/09/2026: Bessent–He Lifeng meeting in New York, about eight hours of talks; American proposal of an AI incident notification mechanism at national-security level; agreement to formalize a "US-China AI dialogue."
- 24/09/2026: planned meeting between Trump and Xi in Washington.

Three weeks between the revelation and the presidents' summit: this is a fast diplomatic tempo, suggesting earlier preparation — one does not set up in three weeks an eight-hour meeting between the Treasury Secretary and a Vice Premier, followed four days later by a presidential summit, without months of prior contacts.
The September 5 Reuters revelation is therefore probably the tip of an older underground effort: the public announcement is only the formalization of an already-engaged process.
This is an analytical reading, but it imposes itself: diplomacy does not sprint, it reveals.

The choice of Scott Bessent as head of the American delegation deserves a deeper look.
The Treasury Secretary is, in the architecture of American power, the guardian of economic sanctions and the administrator of export controls.
He is therefore the man who holds, day to day, the instruments of technological coercion against China: entity lists, chip restrictions, investment screening.
Sending him to negotiate the AI dialogue is sending the jailer to discuss prison rules with the inmate — or, in a more charitable reading, it guarantees that the word given at the negotiating table will be consistent with the acts of the controls administration.
In both readings, the message is the same: for Washington, AI is first and foremost an economic-security file, and the Treasury is in charge of it.
On the Chinese side, He Lifeng is Vice Premier — a rank which, in the Party-State hierarchy, corresponds to a top-level decision-maker, not a mere emissary.
Eight hours of talks at this level means eight hours during which both systems put their highest economic decision-makers face-to-face: AI is treated as a general-management subject of both states, not a technical file.

Let us detail the three agenda items, distinguishing what the facts say from what they leave in shadow.
First item: cooperation on AI-driven cyberattacks.
What the facts say: the subject is on the agenda, both countries envisage cooperating.
What they do not say: the nature of this cooperation — intelligence exchange, common norms of restraint, alert mechanism?
The obstacle is structural: each country is suspected by the other of being the author of some of the attacks the dialogue claims to combat.
Sincere cooperation would therefore assume a form of mutual admission — or at minimum a tacit agreement not to accuse each other —, which makes it the most delicate diplomatic exercise of the three items.
Second item: the "self-policing" of laboratories.
What the facts say: the idea that labs themselves should discipline themselves is on the table.
What they do not say: everything else — who participates, under what rules, with what state oversight?
The idea is attractive in theory: American and Chinese laboratories share an objective interest in avoiding major accidents, which would discredit the entire industry and provoke brutal regulations.
It is dizzying in practice: asking geostrategic competitors to monitor each other assumes a trust that the chip war has methodically destroyed.
Third item: Chinese distillation.
What the facts say: the subject is on the agenda, carried by the Americans.
What they do not say: what Washington is exactly asking for — ban, limitation, transparency?
Distillation is the most concrete friction point of the three: it is a measurable transfer of value — American frontier capabilities replicated at lower cost — and it is the software bypass of hardware controls.
If export controls on chips are the wall, distillation is the tunnel: putting the tunnel on the agenda means admitting the wall is not enough.

The American proposal of a national-security-level incident notification mechanism is the most concrete innovation to come out of New York — and it must be weighed at its true value.
Such a mechanism would assume three prior agreements, none of which is attested by the verified facts.
First agreement: a common definition of the "national-security-level AI incident."
A model escaping a laboratory, a massive AI-assisted cyberattack, a failure of an autonomous weapons system: do all of these fall under the mechanism?
Without a shared definition, the channel will remain a dead letter or be invoked wrongly and excessively.
Second agreement: a commitment to rapid and sincere notification, including when the incident reveals one's own vulnerability.
But notifying means exposing oneself: admitting an incident means admitting a flaw, and in a strategic rivalry, the admission of weakness has a cost.
Third agreement: protecting the channel against instrumentalization — false alerts, information fishing, diplomatic use of notifications.
The verified facts present the proposal as American, without indicating the Chinese response: it is therefore an offer on the table, not a concluded agreement, and it must be flagged as such.
If it were accepted, it would be the first reciprocal-trust infrastructure of the frontier-models era — the functional equivalent, for AI, of the Cold War hotline.

The "US-China AI dialogue" to be formalized and the September 24 Trump–Xi summit in Washington complete the picture.
Formalizing a dialogue means turning it into an institution: regular meetings, an agenda, minutes — and therefore a memory, which prevents starting everything from scratch at each meeting.
It is also a commitment to continuity that will, in principle, survive the moods of the moment.
The September 24 summit, four days after New York, places the sequence under the sign of political validation: Bessent and He Lifeng prepare, Trump and Xi decide.
The verified facts announce this meeting as planned: its outcome — agreement, joint declaration, or mere family photo — is not documented in the dossier, and it would be dishonest to prejudge it.

**Deep dive — New York, eight hours: anatomy of a negotiation**

Eight hours: let us pause on this figure, for it is one of the most telling pieces of information in the dossier.
In diplomacy, the duration of a meeting is a language in itself.
A protocol handshake lasts twenty minutes; a working meeting, one to two hours; a substantive negotiation, an entire day with breaks, asides, back-and-forth between delegations.
Eight hours between Bessent and He Lifeng, on September 20, 2026 in New York, is therefore a substantive negotiation — with, necessarily, support teams, documents on the table, and partial points of agreement built up over the hours.
One does not stay eight hours face-to-face to note a disagreement: one stays because one advances, point by point, on a loaded agenda — cyberattacks, lab self-policing, distillation, notification mechanism.
This is an analytical reading, but it is robust: the duration is a fact, and the fact speaks.

The venue also counts: New York, not Washington, not Beijing, not Geneva.
New York is the financial capital of the world — and the Treasury Secretary is at home there.
It is also a city where both delegations can meet with relative discretion, away from White House cameras and the gilt of official summits.
The choice of New York for eight hours of technical negotiation, four days before a presidential summit in Washington, draws a clear division of labor: in New York, one negotiates; in Washington, one signs — or one notes.
This is the classic method of great diplomatic sequences: the technical "sherpa" prepares, the leaders decide.
Bessent is the sherpa, Trump will be the decider; He Lifeng is the sherpa, Xi will be the decider.

The incident-notification-mechanism proposal deserves a final deeper look, for it is the only concrete deliverable — even at the proposal stage — to emerge from the sequence.
Let us imagine its operation, staying at the level of principle — the verified facts do not give its design, and this must be recalled.
A state detects an AI incident relevant to national security — for example, a model exhibiting unexpectedly dangerous behavior, or a massive AI-assisted attack on infrastructure.
Through the dedicated channel, it notifies the other state: nature of the incident, estimated scale, measures taken.
The other state acknowledges receipt, possibly shares its own information, and both agree on de-escalation measures if the incident has a bilateral dimension.
The historical model is the Kennedy–Khrushchev hotline after the Cuban Missile Crisis: a direct line to prevent the accident from becoming war.
Transposed to AI, the stake is the same: preventing the incident — the derailed model, the degenerating attack — from being interpreted as a deliberate aggression.
For the most dreaded risk in both capitals may not be AI itself, but misperception: a technical failure read as an attack, an attack read as a declaration of war.
The notification mechanism is an anti-misperception machine — modest, technical, and potentially vital.

But the inverse hypothesis must also be considered — always as analysis, never as assertion: what if the mechanism also served intelligence purposes?
Notifying an incident means revealing a vulnerability: the model that derailed, the infrastructure that was breached, the laboratory that lost control.
In a strategic rivalry, every shared piece of information is exploited — or exploitable — information.
The negotiators of both countries know this, and this is why the design of the channel — what one says in it, what one does not say, who has access — will be at least as disputed as its principle.
The American proposal, as of the verified facts, is on the table; the Chinese response is not documented; and the design of the mechanism — if it sees the light of day — will be the true test of reciprocal trust.
A channel in which one notifies only already-public incidents serves no purpose; a channel in which one notifies real vulnerabilities assumes a trust that the chip war has not built.
Between the two lies the entire space of negotiation — and it is there, in that space, that the future of the dialogue will be decided.

The September 24 Trump–Xi summit in Washington is the announced climax of the sequence — and its outcome is not documented in the verified facts, which must be said with clarity.
Three typical outcomes, in prospective analysis.
The consecration outcome: joint declaration, announcement of the notification mechanism, calendar of the formalized "US-China AI dialogue" — the September sequence would become the model of Sino-American AI relations.
The status-quo outcome: handshake, vague communiqué, referral of files back to the sherpas — the dialogue would continue, without deliverables, awaiting a better moment.
The rupture outcome: an incident — commercial, military, or AI-related — would derail the summit, and the September sequence would remain as a missed opportunity.
Whatever the outcome, the mere fact of having held, in September 2026, a bilateral dialogue devoted exclusively to AI — eight hours in New York, a summit in Washington — will have set a precedent: AI is now a head-to-head subject between the world's two leading powers, on par with trade, currency, or weapons.
This precedent will survive the summit's outcome, whatever it is.
