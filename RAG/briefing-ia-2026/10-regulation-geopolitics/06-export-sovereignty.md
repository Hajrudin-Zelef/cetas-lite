# Export controls and technological sovereignty

<a id="s10-7"></a>
### 10.6 Export controls and technological sovereignty

The year 2026 saw export controls cross an unprecedented boundary: for the first time, it is no longer just chips or machines that are hit, but frontier AI models themselves, at the scale of an entire country.
And while Washington locks down, the other powers organize: sovereign mergers, acquisition blocks, compute alliances.
Technological sovereignty is no longer a slogan, it is a budget line and a corporate strategy.

The most striking event is the Fable/Mythos 5 suspension, between June 12 and July 1, 2026.
The verified facts describe it as the first export control hitting frontier models at the scale of a country.
The novelty must be measured: until now, American export controls targeted hardware — GPUs, lithography equipment, components — with the idea that depriving the adversary of compute was depriving it of AI.
Hitting a model is changing the target: it is no longer computing capacity that is restricted, it is the diffusion of the software itself, of its weights, of its capabilities.
And doing so "at the scale of a country" is admitting that the model has become a strategic asset on par with weaponry: one does not export a frontier model to a given country as one does not export missiles.
The suspension window — June 12 to July 1, about two and a half weeks — suggests a targeted, reversible gesture rather than a permanent embargo: a demonstration of capability as much as a sanction.
The message to industry is nonetheless permanent: no American laboratory can any longer consider the global distribution of its models an acquired right; every frontier deployment is now conditioned on geopolitics.
For the targeted countries, the message is symmetrical: dependence on American models is a strategic vulnerability, and autonomy — or distillation, see 10.5 — becomes a necessity.

Second event: the merger between Cohere and Aleph Alpha, announced September 16, 2026.
The verified facts present it as the first transatlantic sovereign AI stack, with an explicit anti-CLOUD Act positioning.
Let us unpack: a "transatlantic sovereign stack" is the ambition to build a complete chain — models, infrastructure, deployment — under allied European and North American jurisdiction, without dependence on American hyperscalers subject to the CLOUD Act.
The CLOUD Act is the American law that allows US authorities to demand access to data held by American companies, wherever in the world it is stored: it is the dread of European compliance departments, which see in it a legal backdoor in any American cloud.
An "anti-CLOUD Act" positioning is therefore a commercial promise as much as a political posture: "your data will never be accessible to Washington."
That this is the first stack of this kind at transatlantic scale makes it a milestone: European AI sovereignty no longer runs only through speeches or public funding, but through capital operations that assemble champions.
Cohere, on the North American side, and Aleph Alpha, on the European side, embody two cultures of enterprise AI and compliance: their merger is the bet that the sum of the two creates an actor capable of rivaling the hyperscalers on the lucrative, strategic segment of sovereign deployments for governments and regulated industries.

Third event, the most brutal: China blocks Meta's acquisition of Manus, in February 2026, for $2 billion.
The verified facts qualify it as the first forced dismantling — meaning: the first time Beijing has scuttled an American acquisition of a Chinese (or Chinese-jurisdiction) AI asset of this magnitude.
Two billion dollars: this is a considerable ticket, which says the strategic interest Meta had in Manus — an AI agent whose value, in Meta's eyes, justified one of the year's largest AI acquisitions.
The Chinese block is the exact mirror of American export controls: Washington prevents its technologies from going to China, Beijing prevents its gems from being absorbed by America.
The symmetry is perfect and it installs a new regime: the global market for AI acquisitions is now a licensed market, where every significant cross-border operation must pass the national-security filter of both capitals.
For investors and founders, the lesson is harsh: an AI startup's valuation no longer depends only on its technology and its market, but on its "nationality" and the list of politically possible acquirers.
The February 2026 "first forced dismantling" will remain as the moment when China showed it too knew how to wield the regulatory weapon — and would not hesitate to use it against the GAFAM.

Fourth event: the agreement between Microsoft and Mistral, concluded July 21, 2026, which installs Vera Rubin GPUs in Mistral's European data centers.
This is the other face of sovereignty: no longer the defensive (blocking, merging against), but the offensive by alliance.
Microsoft, the American hyperscaler par excellence, chooses to anchor part of its next-generation compute — Vera Rubin GPUs, the generation following current architectures — in the European data centers of Mistral, the French champion of open models.
For Europe, this is a compute-sovereignty victory: latest-generation chips run on European soil, in European infrastructure, in service of a European actor.
For Microsoft, this is fine positioning: rather than confronting European sovereignist discourse head-on, the company embraces it, installs itself in it, and becomes indispensable to its realization.
For Mistral, this is a change of scale: access to Vera Rubin GPUs via a partner of Microsoft's size propels its training and inference capabilities into another category.
The agreement illustrates a broader lesson of 2026: sovereignty is no longer obtained by refusing the Americans, but by negotiating territorial anchoring with them — compute remains globalized, but it territorializes by contract.

Fifth fact: 1.3 million Nvidia chips in Malaysia, under export controls.
The figure is staggering: 1.3 million units is the equivalent of several leading data centers, concentrated in a single Southeast Asian country.
Malaysia has become, in recent years, a hub for semiconductor assembly and testing, and a potential circumvention destination for restrictions targeting China.
That 1.3 million Nvidia chips are there under the export-controls regime means Washington is closely watching GPU flows to and from this hub — whether to authorize them under conditions or to prevent their re-export to prohibited destinations.
This single figure tells the whole difficulty of export controls in the era of globalized supply chains: chips no longer go directly from an American manufacturer to a Chinese customer; they transit, are assembled, tested, and redistributed in third countries.
Controlling 1.3 million chips in Malaysia means admitting that control is no longer played at American borders, but in the warehouses and factories of Southeast Asia.
It is also an indication of the scale of demand: even under constraint, the world keeps absorbing colossal volumes of Nvidia compute.

The synthesis of these five events draws the map of 2026's technology war.
The United States strikes upstream (suspension of frontier models at country scale, June 12 to July 1) and logistically downstream (monitoring of 1.3 million chips in Malaysia).
China strikes back on capital (block of Meta's acquisition of Manus, $2 billion, February).
Europe and its allies build alternatives (Cohere–Aleph Alpha merger on September 16, anti-CLOUD Act sovereign stack; Microsoft–Mistral agreement on July 21, Vera Rubin GPUs in Europe).
None of these moves is isolated: together, they describe the methodical fragmentation of the global AI ecosystem into technology blocs whose interoperability — of models, chips, capital — is now conditioned on politics.
The paradox of 2026 is that this fragmentation coexists with the gestures of cooperation described elsewhere in this section: the Carolina Principles unanimously approved at the G20, the eight-hour Bessent–He Lifeng dialogue in New York.
Fragmentation of means, cooperation on accidents: this is the complete grammar of the year.

**Deep dive — the new grammar of controls**

Five gestures, one map — let us recap first, for the density of 2026 on this front is exceptional:

- February 2026: China blocks Meta's acquisition of Manus ($2 billion) — first forced dismantling.
- 12/06–01/07/2026: Fable/Mythos 5 suspension — first export control hitting frontier models at the scale of a country.
- 21/07/2026: Microsoft × Mistral agreement — Vera Rubin GPUs in Mistral's European data centers.
- 16/09/2026: Cohere + Aleph Alpha merger — first transatlantic sovereign AI stack, anti-CLOUD Act positioning.
- 2026: 1.3 million Nvidia chips in Malaysia under the export-controls regime.

The Fable/Mythos 5 suspension deserves all its doctrinal consequences drawn, for it is a precedent — and precedents, in matters of controls, are meant to be reused.
First lesson: the perimeter of export controls has extended from hardware to software, and from software to models.
For decades, controlling exports meant controlling objects: machine tools, components, chips.
Hitting a frontier model means controlling an immaterial capability — weights, behaviors, encapsulated know-how.
This is a change of nature: the controlled object is no longer a thing one can count at customs, it is a digitally reproducible artifact ad infinitum, whose "delivery" can take the form of an API, a download, or simple access.
Second lesson: the "country" scale is an escalation.
One no longer sanctions a company or a laboratory suspected of diversion: one deprives an entire country of access to a model.
This is the logic of arms embargoes applied to AI — and it assumes the model is now classified, in the minds of American authorities, among the strategic assets whose diffusion is a national-security matter.
Third lesson: the duration — June 12 to July 1, about two and a half weeks — suggests a reversible pressure instrument rather than a definitive ban.
This is the grammar of modern sanctions: strike fast, hard, and temporarily, to obtain a behavior, then lift — or threaten to repeat.
American laboratories must conclude that their international deployment roadmaps are now geopolitical variables: every frontier launch carries a risk of administrative suspension.

The block of Meta's acquisition of Manus — $2 billion, February 2026 — is the exact Chinese counterpart of this logic, and its symmetry must be measured.
The United States says: our models do not go to you.
China answers: your capital does not take our gems.
In both cases, the weapon is regulatory, the target is AI, and the message is sovereignty.
The "first forced dismantling" is a formula that says what it means: this is not a mere refusal of authorization, it is the administrative destruction of an already-negotiated, already-priced — $2 billion —, already-announced operation.
For boards of directors and investment funds, the lesson is brutal: every significant cross-border operation on an AI asset now carries a political execution risk, which must be provisioned as a financial risk.
"Regulatory approval" clauses in AI acquisition contracts will become entire chapters, with Beijing scenarios and Washington scenarios.
And for Chinese founders, the message is equally clear: the American exit — acquisition by a GAFAM — is no longer a default option; growth trajectories that do not depend on it will have to be built.

The Cohere + Aleph Alpha merger (September 16, 2026) and the Microsoft × Mistral agreement (July 21, 2026) are the two European — or Euro-Atlantic — responses to this hardening, and they embody two different sovereignty strategies.
The Cohere–Aleph Alpha strategy is that of independence by assembly: two champions — one North American, one European — merge to constitute a complete stack freed from American hyperscalers.
The anti-CLOUD Act positioning is the commercial key of the operation: it is about selling to governments and regulated industries the guarantee that their data will never be accessible to American authorities.
This is an argument that could not have existed ten years ago — when the American cloud was perceived as neutral — and which has become a major selling point in 2026.
The Microsoft–Mistral strategy is the inverse: it is sovereignty by anchoring.
Instead of fleeing the American hyperscaler, one attracts it onto European soil and negotiates the installation of its most advanced GPUs — the Vera Rubin — in European data centers operated by a local champion.
This is a different bet: rather than building a complete alternative to the GAFAM, one makes the GAFAM indispensable to European sovereignty by territorializing them.
The two strategies can coexist — and even complement each other: the sovereign stack for the most sensitive workloads, the territorialized alliance for raw computing power.
Together, they tell of a Europe that has ceased choosing between autonomy and alliance, and now practices both simultaneously.

The case of the 1.3 million Nvidia chips in Malaysia is the logistical reminder of all this geopolitics: controls are played in warehouses, not in speeches.
Malaysia has established itself as a semiconductor assembly and testing hub, at the crossroads of Asian supply chains.
Concentrating 1.3 million Nvidia chips there under the export-controls regime means recognizing that the map of GPU flows no longer runs through direct, legible routes, but through hubs where chips transit, are transformed, and are redistributed.
For Washington, the stake is twofold: authorizing legitimate flows — Malaysia has a real electronics industry that needs compute — while preventing re-exports to prohibited destinations.
This is surgical-precision control applied to industrial volumes: 1.3 million units is the scale of a data-center fleet, not a sample shipment.
The figure also says the scale of global compute demand: even under the strictest controls ever imposed on semiconductors, the world absorbs millions of cutting-edge GPUs.
The chip war has not reduced the thirst for compute — it has only made it more expensive, slower, and more political.

**Deep dive — who pays for fragmentation?**

The 2026 technology war has a cost, and it must be tallied in actors — even if the verified facts give no amounts beyond the $2 billion of the Manus file, the analysis of losers and winners remains possible from the documented events.
First payer: the American frontier laboratories.
The Fable/Mythos 5 suspension (June 12–July 1) taught them that every international deployment is a geopolitical variable: each roadmap must now provision an administrative-suspension risk — dedicated legal teams, fallback scenarios, variable-geometry calendars.
This is a new fixed cost, which weighs all the heavier for being unpredictable: one does not easily provision a two-and-a-half-week suspension decided for national-security reasons.
Second payer: startups and their investors.
The block of Meta's acquisition of Manus ($2 billion, February) introduced a political execution risk into every cross-border operation: valuations must now integrate the probability that an American acquirer will be barred by Beijing — or that a Chinese acquirer will be barred by Washington.
This risk is paid in discount: technology being equal, a startup "acquirable only by national actors" is worth less than a startup "acquirable by everyone."
Third payer: compute buyers.
The 1.3 million Nvidia chips in Malaysia under export controls tell of a market where the cutting-edge GPU has become a quasi-strategic good: lengthened lead times, prices raised by administered scarcity, uncertainty over deliveries.
Those building data centers in 2026 pay the geopolitical premium of compute.

But fragmentation also has its winners — and they are the ones who explain why the movement sustains itself.
First winner: the sovereignty champions.
The Cohere + Aleph Alpha merger (September 16) and the Microsoft × Mistral agreement (July 21) show there is now a solvent market for "sovereign" AI — governments, hospitals, banks, regulated industries — willing to pay more for jurisdictional (anti-CLOUD Act) or territorial (Vera Rubin GPUs in Europe) guarantees.
This market did not exist at this scale before the technology war: the war created it, and its beneficiaries have no interest in its end.
Second winner: the intermediaries of compliance and legal circumvention — lawyers, consultants, auditors — for whom each new control is a new engagement.
Third winner, more subtle: the states themselves, which have discovered in controls an instrument of power.
Washington holds models and chips, Beijing holds acquisitions and the rare earths of the chain (even if this last point goes beyond the facts of the dossier), Brussels holds the single market and its calendar.
Each bloc has its currency of exchange, and the permanent negotiation — Bessent–He Lifeng dialogue, Carolina Principles, summits — is the market where these currencies are traded.
Fragmentation is therefore not an accident that diplomacy would repair: it is a system from which actors draw rents, and rents defend themselves.

Final piece of the picture: the map of dependencies that 2026 revealed in negative.
The country-scale model suspension reveals the dependence of targeted countries on American models.
The 1.3 million chips figure in Malaysia reveals the entire world's dependence on Nvidia compute — and, through it, on the American and Taiwanese supply chain.
The Manus block reveals the dependence of Chinese startups on American acquirers for their exits.
The Microsoft–Mistral agreement reveals Europe's dependence on latest-generation American GPUs, even when it installs them on its soil.
Each 2026 event is thus an X-ray: it shows who depends on whom, and it is this map of dependencies — more than speeches — that will dictate the moves of the coming years.
For in matters of sovereignty, the rule is simple: one only strikes where the other depends on you, and one only protects oneself where one depends on the other.
2026 was the year of the inventory; the following years will be those of exploiting the inventory.
