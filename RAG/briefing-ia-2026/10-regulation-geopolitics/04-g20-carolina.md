# G20 and the 'Carolina Principles'

<a id="s10-5"></a>
### 10.4 G20 — The "Carolina Principles"

On September 1–2, 2026, the G20 Innovation Ministerial was held in Chapel Hill, North Carolina.
At the end of those two days, the twenty member nations unanimously approved the "Carolina Principles," a set of AI governance principles proposed by the United States — carried, on the American side, by Lutnick and Kratsios.
The unanimity of the twenty is the event: on a subject as divisive as AI regulation, getting China, Europe, India, and the United States to agree on the same text is a diplomatic feat.
But the content of the text largely explains this unanimity: the principles are non-binding, they create no new regulatory body, they favor sectoral approaches, and they call for regulating demonstrated risks rather than hypothetical ones.
In other words, the text asks everyone to do what they already do, without imposing any change of method: it is the smallest common denominator that could bring twenty capitals together.

Each element of this content deserves to be weighed, because each is an American doctrinal victory.
"Non-binding": the Carolina Principles are not a treaty, create no legal obligation, provide for no sanction.
A state can approve them in the morning and legislate the opposite in the afternoon without violating any commitment.
This is the condition of unanimity, but it is also its limit: the text engages morally, not legally.
"No new regulatory bodies": this is the explicit refusal of the temptation — very European in spirit — to create an international AI agency on the model of the IAEA for nuclear.
The United States, which fears such a body would become a brake on its technological lead or a lever of adverse influence, obtained that governance remain decentralized.
"Sectoral approaches": rather than a single cross-cutting framework for "AI" in general, the principles recommend regulating sector by sector — health, finance, transport, defense — relying on existing regulators.
This is the historic American doctrine versus the European cross-cutting doctrine: Washington has always preferred entrusting medical AI to the FDA and financial AI to banking regulators rather than creating an AI regulator as such.
"Demonstrated rather than hypothetical risks": this is the clause with the heaviest philosophical consequences.
It sets aside — or at least demotes — regulation based on future catastrophe scenarios, existential risks, and speculative projections, authorizing only the regulation of already-observed harms.
For proponents of preventive action on frontier models, this is a lock; for industry, it is a guarantee against anticipatory overregulation.

The staging of the event is itself a message.
Present in Chapel Hill were three of the biggest names in industry: Huang, the head of Nvidia; Musk; and Altman.
The simultaneous presence of the near-monopolistic supplier of compute (Nvidia), the most disruptive entrepreneur (Musk), and the head of the most prominent laboratory (Altman) turns a G20 ministerial into a show of force by the American ecosystem.
The message addressed to the nineteen other nations is twofold: America innovates, and America writes the rules of the game — or at least the principles.
That the principles were "proposed by the US" and approved unanimously gives Washington considerable diplomatic capital: this is the first time an American-inspired AI governance framework has gathered the formal assent of China and Europe together.

But the status of the text must be signaled with precision, for this is an approximation not to be made: formal adoption by the leaders has not yet taken place.
The Carolina Principles were approved by innovation ministers, not by heads of state and government.
Formal adoption is planned at the December leaders' summit, to be held in Doral, Florida — and it remains, as of the verified facts, still to come.
Until the leaders endorse it, the ministerial approval remains a second-level commitment, more easily reversible.
The history of international summits is full of ministerial texts unanimously hailed and then quietly buried at leaders' level; nothing allows asserting that this will be the case here, but nothing allows ruling it out either.
The December Doral summit will therefore be the real test: either the leaders formally adopt the principles and give them the supreme political seal, or the Chapel Hill unanimity will remain a fine diplomatic moment with no binding aftermath.

The geopolitical analysis of the Carolina Principles must also integrate their articulation with the dossier's other fronts.
On one side, the principles say "no new bodies" and "demonstrated risks" — a line that suits the United States and which, note, frontally collides with the most radical internal American proposals, such as the permanent superintelligence ban carried by Sanders and Casar (see 10.7).
On the other, the unanimity including China is to be set against the US–China bilateral dialogue described in the next subsection: the two capitals can sign the same non-binding principles at twenty while waging, at two, a fierce technological competition over chips, models, and talent.
The principles prevent neither export controls, nor acquisition blocks, nor the data-center race: they coexist with the technology war, they do not suspend it.
This may be their real function: offering a veneer of multilateral cooperation while bilateral competition rages.
Finally, for Europe, the approval of the principles — sectoral, non-binding, based on demonstrated risks — is a diplomatic gesture that changes nothing in its internal calendar: the Digital Omnibus and its deadlines (December 2026, December 2027, August 2028) continue to apply, Chapel Hill principles or not.

**Deep dive — anatomy of an unanimity**

How could twenty nations with divergent interests unanimously approve, on September 1–2, 2026 in Chapel Hill, a text proposed by the United States?
The answer lies in the grammar of the text: each bloc finds its account in it, because the text demands nothing of anyone.
Let us review the possible readings, presenting them as analyses — for the delegations' real motivations do not appear in the verified facts.

For the United States, the Carolina Principles are a pure doctrinal victory.
Non-binding, no new bodies, sectoral approaches, demonstrated risks: this is the catechism of American technology regulation for decades, now initialed by nineteen other capitals.
Lutnick and Kratsios, who carried the text, can return to Washington with a tangible diplomatic success to tout before Congress and industry.
For China, signing a non-binding text that creates no body and imposes no obligation is a zero-cost gesture: Beijing concedes nothing on its model exports, its acquisitions, or its chip policy, and gains in return an image as a constructive partner.
For Europe, approval is equally costless internally: the principles call into question neither the Digital Omnibus nor its calendar (December 2026, December 2027, August 2028), and the sectoral approach the text advocates is compatible with the reality of European sectoral regulators.
For the emerging powers — India, Brazil, Indonesia, South Africa — a text that refuses new bodies and privileges demonstrated risks is a guarantee against a global AI governance locked up by established technological powers.
The Chapel Hill unanimity is therefore less an agreement on substance than an agreement on emptiness: everyone signs because nobody loses anything.

What the principles do not do — and this is as important as what they do.
They do not define what a "demonstrated risk" is: the boundary between the demonstrated and the hypothetical is left to each party's appreciation, which blunts the clause.
They provide for no follow-up mechanism: no committee, no report, no peer review — once signed, the principles live their lives with no guardian.
They address neither export controls, nor technology transfers, nor competition for compute: the entire 2026 "technology war" chapter is out of scope.
They say nothing about open-source models, watermarking, or transparency obligations: the European Article 50 and the American mandatory benchmark coexist with the principles without being mentioned in them.
In short, the Carolina Principles are a text of philosophical orientation, not an instrument of governance: they say in what spirit to regulate, not how.

The presence of Huang (Nvidia), Musk, and Altman in Chapel Hill deserves a second analytical look.
Three hypotheses, not mutually exclusive, on their role.
The sponsorship hypothesis: their presence vouches for the principles in the eyes of other delegations — if the heads of global industry nod along, the text gains credibility.
The surveillance hypothesis: the industrialists want to see up close what states promise each other, to anticipate coming constraints — it is regulatory intelligence in real time.
The message hypothesis: by aligning the compute supplier (Huang), the most unpredictable entrepreneur (Musk), and the labs' figurehead (Altman) behind "demonstrated risks, no new bodies" principles, America shows that its industry and its state speak with one voice against overregulation.
The verified facts attest the presence, not the role: these readings must therefore be presented as interpretations, not certainties.

The December summit in Doral, Florida, is the real judge of peace — and the status must be insisted upon: formal adoption by the leaders has not yet taken place.
Three outcomes are possible, to be considered as scenarios, not predictions.
The consecration scenario: the leaders formally adopt the principles, giving them the highest political seal; the text becomes the ritual reference of all AI-governance discourse, even while remaining non-binding.
The bogging-down scenario: the leaders, absorbed by other urgencies — trade, security, climate — defer adoption or bury it in a catch-all declaration; the Chapel Hill unanimity remains a fine ministerial memory.
The fracture scenario: one or more leaders refuse formal adoption, for instance because the geopolitical context has deteriorated by December (new chip dispute, failed bilateral dialogue); unanimity would shatter in broad daylight.
In all three cases, the legal scope would remain nil — the principles are non-binding by construction — but the political scope would vary enormously.

**Deep dive — the principles tested against the facts of 2026**

Let us now test the Carolina Principles against the other events of this section: what remains of the Chapel Hill unanimity when confronted with reality?
First test: the European Digital Omnibus.
The principles say "sectoral approaches" and "demonstrated rather than hypothetical risks."
Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, for its part, classifies systems by cross-cutting annexes (Annex III, Annex I) and imposes watermarking of all synthetic content — including, as a precaution, content whose risk is not yet demonstrated.
Europe signed the principles on September 2 and keeps applying its calendar on the 3rd: the signature changed nothing in its trajectory.
This is proof by example that non-binding principles coexist with binding regimes without bending them.
Second test: Trump's June 2026 decree on the mandatory benchmark.
The principles say "no new regulatory bodies" and "minimally burdensome" in spirit.
The pre-deployment benchmark, for its part, creates a state-controlled bottleneck at the exit of frontier models — a strong constraint, even without a new body.
Washington proposed the principles in September while applying its decree since June: the two coexist because the principles prohibit nothing.
Third test: the technology war — Fable/Mythos 5 suspension, 1.3 million chips in Malaysia, Manus block.
The principles say nothing about export controls, acquisitions, or chips: they float above the conflict without touching it.
Fourth test: the US–China dialogue.
There, the principles find a use: by signing the same non-binding text, Washington and Beijing give themselves a minimal common language — "demonstrated risks," "sectoral approaches" — that can serve as bilateral negotiation vocabulary.
This is perhaps the only real effectiveness of the Carolina Principles: providing the diplomats of both capitals with words they have already accepted together.

This stress test leads to a formula: the Carolina Principles are governance without government.
They state a philosophy — do not overregulate, do not create bodies, regulate by sector and on evidence — without designating anyone to enforce it.
In the history of technology governance, this is a known form: the OECD principles on AI, the Bletchley declaration, and many other texts have played this role of orientation without constraint.
Their effectiveness is not measured in compliance, but in impregnation: if, in five years, regulators worldwide spontaneously reason in terms of "demonstrated risks" and "sectoral approaches," the principles will have won — without ever having obliged anyone.
It is a slow, invisible victory, perfectly suited to a world where no power accepts anymore submitting to a supranational authority on so strategic a subject.
The Chapel Hill unanimity is therefore not a disguised failure: it is a success of another order — that of the smallest common denominator which, precisely because it is small, has a chance of becoming universal.

The question of the December summit in Doral, Florida remains — and it must be posed with the caution the status of the facts imposes: formal adoption by the leaders has not yet taken place.
Doral is not a neutral place: it is Florida, the American president's state, a choice that says this summit will be American in its staging as Chapel Hill was in its content.
If the leaders formally adopt the principles, Washington will be able to claim a complete diplomatic cycle: American proposal in September, ministerial unanimity, leaders' consecration in December — a clean sweep.
If adoption fails or is deferred, months of diplomatic capital evaporate, and it will be demonstrated that even the smallest common denominator does not survive at heads-of-state level when technological rivalry exacerbates.
Between these two outcomes lies the third way of summits: a declaration that "welcomes" the principles without "adopting" them — diplomatic vocabulary is full of nuances for burying without offending.
Whatever the outcome, the principles will already have fulfilled their September-to-December 2026 function: giving the world the illusion — or the reality, history will decide — of a common AI governance.

**In one sentence**

The Carolina Principles are the most signed and least binding text of 2026: twenty nations agreeing to say that one must regulate lightly, by sector, on evidence — and free to do exactly the opposite the next day.
This is little, and it is all multilateralism could produce on AI in 2026.
The rest — calendars, bans, benchmarks, blocks — was played elsewhere: in Brussels, Sacramento, Austin, Washington, New York, and Beijing.
