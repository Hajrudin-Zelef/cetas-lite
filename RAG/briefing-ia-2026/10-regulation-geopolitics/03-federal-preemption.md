# Federal preemption: the blocked path

<a id="s10-4"></a>
### 10.3 Federal preemption: the blocked path

Faced with the state patchwork, the federal executive attempted two offensives in 2025–2026: one judicial, one regulatory.
Both failed to produce effective preemption, and Congress finished burying the third, legislative path with a vote of rare brutality.
The result is clear: the federal path is blocked, and the state patchwork is set to endure.

First offensive: the December 2025 executive order, which creates an AI Litigation Task Force within the Department of Justice.
Its mission is explicit: to challenge in court state laws deemed "overly restrictive" on AI.
This is a strategy of preemption by litigation rather than by statute: instead of having Congress pass a text that would override state legislation, the executive asks its own lawyers to go get them annulled one by one before the courts.
The logic is understandable — it avoids Congress, where any AI bill bogs down — but it is slow, costly, and uncertain by construction.
Each challenge is a lawsuit, each lawsuit is fought state by state, and each decision can be appealed.
Moreover, the notion of an "overly restrictive" law has no settled legal definition: it is a political standard, not a tested constitutional test.
The Task Force may hope to nibble at the most aggressive state texts, but it cannot, by nature, produce the uniformity that a federal preemption law would produce in one stroke.
This is a judicial war of attrition, not a harmonization solution.

Second offensive: executive order 14409, signed in June 2026, which promotes "minimally burdensome" regulation.
The doctrine is crystal clear: the federal executive considers that AI regulation must weigh as little as possible on American innovation and competitiveness.
In itself, an executive order preempts nothing: it gives direction to federal agencies, it cannot annul a Californian or Texan law.
But the political signal is strong: the administration displays its preference for a light federal regime, which puts it at odds with states that, for their part, legislate heavily — California with its publication obligations, Colorado with its consequential decisions.
EO 14409 is therefore less a legal instrument than a manifesto: it tells states "do not over-regulate," without giving them an enforceable order.
Its main practical effect is to politically arm the DOJ Task Force — "overly restrictive" is now read against the "minimally burdensome" yardstick — and to give industry an argument in its own litigation against state laws.

Third path, the legislative one: it died in the Senate, killed by 99 votes to 1.
A ten-year federal preemption measure — ten years during which states would have been barred from legislating on AI — was rejected by a 99-to-1 vote.
This score deserves attention: 99 to 1 is the near-unanimity of the Senate, all stripes combined, against the very idea of preemption.
This is not a partisan split — a partisan split would give 55–45 or 60–40.
It is an institutional consensus: senators, Democrats and Republicans alike, refuse to strip their states of a subject that their voters and local legislatures have already taken up.
The reasons for this consensus are multiple and reinforcing: states that have already legislated (California, Texas, New York, Colorado) do not want to see their texts erased; states that have not yet legislated do not want to be forbidden from doing so for ten years; and nobody in the Senate wants to bear the political responsibility for a ten-year federal regulatory vacuum if no substitute federal law is ready — which is the case.
The 99-1 is therefore less a vote on AI than a vote on federalism: the Senate said that AI would remain, for now, a shared competence where the states keep the upper hand.

The overall analysis is unambiguous: the three federal paths are blocked.
The judicial path (the DOJ Task Force) is too slow and too uncertain to harmonize anything in the short or medium term.
The regulatory path (EO 14409 "minimally burdensome") is a signal with no preemptive force.
The legislative path (ten-year preemption) is politically dead, buried by 99 senators out of 100.
There is therefore, on the foreseeable horizon, no federal mechanism capable of replacing the state patchwork with a unified regime.
Companies must draw the operational consequence: AI compliance in the United States will be played state by state, text by text, for years to come.
California, Texas, New York, Colorado — and tomorrow other states that will legislate in turn, emboldened by the failure of preemption — form a regulatory archipelago where each island has its rules, its definitions, and its sanctions.
The cost of American compliance is no longer the cost of one law, it is the cost of permanent mapping.

One must also measure what this federal failure means for the international position of the United States.
While Europe applies a single calendar — even re-phased — to twenty-seven member states, the United States presents the world with four regimes, soon more, with no federal pilot.
In international negotiations — G20, bilateral dialogue with China — Washington speaks with one voice on principles, but cannot guarantee any internal harmonization.
This is a structural weakness in any negotiation where the counterpart expects enforceable commitments: the "Carolina Principles" may be unanimously approved at the G20, their translation into American domestic law will remain a federalist headache.
The Senate's 99-1 is therefore not only a domestic political event: it is a parameter of American technology diplomacy for the years to come.

**Deep dive — anatomy of a triple federal failure**

Let us retake the federal sequence in chronological order, because the chain of events is itself a diagnosis.
In December 2025, the executive creates the AI Litigation Task Force within the DOJ, tasked with challenging state laws deemed "overly restrictive."
In June 2026, executive order 14409 proclaims the "minimally burdensome" doctrine: federal AI regulation must be minimally constraining.
Then the Senate buries the ten-year preemption measure by 99 votes to 1.
Three acts, three failures of different natures: failure by slowness (the Task Force), failure by legal impotence (EO 14409 preempts nothing), failure by democratic refusal (the 99-1).

The AI Litigation Task Force deserves attention on its method, because it reveals the structural limits of preemption by litigation.
Challenging a state law before federal courts first requires a basis: federal supremacy, the commerce clause, or a conflict with an existing federal norm.
But there is no federal AI law with which state laws would frontally conflict: the Task Force will therefore have to build, case by case, legal theories — for example that a given Californian provision burdens interstate commerce, or that a given Texan prohibition is preempted by a sectoral federal text.
Each theory is contestable, each lawsuit lasts years, and meanwhile the challenged laws continue to apply — unless judicially suspended, which is never a given.
Moreover, the Task Force is the instrument of one administration: a change of majority or priorities can dissolve it or leave it dormant, while state laws survive alternations of power.
It is therefore a double-edged weapon for the executive itself: by attacking state laws in court, it implicitly acknowledges it cannot erase them by statute — an admission of legislative weakness.
And each judicial defeat of the Task Force would conversely consolidate the legitimacy of state texts, giving them the seal of court validation.

EO 14409, for its part, is a text of doctrine more than of law.
"Minimally burdensome" is not a tested legal standard, it is a regulatory-policy slogan: it tells federal agencies to weigh every new rule against its cost to innovation.
Its most concrete effect is internal to the administration: it restrains the regulatory ambitions of agencies that might have wanted to frame AI on their own initiative.
Its external effect is purely rhetorical: it gives nobody the power to annul SB 53 or TRAIGA.
But rhetoric counts in American politics: EO 14409 provides industry litigants attacking state laws with an authority argument — "the executive itself deems these regulations excessive" — and it provides the DOJ Task Force with its interpretive compass for "overly restrictive."
It is thus a force multiplier of the other two paths, not an autonomous path.

The Senate's 99-1 is the most decisive of the three events, and its political mechanics must be analyzed.
A ten-year preemption measure is ten years of prohibition on states legislating on AI: it is a proposal of considerable institutional audacity, which would have frozen state AI law until around 2036.
That it was rejected 99 to 1 means it did not merely lose: it was judged indefensible by nearly the entire senatorial political class.
Three forces explain this consensus.
First force: the pioneer states — California, Texas, New York, Colorado — have powerful senatorial delegations that will never accept the erasure of their own laws.
Second force: states that have not yet legislated see in it an amputation of their future sovereignty, with no credible counterpart — since no substitute federal law is on the table.
Third force: the political risk of the vacuum.
Voting ten years of regulatory freeze with no replacement federal regime means exposing oneself to being held responsible for the first major AI scandal occurring during the freeze period.
No senator wants to bear that risk: the 99-1 is also a vote of self-protection.

Future scenarios — to be read as prospective analysis, not as facts — follow from this triple failure.
Most likely scenario: the patchwork thickens.
Emboldened by the failure of preemption, other states will legislate, each in its philosophy, and the map of American compliance will soon count six, eight, ten regimes.
Intermediate scenario: the DOJ Task Force wins a few targeted judicial victories, trimming the most aggressive provisions of state laws without ever toppling them entirely — nibbling, not a clean slate.
Least likely scenario in the short term: a compromise federal law emerges, no longer trying to preempt but to harmonize — for instance by setting common definitions (what is a critical incident?) while letting states legislate.
This scenario would assume a consensus that does not exist today, as the 99-1 showed.
In all cases, the foreseeable horizon is that of an America with multiple regulatory speeds, where compliance is negotiated state by state.

**Deep dive — American federalism as regulatory destiny**

The 99-1 is not a fluke: it is the expression of a deep structure of the American system, which must be understood to measure the durability of the patchwork.
In the United States, policing economic activities — and protecting consumers, workers, and citizens — has historically been the states' business.
California invented modern privacy regulation with the CCPA before Washington moved; Texas and Florida have their own content-moderation regimes; Colorado legislates on algorithms as it once legislated on consumer credit.
AI does not escape this grammar: each state projects its political culture onto it — technocratic and transparent California, criminal and behavioral Texas, financial and prudential New York, consumerist and social Colorado.
Asking the Senate to freeze all of this for ten years was asking each senator to renounce his own state's legislative tradition: the 99-1 says nobody was ready to do so.

Compare with Europe, for the contrast is instructive.
The European Union produces a single regulation — 2026/1744 — applicable in twenty-seven member states on a single calendar, even re-phased.
The United States produces four laws — soon more — on four calendars and four philosophies, with no harmonization mechanism.
Europe chose unity in time (a tiered calendar, but the same for all); America chose plurality in space (each state its own regime).
Both systems have their pathologies: Europe risks the slowness and rigidity of a calendar negotiated by twenty-seven; America risks the illegibility of an archipelago where each island invents its definitions.
But in 2026, it is Europe that offers companies the best predictability — four known dates — while America offers the greatest uncertainty — four known regimes and an unknown number to come.

The political economy of the patchwork deserves a final development, because it explains why the system is stable.
First involuntary beneficiary: the large laboratories and large platforms.
Only they have the armies of lawyers and lobbyists capable of tracking four regimes, mapping conflicts of laws, and negotiating with four capitals.
The patchwork is therefore, paradoxically, a barrier to entry that protects incumbents against challengers — exactly as fragmented regulatory regimes have shown in other sectors.
Second beneficiary: law firms and compliance consultants, for whom each new state law is a new market.
Third actor, the most interesting: the states themselves, which have discovered in AI a terrain of political distinction.
Legislating on AI is existing nationally: a governor who gets a frontier-models law passed poses as protector of his citizens and actor of modernity.
As long as this political benefit exists, states will keep legislating — and as long as they legislate, the Senate will refuse preemption.
It is a self-sustaining equilibrium: the patchwork produces the incentives that perpetuate it.
