# The US debate: moratorium, mandatory benchmark and legislative proposals

<a id="s10-8"></a>
### 10.7 The American debate: moratorium, mandatory benchmark, and legislative proposals

While states legislate and the executive maneuvers, a third front opened in 2026: that of ideas.
A resounding essay, a presidential decree, and two legislative proposals at opposite ends of the spectrum installed in the American public debate a question that had never figured there so bluntly: should the race to the frontier be slowed — or even banned?

On September 12, 2026, Dario Amodei publishes an essay titled "We Must Pace the Frontier."
The title is a program: one must "pace," "cadence" the frontier — in other words, voluntarily slow the race for the most advanced models.
The essay proposes a three-step plan — whose precise content the verified facts of the dossier do not detail, and which must therefore not be reconstructed by speculation.
What the facts say, and this is already significant, is the reception: Sam Altman and Elon Musk publicly approve the essay.
That the head of OpenAI and the head of xAI — two direct competitors of Anthropic, Amodei's laboratory — publicly salute a call to slow the frontier is a political event in itself.
Three readings are possible, and they are not mutually exclusive.
First reading, sincerity: the three men would share a genuine concern about the current pace and would deem a collective cadence preferable to a frenzied race.
Second reading, strategy: approving a call to slow down costs nothing as long as no constraint applies, and it positions each as "responsible" in the eyes of regulators and the public.
Third reading, the signal addressed to public authorities: by publicly agreeing on the need to "pace" the frontier, the three industry leaders send Congress and the White House the message that self-regulation has limits and that a framework — even a binding one — would be preferable to competitive chaos.
Whatever the reading, the September 12 essay marks the moment when the idea of a coordinated slowdown passed from the status of industrial taboo to that of a legitimate proposal carried by the interested parties themselves.

In June 2026, Donald Trump signed a decree imposing a mandatory benchmark before the release of frontier models.
The measure has an implacable logic: no frontier model can be deployed without first passing a battery of evaluations — a benchmark — whose results condition the authorization to release.
This is the regulatory transposition of the "pacing" idea: one does not slow research, one locks the exit door.
The mandatory benchmark creates an institutional bottleneck between training and deployment, and this bottleneck is controlled — directly or indirectly — by the state.
The implications are considerable.
First, it gives the executive systematic visibility into frontier-model capabilities before their diffusion: no more surprise releases, each model is X-rayed before reaching the public.
Second, it creates a de facto standard: the content of the benchmark — what is measured, the thresholds, the protocols — becomes the instrument by which the state defines what is deployable and what is not.
Finally, it raises the question of international asymmetry: American laboratories undergo pre-deployment control that their Chinese competitors do not undergo — or undergo in other forms —, which can be read as a competitive handicap or a badge of seriousness, depending on the camp.
The June 2026 decree is thus the first materialization, by the American state, of the idea that the frontier must be "paced" — a few months before Amodei's essay popularized the expression.

At the radical end of the spectrum, the "Ban Artificial Superintelligence" bill, carried by Bernie Sanders and Greg Casar, was introduced on September 3–4, 2026.
Its content is maximalist: a permanent ban on the development of artificial superintelligence, coupled with the creation of a dedicated federal agency.
The status of this text must be signaled with the utmost clarity, for everything else follows from it: there is no formal bill number for this proposal, and its chances of adoption are near zero.
This is therefore not a law in the making, it is a political gesture — but a political gesture that counts.
First, because it emanates from leading figures: Sanders, the iconic senator of the American left, and Casar, a representative of the new progressive generation.
Second, because its timing — September 3–4 — places it nine days before Amodei's September 12 essay: the radical proposal precedes the moderate call for slowdown, and both belong to the same political moment.
Finally, because the content — permanent ban plus federal agency — draws the maximalist horizon of the debate: even if the text never passes, it shifts the Overton window.
Henceforth, between "doing nothing" and "banning forever," the entire intermediate space — temporary moratoriums, mandatory benchmarks, deployment licenses — becomes debatable, hence conceivable.
This is the classic function of extreme proposals in a legislative system: they do not aim at adoption, they aim at making intermediate measures reasonable by contrast.
Note also the tension with the Carolina Principles approved nine days earlier in Chapel Hill: the principles say "regulate demonstrated rather than hypothetical risks," while the Sanders–Casar proposal wants to ban a superintelligence which, by definition, does not yet exist — the regulation of the hypothetical pushed to its paroxysm.
Both positions coexist in the same month of September 2026, carried by the same country: this is patchwork America down to its ideas.

At the other end of the legislative spectrum — the constructive, bipartisan pole — stands the Frontier Safety Act.
The verified facts describe it as bipartisan, and its adoption as uncertain.
Each of these two qualifiers must be dwelled upon.
"Bipartisan": in the 2026 Congress, where the Senate's 99-1 on preemption showed that AI could unite both parties in refusal, a bipartisan text on frontier-model safety is the only legislative form with a theoretical chance of succeeding.
"Adoption uncertain": but this theoretical chance does not convert into probability, and the dossier requires saying so — the Frontier Safety Act may just as well die in committee as become the first federal law on frontier models.
The uncertainty is not an information defect of the dossier, it is the real state of the text as of the verified facts.
The contrast with the Sanders–Casar proposal is instructive: on one side a maximalist text with no bill number and no legislative future, on the other a bipartisan text with an undecidable future.
Between the two, Trump's June 2026 decree — the mandatory benchmark — already serves as applicable law, without having gone through Congress.
This is the American configuration of 2026 summarized in three objects: the executive acts by decree, the extremes propose the impossible, and the legislative center hesitates.

Putting these four events in perspective — the June decree, the September 3–4 Sanders–Casar proposal, the September 12 Amodei essay, the Frontier Safety Act on hold — reveals an American debate structured in three layers.
The executive layer: the mandatory benchmark, already in force, which "paces" the frontier through pre-deployment control.
The intellectual and industrial layer: Amodei's essay and the public approval of Altman and Musk, which legitimize the idea of a coordinated slowdown.
The legislative layer: torn between permanent ban (with no future) and bipartisan safety (with an uncertain future), with the memory of the 99-1 recalling that Congress prefers, for now, to let the states legislate.
None of these layers dominates the others, and it is this indeterminacy that makes the climate of 2026: everyone agrees that the frontier poses a pacing problem, nobody agrees on the instrument — decree, self-regulation, ban law, or safety law.
The June 2026 mandatory benchmark may remain as the only effective measure of this sequence; or the Frontier Safety Act will end up giving it a legislative framework; or both will be swept away by a major incident that reopens the whole debate.
The verified facts do not allow deciding, and this must be said: the outcome of the American debate on "pacing" the frontier is, at this stage, undecidable.

In closing this section, one line of force emerges from the year 2026.
Europe chose to re-phase without deregulating: immediate transparency, deferred compliance.
The American states chose to legislate each in their philosophy: compute thresholds, intent, reporting, consequential decisions.
The American federal executive failed to preempt and fell back on decree and litigation.
The G20 produced unanimous, non-binding principles whose formal adoption is still to come at Doral.
The United States and China opened an unprecedented bilateral dialogue while continuing their war of chips, models, and acquisitions.
And the debate over the very pace of the frontier — slowing, benchmarking, banning — settled at the heart of American politics.
2026 is not the year the world regulated AI: it is the year the world admitted it would have to regulate it fast, hard, and without agreeing on the method.

**Deep dive — four objects, four statuses, one month of September**

September 2026 concentrates the bulk of the American debate on the pace of the frontier: let us retake the chronology, for the order of events illuminates their meaning.

- 01–02/09/2026: G20 Innovation Ministerial in Chapel Hill — the 20 nations approve the Carolina Principles ("demonstrated rather than hypothetical risks").
- 03–04/09/2026: "Ban Artificial Superintelligence" proposal (Sanders/Casar) — permanent ban + federal agency (no formal bill number, near-zero chances of adoption).
- 05/09/2026: Reuters reveals the US–China dialogue on AI.
- 12/09/2026: Amodei's essay "We Must Pace the Frontier" (3-step plan, public approval from Altman and Musk).
- 20/09/2026: Bessent–He Lifeng meeting in New York (~8 hours).
- 24/09/2026: planned Trump–Xi meeting in Washington.

Nine days separate the approval of the Carolina Principles (September 2) from Amodei's essay (September 12), and it is in this interval that the Sanders–Casar proposal inserts itself (September 3–4).
The sequence is dizzying: on September 2, twenty nations agree to regulate only demonstrated risks; on September 3, two American elected officials propose banning forever a superintelligence that does not exist — the hypothetical risk par excellence; on September 12, three lab bosses call for "pacing" the frontier.
The three positions are logically incompatible — one cannot at once regulate only the demonstrated, ban the hypothetical, and slow the present — and yet they coexist in the same month, in the same country, sometimes in the same circles.
This is because the American debate of 2026 is not a debate seeking coherence: it is a debate exploring, in parallel, all options — from multilateral abstention to total ban — because nobody knows which will be necessary.

Let us now distinguish the four legal objects of the debate, for confusing them would be the first reading error.
First object: Trump's June 2026 decree (mandatory benchmark before frontier-model release).
Status: applicable law, emanating from the executive, without passage through Congress.
It is the only one of the four objects that produces immediate legal effects: the laboratories concerned must comply or face executive sanctions.
Second object: Amodei's September 12 essay.
Status: opinion — a text of ideas, with no legal value, but with strong political value by virtue of its author and its public approvers (Altman, Musk).
Its power is that of the agenda: it makes the coordinated slowdown sayable — and therefore conceivable.
Third object: the September 3–4 "Ban Artificial Superintelligence" proposal.
Status: proposal with no formal bill number, near-zero chances of adoption — this must be restated clearly, for it is the safeguard against any overinterpretation.
Its power is that of contrast: it makes intermediate measures — temporary moratoriums, licenses, benchmarks — reasonable by comparison.
Fourth object: the Frontier Safety Act.
Status: bipartisan proposal, uncertain adoption — neither promised nor buried.
Its power is that of the possible: it is the only credible legislative path toward a federal frontier-model safety framework, but "credible" does not mean "likely."

The mandatory benchmark deserves its own deeper look, for it is the most concrete measure of the entire sequence.
A pre-deployment benchmark is a rite of passage: the model is released only if it passes.
Everything therefore depends on the content of the examination — and the verified facts of the dossier do not detail that content, which must be flagged as a major blind spot.
Three questions, unanswered in the dossier, will determine the decree's real effect.
First question: who writes the benchmark? A federal agency, an industry consortium, an independent body?
Depending on the answer, the benchmark will be an instrument of the state, a self-regulation exercise dressed as constraint, or a neutral technical standard.
Second question: what is measured? Dangerous capabilities (weapons, cyberattacks, manipulation), biases, robustness, all at once?
The perimeter of measurement defines the perimeter of the de facto ban: what is not measured is not blocked.
Third question: what happens in case of failure? Outright ban, conditional deployment, or mere publication of results?
A benchmark whose failure does not prevent release is only a transparency exercise; a benchmark whose failure blocks release is a deployment permit.
The June 2026 decree thus opens a project at least as important as the decree itself: the benchmark factory.
And this project will play out in the shadows — technical committees, consultations, arbitrations — while the spotlights stay trained on the great public debates.

The public approval of Altman and Musk for Amodei's essay also deserves a second level of reading.
Recall the respective positions: Amodei heads Anthropic, Altman heads OpenAI, Musk heads xAI — three direct competitors at the frontier.
When three competitors publicly agree that one must slow down, three competing interpretations offer themselves to the observer, and the verified facts do not allow deciding between them.
Cooperative interpretation: the three men sincerely share the concern and prefer a collective cadence to a race whose outcome nobody controls.
Strategic interpretation: each approves because approval is free — no binding mechanism is on the table — and because it buys a reputation for responsibility at little cost.
Regulatory interpretation: by displaying an industry consensus for slowdown, the three leaders invite the state to frame the race — which, paradoxically, protects incumbents against new entrants, since a regime of benchmarks and licenses is always easier for large labs to bear than for challengers.
These three interpretations are not mutually exclusive: a single approving tweet can be sincere, strategic, and self-interested at once.
What matters is the effect: after September 12, 2026, no serious actor in the American debate can any longer treat the idea of a coordinated slowdown as a Cassandra's whim — it has the public support of the three heaviest names in the industry.

The Sanders–Casar proposal, finally, must be read for what it is: an ideological marker, not a bill.
"Permanent ban": the word "permanent" is the most radical in the legislative vocabulary — this is not about suspending until things are clearer, but about closing the door and throwing away the key.
"Federal agency": creating a dedicated agency assumes the ban will be monitored, controlled, sanctioned — a whole state apparatus to be built around refusal.
But with no formal bill number and near-zero chances of adoption, the edifice remains a sketch.
Its real function is twofold.
Signaling function: it tells the progressive electorate that its elected officials grasp the measure of existential risk and do not settle for half-measures.
Boundary function: it sets the end of the spectrum, which makes everything else — including Trump's mandatory benchmark, already radical in principle — moderate by comparison.
This is the paradox of extreme proposals: by failing to become laws, they succeed in becoming yardsticks.
And the September 2026 Sanders–Casar yardstick will long serve to measure — and justify — the intermediate measures to come.

In summary of the American debate: the executive acted (June 2026 benchmark), industry spoke (September 12 essay, Altman and Musk approvals), the extremes proposed the impossible (September 3–4, no bill, no future), and the legislative center awaits its hour (Frontier Safety Act, bipartisan, uncertain adoption).
No layer summarizes the others, none cancels them: it is a four-voice debate that has not yet found its final chord.
