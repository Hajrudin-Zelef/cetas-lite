# Personal agents: positioning, business models, maturity

<a id="s11-4"></a>
### 11.3 Comparative analysis: positioning, business models, maturity

**Positioning.** The seven offerings split across three
axes. Axis 1, the field of action: Muse and Pi Journeys
target personal life (transactional for one, relational
for the other); Scout, Cowork and ChatGPT Work target
work (governed for Scout, cloud infrastructure for
Cowork, long sessions for Work); Siri AI targets personal
mobile via cross-app; Solara targets ambient hardware.
Axis 2, the relationship with the user: Pi Journeys and
Scout (nameable identity) bet on the durable
relationship; the others bet on execution capability.
Axis 3, architecture: on-device and privacy (Siri AI),
usage-billed cloud (Cowork), isolated VM (Muse), hardware
platform (Solara). No actor covers the whole spectrum:
2026 is the year of experimental fragmentation, not yet
of consolidation.

**Business models.** Three pricing grammars coexist. The
tiered flat-rate subscription (Muse: free / $20 / $100
per month) reprises the premium chatbot model.
Usage-based billing (Cowork: Copilot Credits at $0.01)
aligns price with actually consumed compute — logical for
agents whose marginal cost varies widely with task
duration and complexity. The third model is implicit: the
agent as a hardware or ecosystem differentiator (Siri AI
sells iPhone 15 Pro+, Solara sells a platform to
manufacturers, Scout is backed by the GitHub Copilot
subscription and Microsoft 365). The coexistence of these
models in 2026 suggests the market has not yet decided
between "the agent as a service" and "the agent as a
platform feature".

**Maturity.** The spectrum runs from the globally
generally-available product (Cowork, 16/06) to hardware
concepts expected late 2026/2027 (Solara), via
waitlisted betas (Siri AI, 14/09), recent consumer
launches (Muse, 08/09), experimental programs (Scout and
its Frontier program) and offerings differentiated by
relationship (Pi Journeys). Notable fact: it is the most
"infrastructure" offering — Cowork — that shows the
strongest adoption in the history of the Frontier
program, not one of the most publicized consumer
offerings. This suggests that real agent traction in 2026
comes first from intensive professional use, while the
general public remains in a discovery phase — confirmed
by Muse's geographic and age restrictions (US, 18+) and
the Siri AI waitlist.

**Access restrictions as risk management.** An
underappreciated common thread of the 2026 launches:
almost no agent arrived with fully open access. Muse is
reserved for the United States and 18-and-over users;
Siri AI is in waitlist beta and requires an iPhone 15 Pro
or newer; Scout requires a GitHub Copilot subscription.
These restrictions are not only marketing (the waitlist
creates desire) or technical (on-device requires recent
hardware): they are a form of risk management. An agent
that can buy, negotiate, act inside the user's apps
presents a failure surface beyond all comparison with a
chatbot: each launch is therefore calibrated —
geography, age, hardware, subscription — to limit
exposure while safeguards mature. Generalization will
come, but 2026 is the year of deployments under glass.

**A compressed calendar.** Another striking fact: nearly
all launches fit into three and a half months, from 2
June (Scout) to 16 September (Siri AI on 14/09, Muse on
08/09). This compression is no calendar coincidence: it
reflects a race in which each player wanted to occupy
ground before year end — Microsoft from June with two
offerings, OpenAI in July, Inflection in late July, Meta
in early September, Apple in mid-September. For the
dossier's reader, this means the positions described here
are a moving snapshot: at this pace, the first-half-2027
landscape could already differ substantially, through
consolidation, abandonment or unexpected breakthrough.
