# April 2026: Anthropic's return and the pact of the century

<a id="s01-18"></a>
## 7. April 2026: Anthropic's return and the pact of the century

<a id="s01-19"></a>
### 7.1. Claude Opus 4.7 temporarily retakes the top of the rankings

On April 16, 2026, Anthropic launched Claude Opus 4.7, which temporarily retook the
leadership of the model rankings — a formulation worth pausing over, for the word
"temporarily" alone tells of the ferocity of 2026's competition. At that time of the
year, the "best model" crown changed heads at the pace of releases: Opus 4.7 in
April, then Opus 4.8 in May with 88.6% on SWE-bench Verified, then the Fable 5s in
June, Opus 5 in July, Fable 5.1 in September — not counting Google's assaults with
Gemini 3.5, 3.6, 3.7 and 3.8 Flash, nor GPT-5.6 in July then GPT-6 Astra in September.
In this context, retaking the lead, even briefly, was already a feat testifying to
Anthropic's velocity.

This velocity is partly explained by the financial means secured in February: the
$30 billion Series G had given Anthropic the resources to chain training and
fine-tuning cycles at a pace few labs could follow. Opus 4.7 was not an isolated
accident but the first visible fruit of February's capitalization, and it announced
the sequence that would make Anthropic the year's most prolific lab in major
releases. April's lesson is therefore also financial: in 2026's model race, iteration
speed was directly proportional to pocket depth, and Anthropic had just secured the
deepest pockets in the sector after the SpaceX–xAI merger.

The temporary character of Opus 4.7's leadership finally illustrates a structural
property of the year 2026: a model's competitive advantage was measured in weeks, not
years. This compression of cycles would have profound consequences for industrial
strategies: since no model durably kept the advantage, competition shifted toward
infrastructure — compute, distribution, prices — and toward uses — agentic,
cybersecurity, science. It is precisely this shift that the deal with Amazon, signed
four days later, would enshrine: the battle would no longer be won only in model
weights, but in the gigawatts that run them.

<a id="s01-20"></a>
### 7.2. The Anthropic–Amazon deal: over $100 billion over ten years

On April 20 and 21, 2026, Anthropic and Amazon announced a deal of unprecedented
scale: Anthropic committed to spending over $100 billion on AWS over ten years,
backed by 5 GW of compute capacity, with a ramp-up of Trainium chips — from
Trainium2 to Trainium4 — and Graviton, and AWS becoming the lab's primary cloud. In
return, Amazon invested $5 billion immediately, with up to $20 billion more
conditional, after about $8 billion already invested — taking Amazon's total exposure
to an unprecedented level. The verified facts describe the operation as the largest
cloud forward-commitment ever signed by a frontier lab, and the expression matches
the event: $100 billion over ten years is the equivalent of a small Marshall Plan
devoted solely to training and inference of Claude models.

The structure of the deal deserves detailed analysis, for it invents a new economic
model in the AI industry. On one side, a spending commitment — the forward-commitment
— which gives AWS the visibility needed to build dedicated data centers and
Anthropic guaranteed rates and capacity over a decade; on the other, a capital
investment — $5 billion immediate plus $20 billion conditional — which aligns
Amazon's interests with Anthropic's valuation. This dual mechanism resolves the
fundamental dilemma of 2026's frontier labs: they need quantities of compute that
exceed their balance sheets, and hyperscalers need guaranteed demand to justify
colossal infrastructure investments. The April deal is the meeting of these two
needs, formalized in figures.

The Trainium2 → Trainium4 ramp-up, coupled with Graviton processors, reveals the
technological dimension of the pact: Anthropic was not merely renting generic
compute, it was mooring itself to Amazon's silicon roadmap, betting that AWS's
in-house chips — cheaper than Nvidia GPUs — would give it a decisive cost advantage
over ten years. This bet made full sense in a year when inference cost became the
nerve of the commercial war: with models offered at a few dollars per million tokens
— Opus 4.8 at $5/$25 in May, Sonnet 5 at $2/$10 in June — every cent saved on compute
translated into margin or market share. The April deal was therefore also a delayed
pricing weapon, whose effects would be felt throughout the year.

<a id="s01-21"></a>
### 7.3. Anatomy of a forward-commitment: 5 GW, Trainium, Graviton

The figure of 5 GW of compute capacity backing the deal deserves to have its order
of magnitude measured: five gigawatts is the electrical power of about five nuclear
reactors, dedicated solely to training and inference of one lab's models. This scale,
unthinkable a few years earlier, had become in 2026 the unit of account for AI
ambitions: the July AMD–Anthropic deal would cover up to 2 GW of MI450-series, and
the OpenAI–AMD deal 6 GW with warrants up to 10% of AMD's capital. April 2026, with
its 5 GW, thus set the first milestone of a year in which contracted electrical power
would become an indicator as scrutinized as valuations.

The Trainium2 → Trainium4 trajectory inscribed in the deal tells another story: that
of the diversification of training silicon against Nvidia's hegemony. In April,
betting on Trainium meant betting that Amazon would converge its in-house chips
toward performance competitive with Nvidia GPUs for Anthropic's workloads — an
audacious bet when one knows that Nvidia, in September, would acquire Hugging Face
for nearly $12 billion to lock in the software ecosystem. The ramp-up across three
chip generations, spread over ten years, gave Amazon the time to keep that promise
and Anthropic a gradual exit door if it did not. It was, in short, a bet hedged on
both sides, typical of very-long-term infrastructure contracts.

The addition of Graviton processors — AWS's in-house ARM CPUs — to the arrangement
completes the picture of a deep vertical integration: not only AI acceleration with
Trainium, but also general compute with Graviton, storage, networking — the whole
stack became AWS-native for Anthropic. Making AWS its "primary cloud" meant for
Anthropic giving up part of its multi-cloud flexibility in exchange for deep
optimization and preferential rates — an arbitration only $100 billion volumes could
justify. This concentration, in return, made Anthropic AWS's reference customer for
generative AI, technological and commercial showcase at once. The April deal thus
created an assumed mutual dependence, sealed for ten years.

<a id="s01-22"></a>
### 7.4. What the deal changes for the cloud map

The April 20 and 21 deal redrew the map of cloud-AI alliances for the rest of the
year, establishing a precedent that competitors would hasten to follow or counter.
For AWS, it was the war prize: securing the moment's most dynamic lab as a captive
customer for ten years, with a quantified commitment that alone justified massive
infrastructure investments. For Google Cloud, which had invested up to $40 billion in
Anthropic in February, the April deal was a relative setback: February's money had
not sufficed to make Google the primary cloud, and Anthropic chose AWS for its
compute. For Microsoft, the stake became replicating with its own partners — which it
would do in July with Mistral and its Vera Rubin GPUs in European data centers.

The February → April sequence reveals the overall logic: in February, hyperscalers
took financial stakes in the labs; in April, these stakes converted into exclusive
or priority compute contracts. Google and its $40 billion, Amazon and its initial
$25 billion of February then its $5 + $20 billion of April: capital money served as
the entry ticket to compute money, far more considerable over ten years. This
mechanism would repeat in July with AMD — up to $5 billion of investment for up to 2
GW of MI450 capacity — and with OpenAI — warrants up to 10% of AMD for 6 GW. April
had invented the model, July would generalize it to silicon itself.

Finally, the deal raises the question of concentration: as labs moor themselves to a
primary cloud for ten years and tens of billions, the AI market structures itself
into vertically integrated blocs — Anthropic–AWS, OpenAI–Microsoft with its AMD
chips in reinforcement, xAI–SpaceX in assumed autarky, Google in historic vertical
integration. This consolidation into blocs, begun in April, would fuel September's
antitrust debates, with the complaint filed around September 20 in the wake of the
security coordination between OpenAI, Anthropic and Google confirmed on the 15th. The
April pact of the century carried in germ the autumn's litigations: when vertical
integration becomes the norm, the competition question inevitably arises.
