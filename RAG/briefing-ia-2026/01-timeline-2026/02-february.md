# February 2026: the capital deluge

<a id="s01-9"></a>
## 5. February 2026: the capital deluge

<a id="s01-10"></a>
### 5.1. The SpaceX–xAI merger: $1.25 trillion

The event that defines February 2026, and perhaps the entire year, is the announced
merger on February 2 between SpaceX and xAI, carried out entirely in stock for an
amount of $1.25 trillion. The operation brings under one roof the world's largest
private space company and Elon Musk's AI lab, creating an integrated whole in which
rockets, Starlink satellites, data centers and AI models — the Grok family — are
combined in a single capital structure. The choice of all-stock payment, with no cash
component, signals the parties' confidence in the valuation of the new entity: at
$1.25 trillion, the transaction shatters all precedents in the technology sector and
durably installs AI at the top of the hierarchy of global assets.

The implications of this merger reach far beyond the two companies concerned, for it
redefines what an "AI lab" is in 2026: no longer a mere software company training
models in rented clouds, but a vertically integrated conglomerate mastering energy,
space, networks and compute. This logic of vertical integration illuminates, by
contrast, the strategies of the competitors that would follow: Anthropic leaning on
Amazon and AWS for its compute with the April deal of over $100 billion over ten
years, OpenAI negotiating warrants up to 10% of AMD in July as part of a 6 GW deal,
Microsoft allying with Mistral for Vera Rubin GPUs in its European data centers. Each
sought, in its own way, to secure the year's rare resource — compute — and the
SpaceX–xAI merger gave its most radical version: depending on no one.

On the stock-market and financial front, the February 2 announcement acted as a signal
sent to the entire market: if an AI-space entity could be worth $1.25 trillion, then
the valuations of the other frontier labs were mechanically lifted. It is no
coincidence that Anthropic's Series G, ten days later, closed at a $380 billion
valuation for $30 billion raised, nor that SpaceX's IPO in June reached approx. $1.77
trillion, an absolute historic record. The February merger set the market's new
psychological ceiling, and all the year's operations would henceforth be measured
against it. It is in this sense that February was the month in which AI became, in
the eyes of capital, the central asset of the century.

<a id="s01-11"></a>
### 5.2. Anthropic, Scale AI, Mistral: the reshaping of alliances

Ten days after the SpaceX–xAI merger, on February 12, Anthropic announced its Series
G: $30 billion raised for a $380 billion valuation. The operation made Anthropic one
of the best-capitalized private actors on the planet and financed a growth trajectory
whose figures would speak for themselves: a run-rate above $47 billion in May
according to official figures, then above $65 billion in July according to Bloomberg
— a figure unconfirmed by Anthropic — and above $100 billion in September according
to the New York Times — again unconfirmed by Anthropic. This sequence of rounds and
growth illuminates the Series H of May 28, where $65 billion more would be raised at
a $965 billion valuation: February laid the financial foundations on which Anthropic
would build its year, up to the over-$100 billion deal with AWS in April.

The same month of February saw Meta invest $14.3 billion for 49% of Scale AI, with
the appointment of Alexandr Wang as Meta's Chief AI Officer and the departure of Yann
LeCun as part of the deal. The operation is triple in its meaning: financial, by the
amount; organizational, by Wang's arrival at the head of Meta's AI; symbolic, by the
departure of LeCun, a historic figure of deep learning and long the scientific
embodiment of Meta. By taking nearly half of Scale AI, the specialist in training
data, Meta secured one of the most critical resources in the AI value chain —
annotated data — at the very moment the model race entered its most capitalistic
phase. LeCun's departure, for its part, marked the end of an era when open, academic
research could coexist with industrial logic: in 2026, Meta's AI became a commercial
war machine steered by an entrepreneur, Wang, rather than by a researcher.

Meanwhile, Google invested up to $40 billion in Anthropic and Amazon up to $25
billion, tightening the hyperscalers' grip around the lab: these February investments
directly prepared the April deal by which Anthropic would commit to spending over
$100 billion on AWS over ten years, with AWS as primary cloud. The logic is crystal
clear: in February, the cloud giants bought their way into Anthropic's capital; in
April, they converted that position into a massive compute-spending commitment,
backed by 5 GW of capacity and the Trainium then Graviton chips. February was the
month of equity stakes, April that of their monetization into compute contracts — two
acts of a single strategy of mooring frontier labs to cloud infrastructures.

Finally, Mistral's acquisition of Koyeb in February, more modest in amount —
undisclosed in the verified facts — is no less significant: the French gem of
European AI bought itself a serverless deployment platform, a sign that the battle
was also moving toward the infrastructure for putting models into production. This
acquisition fits into the trajectory that would lead Mistral, in May, to transform Le
Chat into the "Vibe" enterprise agentic platform, then in July to sign a multi-billion
deal with Microsoft for Vera Rubin GPUs in European data centers, and finally in
September to raise €3 billion at a valuation above €21 billion, a European record.
February laid the first stone of a year in which Mistral would assert itself as
Europe's AI champion, between claimed sovereignty and assumed American partnerships.

<a id="s01-12"></a>
### 5.3. Beijing blocks the Manus acquisition: the first red line

The major geopolitical event of February 2026 is China's blocking of Meta's $2
billion acquisition of Manus, described in the verified facts as the first forced
dismantling. For the first time, Beijing purely and simply prohibited the sale of a
Chinese AI asset to an American acquirer, establishing a precedent whose scope would
prove considerable: Chinese AI startups, however attractive to Silicon Valley giants,
could no longer be acquired without the — henceforth refused — approval of the
Chinese authorities. The $2 billion amount, modest in light of the month's
operations, is not the point: the point is the principle, and the principle is a
rejection of the free circulation of AI assets between China and the United States.

This blocking fits into an escalation of which February constituted only the first
defensive act, before the year revealed the offensive dimension of the Chinese
strategy. In September, the joint CISA, NSA and FBI advisory — reference AA26-251A —
would accuse six Chinese labs, including DeepSeek, Moonshot, Alibaba, MiniMax,
StepFun and Z.AI, of industrial distillation of the Claude, GPT, Gemini and Grok
models since late 2024, for billions of tokens extracted via native APIs, remote
clouds, aggregators, gray-market "transfer stations" and premium subscriptions bought
in bulk. The symmetry is striking: in February, Beijing prevented Americans from
buying Chinese AI; in September, Washington accused the Chinese of siphoning American
AI. In between, American export controls would strike — 1.3 million Nvidia chips
intercepted in Malaysia in August — and diplomatic meetings would multiply, up to the
eight-hour tête-à-tête between Scott Bessent and He Lifeng in New York on September
20.

The choice of Manus as the ground for this first confrontation is no accident: Manus
had made a name for itself as a particularly capable AI agent, and its $2 billion
acquisition by Meta testified to the value Silicon Valley placed on Chinese agentic
talent and technology. By blocking the operation, Beijing signaled that agentic —
precisely the domain in which, from February to August, the share of R&D "led" by
Claude at Anthropic would rise from below 1% to 26% — was henceforth considered a
national strategic asset. This reading also illuminates, in negative, the rest of the
year: agentic would become the central battleground, from the proteins designed by
Claude in August to Fermat's theorem formalized in September, and China fully
intended to keep its champions there.

### 5.4. The weak signals: Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude-"led" R&D and the ALMA paper

Amid February's din of billions, three weak signals deserve attention, for each
announced, in its own way, a major theme of the year. The first is the arrival of
Grok 4.1 Fast in preview on Copilot Studio as early as February 19: for the first
time, an xAI model — now backed by SpaceX since February 2 — was entering
Microsoft's productivity ecosystem. This discreet presence foreshadowed Satya
Nadella's September 12 announcement of the integration of Grok models, selectable in
Word, Excel and PowerPoint for Frontier customers: in February, xAI was testing the
enterprise waters via Copilot Studio; in September, it entered through the front
door. Grok's 2026 trajectory — 4.1 Fast in February, 4.5 in July, 4.6 in August, 4.7
downgraded by Musk himself on September 21, 4.8 announced on September 13 with 2.5
trillion parameters — tells of a bumpy climb to power, but one of which February
laid the first enterprise milestone.

The second weak signal is a figure revealed seven months later: in February 2026,
the share of Anthropic's internal R&D "led" by Claude was below 1%, versus 26% in
August according to data published on September 17 and 18 — with 0% in fully
autonomous mode. This February figure is the starting point of one of the year's most
spectacular curves: in six months, Claude agents went from a marginal contribution to
over a quarter of a frontier lab's internal research. February thus marks the
"before" of 2026's agentic revolution, whose stages would follow one another: the
Irregular CTF test in May where Gemini compromised three real companies, the
proteins designed by Claude in August with 354 validated minibinders, the
formalization of Fermat's Last Theorem on September 4 in 13 million lines of Lean.
The contrast between February's below-1% and August's 26% gives the measure of the
shift: the year AI started doing research, and February is its documented point
zero.

The third signal is academic: the publication of the ALMA paper on arXiv under
reference 2602.07755, devoted to meta-learning of memory designs for agents. In
February, research on agent architectures — how to give an agent an effective memory
— was still at exploratory works like this one; in September, the question had become
industrial, with Sakana's learned orchestrators — Fugu Max and Fugu Ultra v2.0,
announced on September 11 — which route tasks to a pool of models rather than
constituting foundations themselves. Between February's paper and September's
products, the trajectory is that of an idea moving from lab to market in seven
months, at the characteristic speed of 2026. ALMA thus deserves its place in
February's narrative not for its technical content, but as a dated witness to the
state of the agentic art at the moment everything was about to accelerate.
