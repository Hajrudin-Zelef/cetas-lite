# March 2026: the Californian regulatory lever

<a id="s01-14"></a>
## 6. March 2026: the Californian regulatory lever

<a id="s01-15"></a>
### 6.1. Newsom's Executive Order N-5-26 (March 30)

The only verified fact of March 2026 is the signing by Gavin Newsom, on March 30, of
Executive Order N-5-26 on AI procurement by the State of California. At first
glance, a decree on public procurement may seem a minor administrative object in
light of the billions traded in February; it is in reality a regulatory lever of
formidable effectiveness, of which California would make its trademark throughout the
year, up to Executive Order N-9-26 of September 18 on the study of a kill switch and
embedded verification. By conditioning the purchases of the most populous and
richest American state on AI requirements, Newsom used public purchasing power as a
disguised instrument of regulation: to sell to California, one would have to comply
with its rules.

The scope of this March 30 decree goes all the more beyond mere administrative
management in that California hosts nearly all American frontier labs — OpenAI,
Anthropic, Google DeepMind for most of its teams, xAI, Meta. Regulating the state's
AI procurement is therefore, indirectly, imposing standards on companies whose
headquarters lie a few kilometers from the Capitol in Sacramento, without going
through a law voted by Congress or a federal regulation. This Californian strategy of
circumvention would be confirmed in September with the signing, on September 9, of
laws SB 813 and AB 1405, then EO N-9-26 of September 18: California methodically
built, decree after decree and law after law, its own regulatory corpus, exploiting
every available lever — public procurement in March, security studies in September.

Finally, EO N-5-26 must be placed in the 2026 federal context, marked by displayed
deregulation: the June Executive Order 14409, described as "minimally burdensome",
and the Trump decree imposing a simple mandatory benchmark before frontier model
releases sketched a light, almost permissive federal approach. Faced with this federal
restraint, California chose targeted activism: no frontal prohibition, but conditions
of access to public procurement which, by capillarity, become de facto industry
standards. March 2026 is thus the month in which the American division of regulatory
labor crystallized — Washington lightens, Sacramento demands — a division that would
structure all the year's debates.

<a id="s01-16"></a>
### 6.2. Why procurement is a regulatory lever

To understand the importance of EO N-5-26, one must grasp the particular mechanics
of procurement as an instrument of regulation: when a state the size of California —
whose GDP exceeds that of most countries — sets conditions on its AI purchases, it
creates a market standard that suppliers have an interest in adopting across their
entire activity rather than maintaining two product lines. It is the same mechanism
that, in other domains, made Californian regulation on automobile emissions a de
facto national standard: the cost of fragmenting product lines exceeds that of
generalized compliance. In March 2026, Newsom applied this old Californian recipe to
the new object that is AI.

This approach has three decisive advantages over frontal regulation, which is why it
deserves attention. First, it avoids direct confrontation with industry on the
terrain of prohibitions: nothing is prohibited, access to public procurement is
conditioned, which is politically less costly and legally more solid. Second, it is
quick to implement: a governor's executive order requires neither a vote of Congress
nor a long federal regulatory procedure, and March 30 provides the demonstration —
one signature suffices. Finally, it is cumulative: each procurement decree adds to
the previous ones and progressively tightens the vise, as the N-5-26 sequence in
March then N-9-26 in September will show, the latter adding the study of a kill
switch and embedded verification with recommendations expected on November 16.

The limit of this lever obviously lies in its scope: procurement only regulates the
state's suppliers, not the entire market, and its effectiveness rests on the
assumption that public standards will diffuse to the private sector. But in 2026,
this assumption was particularly credible, because frontier labs were already selling
massively to administrations — and would sell even more, with the adoption of AI by
public services. The March 30 EO N-5-26 must therefore be read as a strategic bet:
that the market would follow the state, and that California, by regulating its own
purchases, would in practice regulate part of the American AI industry. The year
would prove this bet right, at least partially, with the multiplication of security
requirements throughout the chain.

### 6.3. March as a hinge: between February and April

March 2026 occupies in the chronology a position of discreet but revealing hinge:
between February's capital deluge and April's industrial pact, it was the month in
which public power — Californian in this case — reminded everyone that it intended
to set the rules of the game. This February–March–April sequence alone tells the
year's grammar: first private money runs wild, then the state sets its conditions,
finally industry seals its alliances within the framework thus defined. The
Anthropic–Amazon agreement of April 20 and 21, with its over-$100 billion commitment
on AWS over ten years, would fit into a landscape in which California had already
signaled, on March 30, that public power would not remain a spectator.

This three-act reading also illuminates what March was not: a month of model
announcements or fundraising rounds, of which the dossier retains no verified trace.
The contrast with February, the month of all financial operations, and with April,
the month of Claude Opus 4.7's return to the top of the rankings on April 16, is
total. March was a month of pure regulation, almost a month of lawyers in the middle
of a year of engineers and financiers — and that is precisely what makes it
narratively interesting: it recalls that the history of AI in 2026 was written not
only in data centers and trading rooms, but also in governors' offices and the texts
of decrees.

Finally, March prepares September in an almost symmetrical way: the March 30 EO N-5-26
on procurement finds its echo in the September 18 EO N-9-26 on the kill switch, as if
Newsom had deployed a Californian AI doctrine in two stages — first procurement,
then intrinsic security. In between, California would have signed laws SB 813 and AB
1405 on September 9, and the European Union would have brought its Digital Omnibus
into force on July 27. March 2026 thus appears, retrospectively, as the first
milestone of a year in which regulation, far from braking innovation as its
detractors feared, would accompany it step by step — from spring procurement to
autumn kill switches.
