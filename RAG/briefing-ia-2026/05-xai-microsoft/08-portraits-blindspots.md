# Portraits (Suleyman, Musk) and acknowledged blind spots

<a id="s05-35"></a>
### Suleyman, the architect of independence

Mustafa Suleyman is the name recurring at every 2026 Microsoft turning point, and the verified facts allow drawing a portrait in action.
At Build 2026 (June 2–3, Fort Mason San Francisco), it is he who announces the seven MAI models "trained from scratch, no distillation."
The formula is his, and it will stand as Microsoft's declaration of independence from OpenAI.
The word choice is calculated: "from scratch" asserts foundation work, "no distillation" cuts short any suspicion of intellectual dependence.
Suleyman does not merely announce models: he announces a posture — Microsoft is no longer others' models' distributor, it is a full-fledged lab.
Three months later, on September 14, it is his division — Microsoft AI — that publishes the draft Code of Conduct for Humanist AI (37 pages, six-week public consultation).
The Code bears his philosophical mark: "people matter more than AI," AI as subordinate tool, rejection of model welfare.
And it is he who carries the clash with Dario Amodei over model consciousness and welfare.
Suleyman thus wages war on two fronts with remarkable coherence: on the technical front, independence through in-house models; on the normative front, humanism against Anthropic's model welfare.
The two fronts reinforce each other: you can only preach subordinate-tool AI if you master the models you subordinate — and the MAI family is the proof of that mastery.
The boldness of the positioning must be measured: challenging OpenAI (the historic partner) on models and Anthropic (Cowork's partner) on philosophy, simultaneously, within one quarter.
It is an assumed rupture strategy, presupposing total confidence in teams and results.
The claimed benchmarks — MAI-Thinking-1 preferred over Sonnet 4.6, at Opus 4.6's level on SWE-Bench Pro; MAI-Transcribe-2 #1 on FLEURS — are that strategy's ammunition: Suleyman does not challenge lightly, he challenges with figures.
The revised Code, expected late 2026 to guide 2027 development, will be his internal-leadership test: turning a philosophical charter into engineering practices for thousands of developers.
If he succeeds, Microsoft will have something no competitor possesses: an explicit, public, operational doctrine of what AI should be.
That may be Suleyman's most durable 2026 legacy: not just models, but a doctrine.

<a id="s05-36"></a>
### Musk: communication through excess — and through admission

Elon Musk crosses 2026 as he crosses every year: through excess, but with a novelty — the public admission of imperfection.
Excess first: the SpaceX–xAI merger at $1.25 trillion (February 2), the record IPO at ~$1.77T with +19% on day one (June 12), Colossus v2 and its 550,000 GPUs presented as the world's largest AI supercomputer, Grok 4.8 and its 2.5 trillion parameters on an in-house C++ stack (announced September 13).
Each announcement is sized to crush the news cycle: the largest, the historic record, the never-seen.
It is the proven Musk method: occupy all media space so competitors play in reaction.
But 2026 brings an unprecedented variation: the Grok 4.7 episode.
Five delays, then a public downgrade of the model by Musk himself before its September 21 release.
A Musk saying "it will be worse than expected" is an event in itself — and one must ask whether it is weakness or tactics.
The tactical hypothesis is solid: by downgrading 4.7 eight days after the 4.8 announcement (September 13), Musk shifts expectations toward the future (2.5T parameters) and ring-fences the present (4.7 shipped without pressure).
It is high-level attention management: you do not suffer disappointment, you orchestrate it.
The constraint hypothesis is too: after five delays, silence was no longer tenable, and admission was the only credible discourse.
Either way, the result is the same: xAI showed it could publicly manage a plateau — a maturity the industry, obsessed with ascending curves, rarely practices.
The contrast between the year's two halves must also be noted.
First half: finance (February 2 merger, June 12 IPO) — Musk raises and merges, he builds the war balance sheet.
Second half: models (4.5 in July, 4.6 in August, 4.7/4.8 in September) and distribution (Bedrock August 19, GitHub Copilot in August, Copilot September 12) — Musk deploys the arsenal funded in the first half.
The sequence is logical: first the means, then the products, then distribution.
And in the middle, the $1.5B/month burn: the price of that logic, assumed as long as the valuation (~$230B) follows.
Finally, there is the rivalry with Microsoft — or rather its overcoming.
Musk and Microsoft opposed each other publicly for years; in 2026, Grok models enter Copilot Studio (February 19), GitHub Copilot (August), then Copilot (September 12, announced by Nadella himself).
Musk did not make peace with Microsoft: he did business with Microsoft.
That may be the year's most Muskian lesson: personal enmities are negotiable when distribution is at stake.

### Blind spots: what the facts don't say (yet)

An honest reference dossier must also say what it does not know: here are the blind spots, noted without speculation.
Grok 4.8: no release date, no price, no context window, no benchmarks — only the September 13 announcement (2.5T parameters, in-house C++ stack) is verified.
Any comment on its performance or schedule would be fabrication.
Grok 4.7: the cause of the five delays is not documented — quality, ambition, technical incident: the facts do not decide.
The financial terms of the Grok-in-Copilot integration (revenue share, tariffs, commitments) are not public in the verified facts.
MAI-Voice-2: the Flash variant's date ("coming soon") is unspecified; the voice-cloning safeguards are not detailed in the facts.
Cowork 1, the "coming" in-house fine-tuned model, has neither verified date nor characteristics.
Project Solara: beyond Qualcomm and MediaTek (reference designs) and the four pilots (AccuWeather, Best Buy, CVS Health, Target), the actual manufacturers and precise dates (late 2026/2027) remain windows, not commitments.
MAI-Transcribe-2's permanent tariff after the December 31 promo is unknown: $0.10/hour is an introductory price, not a list price.
The revised Code of Conduct (expected late 2026) does not exist yet: only the September 14 draft (37 pages, six-week consultation) is verified.
The public consultation's results are not known.
Finally, market shares, usage volumes, and revenues generated by each of these announcements are not in the verified facts — and that is normal: 2026 is the year of launches, the balance sheets will come in 2027.
These blind spots are not the dossier's weaknesses: they are its boundaries, and respecting them is what makes it reliable.
Each missing fact is a question for later — and the next section (or the next dossier) will have to answer it.
