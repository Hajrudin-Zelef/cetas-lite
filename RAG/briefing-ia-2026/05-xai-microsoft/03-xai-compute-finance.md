# xAI: Colossus v2, war chest and the SpaceX–xAI merger

<a id="s05-9"></a>
### Colossus v2: the 550,000-GPU machine

Colossus v2, xAI's supercomputer, lines up 550,000 GPUs.
It is presented as the world's largest AI supercomputer.
The figure deserves a pause, because it redefines the scale of the AI race.
550,000 GPUs: that is an order of magnitude beyond the most ambitious training clusters publicly documented by competitors.
At that scale, we are no longer talking about a datacenter but an industrial infrastructure, with its own power plants, its own networks, its own logistics.
The name itself — Colossus — is a program: the second version embraces an excess that the first only sketched.
The presentation as the "world's largest AI supercomputer" is a claim of infrastructure leadership.
In the AI race, there are three superimposed competitions: models, talent, and compute.
xAI chooses to strike hard on the third, the most capital-intensive and hardest to replicate quickly.
A competitor can copy a model architecture in a few months; it cannot copy 550,000 GPUs without years of investment and procurement.
Colossus v2 is therefore also a defensive moat: a barrier to entry erected in silicon.
The link with Grok 4.8 is direct and assumed in this dossier: you do not announce 2.5 trillion parameters without the compute muscle to go with it.
Colossus v2 is the material condition of possibility for 4.8, and 4.8 is the economic justification for Colossus v2.
One without the other, each announcement would seem disproportionate; together, they form a coherent narrative: the largest supercomputer for the largest model, on an in-house C++ stack optimized for that precise hardware.
It is also an implicit answer to the limits of the post-training upgrade strategy.
As long as xAI iterated on V9 via post-training, a supercomputer of that size could seem oversized.
With 4.8 and its 2.5T parameters, the sizing makes sense: post-training was the smart waiting phase, Colossus v2 the assault phase.
Colossus v2 must also be read in light of xAI's finances: $1.5 billion burned per month, a valuation of about $230 billion.
A 550,000-GPU supercomputer represents a capital investment of several billion dollars — GPUs, networking, energy, buildings.
That CapEx can only be financed by massive raises or by leaning on a larger balance sheet.
And that is exactly what the SpaceX–xAI merger (February 2, $1.25 trillion, all-stock) brings: a combined balance sheet capable of carrying investments that xAI alone could not durably sustain.
Finally, Colossus v2 raises the energy question, even if the verified facts do not quantify it.
550,000 GPUs under load is electricity consumption on the order of a hundred megawatts continuously — the equivalent of a mid-size city.
In a context where energy access is becoming the bottleneck of the AI race, owning the largest supercomputer also means owning the energy contracts that go with it.
The real scarcity, at this level, is no longer the GPU: it is the megawatt.

### The war chest: SpaceX IPO, burn rate, and valuation

xAI's ambitions cost money, and the verified facts give three figures that sketch the financial picture: the SpaceX IPO, xAI's burn rate, and its valuation.
On June 12, 2026, the SpaceX IPO takes effect: Nasdaq listing under ticker SPCX, $75 billion raised at $135 per share, for a valuation of about $1.77 trillion.
It is a historic record: the largest IPO ever executed, by far.
The stock gains 19% on its first trading day.
19% on a $75 billion IPO is instant value creation of more than $14 billion for first-day subscribers — the market enthusiastically validates the proposed price.
Several lessons emerge from this record IPO.
First, the market's appetite for Musk assets remains intact in 2026, despite — or because of — the man's volatility.
Second, $75 billion raised in one shot is a war chest that changes the game for the entire Musk ecosystem, including xAI via the merger.
Third, a $1.77T valuation places SpaceX in the very closed circle of the world's most valued companies, at the level of historic tech giants.
On the other side of the balance sheet, xAI burns $1.5 billion per month.
$1.5B/month is $18B per year: a capital-consumption pace that quickly makes any classic fundraising insufficient.
At that pace, the SpaceX IPO's $75 billion would alone represent a bit more than four years of xAI's burn — a theoretical calculation, but one that gives the scale.
This burn rate is explainable: training giant models, building Colossus v2, recruiting the best talent, distributing at aggressive prices ($2/$6 across the whole Grok 4 lineage).
xAI is buying its growth at gold prices, betting that scale will eventually pay.
xAI's valuation is given at about $230 billion.
$230B for a company burning $1.5B/month is investors betting on a trajectory, not on present profits.
It is also a valuation that places xAI at the level of the best-funded large labs on the planet, and that makes the next step credible: the merger with SpaceX.
Bringing the three figures together tells a simple story: SpaceX brings the balance sheet ($1.77T, $75B raised), xAI brings growth and ambition ($230B valuation, $1.5B/month burn), and the merger marries them.
Without SpaceX, xAI would have to raise tens of billions at an unsustainable pace to fund Colossus v2 and Grok 4.8.
Without xAI, SpaceX would be a very profitable space and telecom company but without an AI narrative — yet in 2026, the AI narrative is what justifies the highest valuation multiples.
The June 12 IPO is therefore also, indirectly, the financing of Musk's AI ambition: the $75B raised and the stock-market currency (the SPCX share) become the ammunition of the AI decade.

<a id="s05-11"></a>
### SpaceX–xAI merger (February 2): the $1.25 trillion bet

On February 2, 2026, the merger between SpaceX and xAI is announced: a 100% stock operation (all-stock), for an amount of $1.25 trillion.
It is one of the largest mergers in industrial history, and it deserves to be dissected.
First the structure: all-stock means no cash changes hands — xAI shareholders receive shares in the combined entity.
It is the signature of mergers between companies rich in valuation but poor in liquidity: you pay with your own currency, i.e., with the market's confidence in future value.
Then the amount: $1.25 trillion — 1.25 trillion.
Compare with xAI's $230B valuation and SpaceX's ~$1.77T at its IPO (four months later): the operation values the combined whole at a level reflecting both assets and their expected synergies.
The date is significant: February 2, more than four months before the SpaceX IPO (June 12).
The merger thus precedes the IPO: it is the combined entity — or at least the combined project — that will go to market in June.
SpaceX's record IPO then reads differently: it is not just the listing of a space company, it is the listing of the Musk conglomerate integrating xAI and its AI ambitions.
The $75B raised funds rockets, satellites, and the 550,000 GPUs indifferently.
Strategically, the merger answers a simple question: how to durably fund a lab that burns $1.5B/month and wants to build the world's largest supercomputer?
Answer: by leaning it against a company that generates massive recurring revenue (space launch, Starlink) and can raise $75B in a day.
It is the vertically integrated conglomerate model: today's profits fund tomorrow's bets.
The stated — or plausible — synergies go beyond finance.
SpaceX brings xAI: launch capacity for possible space-based compute infrastructure, a Starlink network for distribution and data collection, an extreme-engineering culture compatible with Colossus v2's.
xAI brings SpaceX: the AI narrative that justifies the highest stock multiples, models for autonomy (vehicles, robots, satellites), and diversification beyond space.
There is also a defensive dimension: by merging before the IPO, Musk locks in control of the whole and prevents an independent xAI from becoming prey or a lever for activist investors.
The all-stock operation preserves the founder's control structure: no cash dilution, no new dominant shareholder.
Finally, one must measure what $1.25 trillion means for the AI industry.
It is a signal sent to all competitors: the AI race is no longer played between venture-funded labs, but between conglomerates capable of mobilizing hundreds of billions.
OpenAI has Microsoft; Anthropic has Amazon and Google; xAI now has SpaceX.
Consolidation is underway, and the February 2 merger is its most spectacular act to date.
One unknown the facts do not resolve remains: the combined entity's operational governance.
But the essential is elsewhere: with this merger, Musk gave himself the financial means of his ambition — 550,000 GPUs, 2.5T parameters, and an assumed burn rate.
