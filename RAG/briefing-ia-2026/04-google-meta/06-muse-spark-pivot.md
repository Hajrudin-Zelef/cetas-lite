# The Muse Spark pivot: Meta cuts into the LLaMA lineage

<a id="s04-7"></a>
### The Muse Spark pivot: Meta cuts into the LLaMA lineage

It is one of the year's most brutal decisions on Meta's side:
the release of LLaMA 5, already contested, is followed by an
avowed pivot — the company is now concentrating its forces on
the Muse Spark lineage, and the associated calendar speaks of a
2027 horizon for what comes next. In other words, Meta is
provisionally burying its historic open-models brand to bet
everything on a new lineage. The symbolic violence of the
gesture must be measured: LLaMA is the family that made Meta an
unavoidable actor in open AI since 2023, the one that nourished
tens of thousands of derivative projects. Abandoning it, even
temporarily, is an admission that the brand no longer carries
the promise.

What does a "contested release" mean for LLaMA 5? The verified
facts do not give the detail, but the expression itself is
telling: a contested release is a release whose legitimacy is
debated — by the community, by the press, by partners, perhaps
internally. One can imagine the usual ingredients of such
situations: benchmarks below expectations, a fuzzy positioning
between open and closed, a calendar upended by the competition.
Whatever the exact cause, the result is clear: instead of
patching, Meta chose to rebuild. It is a partial admission of
failure, but it is also a mark of strategic lucidity —
persisting with a contested lineage would have cost more in
credibility than the pivot.

The choice of the Muse Spark lineage as the new standard-bearer
is not neutral. The name itself — "Muse," the inspirer —
contrasts with the technical acronym "LLaMA." It is a
consumer-product name, not a research-project name. The message
is clear: Meta no longer wants merely to supply model weights to
researchers; it wants products used by hundreds of millions of
people — like Meta Muse, the personal agent launched on
September 8 and built on this lineage. The pivot is therefore
not only technical, it is commercial: moving from a logic of
model supply to a logic of products.

The 2027 horizon also deserves careful reading. By pushing the
deadline back, Meta buys itself time — time to make Muse Spark
a mature lineage, time to let the LLaMA 5 controversy die down,
time to watch the competition's trajectory. But 2027 is also
far away in an industry where cycles are counted in months: by
then, Google may have released Gemini 4, the Flash family may
be on its twelfth version, and the personal-agent market will
have settled. Meta's bet is therefore twofold: that the Muse
Spark lineage is strong enough to hold the interim, and that
the 2027 return happens on sound foundations rather than on the
ruins of a damaged brand.

This pivot must finally be placed in Meta's broader 2026
trajectory. In the same year, the company launches Muse Glimmer
under the Apache 2.0 license — a return to avowed openness —,
enters its FAIR agents in Kaggle competitions, and deploys
massive infrastructure with NVIDIA. The Muse Spark pivot is
therefore not a retreat; it is a reorganization: open continues
to exist (Glimmer), research continues (FAIR, AIRA₃), but the
commercial spearhead changes name and logic. Meta seems to have
drawn a lesson from its LLaMA years: open source is a
formidable ecosystem lever, but it is products that win users.
Muse Spark is the name of that lesson.

The question the whole industry is asking remains: is LLaMA
dead, or dormant? The verified facts speak of a postponement to
2027 and a concentration on Muse Spark, not a definitive burial.
The cautious formulation is therefore required: the LLaMA
lineage is placed in strategic pause, its brand left fallow.
But brands die hard in tech, and the community built around
LLaMA will not vanish by decree. If Muse Spark disappoints in
turn, the temptation of a return to LLaMA will be strong. The
2026 pivot is a decision, not a destiny — and like any strategic
decision, it will be judged on its results.
