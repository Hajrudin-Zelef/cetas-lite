# Gemini 4: the ghost model, teased but not released

<a id="s04-6"></a>
### Gemini 4: the ghost model — teased, training, but not released

Of Gemini 4, we officially know one thing: Google teased it, and
its training is underway. Its release is unconfirmed. This
sentence deserves to be written out in full, because in the 2026
media noise, the temptation is strong to talk about Gemini 4 as
if it already existed — to lend it benchmarks, dates, prices.
None of that appears in the verified facts. Gemini 4 is, at this
stage, an object of communication and a training project, not a
product. Everything else is speculation, and this dossier
refuses to engage in it.

The teasing itself is nevertheless a strategic fact worth
analyzing. Why announce the training of a model whose release is
unconfirmed? The answer comes down to three classic motivations.
First, reassurance: in a year when Meta launches Meta Muse, when
personal agents become the battleground, Google must show its
roadmap does not stop at the 3.8 family. Second, recruiting and
retention: researchers want to work on the next frontier, not
maintain the previous one. Third, occupying media space: a
"Gemini 4 in training" is a way to capture attention without
committing to a date — the riskiest commitment of all in this
industry, where delays are the norm.

The teasing must also be understood as a signal addressed to the
competition. Announcing that you are training the next model is
telling the other labs: speed up, we are not stopping. It is a
form of soft deterrence, costing little — a sentence in a
keynote, a mention in a blog post — that can pay off handsomely
if it pushes a competitor to rush its own release. But it is
also a double-edged weapon: tease without delivering for long
enough, and you burn announcement credit. Google, which has
known premature announcements mocked by the press in the past,
seems this time to be playing the sobriety card: one tease, one
fact — training is underway — and nothing more.

From the market's perspective, Gemini 4's absence in 2026 is
almost as informative as its presence would have been. It means
Google believes it can hold the year with the 3.x family: four
Flash versions in three months, a voice Live variant, a Cyber
variant for defense. This demonstrates that the iterative
strategy described above is not a stopgap while waiting for the
big model, but a deliberate choice. If Gemini 4 were ready, or
close to it, would Google have multiplied 3.8 versions? Perhaps
— lineups coexist — but the Flash series' frantic cadence
suggests instead a company that has decided to make rapid
iteration its normal operating mode, relegating the "big models"
to rarer events.

For developers and companies building on Gemini, the lesson is
practical: plan nothing on Gemini 4. No date, no price, no spec
sheet is confirmed. Rational bets are placed on the 3.8 family —
whose promotional prices run through end of 2026, which
incidentally gives an indication: Google does not expect its
price grid to be upended by a major release before year's end,
or else it has chosen to lock developers in on attractive prices
precisely before migrating them. Either way, the reasonable
planning horizon stops at the 3.8 family.

Finally, there is an irony to note: in a year when Meta purely
and simply abandons LLaMA 5 (see the next subsection), Google
does the opposite — it promises nothing it cannot deliver, it
teases without dating, it iterates instead of revolutionizing.
Two opposite communication strategies facing the same
uncertainty about big models: Meta cuts deep and owns the break,
Google temporizes and capitalizes on continuity. History will
tell which was right. Meanwhile, let us hold to the discipline
of the facts: Gemini 4 is in training, its release is
unconfirmed, and everything else is conditional.
