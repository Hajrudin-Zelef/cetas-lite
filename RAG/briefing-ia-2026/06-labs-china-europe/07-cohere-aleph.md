# Cohere: Command A+ and the Aleph Alpha merger

<a id="s06-8"></a>
### Cohere: Command A+ and the merger with Aleph Alpha, a textbook case of sovereignty

Cohere, the Canadian lab, signs in 2026 a double news item — product
and corporate — the second of which could redraw the map of "sovereign"
Western AI.
Start with the product: on May 20, 2026, Cohere published the detailed
spec sheet of Command A+.
It is a 218-billion-parameter MoE, 25 billion active, published in
open weights under the Apache 2.0 license on Hugging Face.
The Apache 2.0 license, like the MIT, permits commercial use; it adds an
explicit clause on patents, appreciated by enterprises.
The model integrates native citations — a Cohere signature feature,
which anchors responses in verifiable sources and answers the
traceability needs of enterprise deployments.
W4A4 quantization is described as near-lossless: the compressed model
runs on a single B200 or two H100s, making it deployable on reasonable
infrastructure for a model of this size.
The context window is 128K tokens — modest against the million tokens
now standard at Chinese labs, but sufficient for the targeted enterprise
use cases.
The model covers 48 languages, is multimodal, and consolidates the four
previous Command A variants into a unified offering.
It is therefore a "pragmatic enterprise" model: not the largest, not
the longest context, but open, citing its sources, multilingual and
deployable — exactly the spec sheet of regulated CIOs.

On September 16, 2026, Cohere and Aleph Alpha signed the definitive
agreement for their merger — concretizing a plan announced in April —
to create a combined entity of about $20 billion.
The terms are precise: the entity operates under the Cohere name, with
dual headquarters in Toronto and Berlin, more than 1,000 employees, the
Heidelberg office as research hub, and Aleph Alpha co-leader Ilhan
Scheer becoming COO.
It is the meeting of the Canadian champion of enterprise AI and the
German champion of sovereign AI — Aleph Alpha, based in Heidelberg,
having built itself on the promise of a European AI compliant with
European regulation.
The resulting entity is presented as the first transatlantic sovereign
AI stack, backed by Canada and Germany.
The sovereignty positioning is explicit, and it has a name: sidestepping
the U.S. CLOUD Act — the law that allows U.S. authorities to demand
access to data hosted by American companies, wherever it is stored.
By combining a Canadian and a German headquarters, with no American
parent company, the new entity offers European and Canadian clients a
legally watertight alternative to American extraterritoriality.
It is a commercial argument as much as a political one: for governments,
banks, and industrial companies subject to data-sovereignty
requirements, it is a unique value proposition on the market.

The deal's mechanics call for several precisions, because the figures
circulating are confusing.
First, the transaction remains subject to regulatory approvals — Canada,
Germany, and possibly the European Union — with closing expected at the
end of 2026: the merger is therefore not yet completed as of the
briefing date, and a regulatory veto, however unlikely, remains
theoretically possible.
Second, two figures often wrongly cited as linked to the merger must be
distinguished from it.
The Schwarz Group announces €500 million (about $600 million, according
to Reuters) in Cohere's next Series E: this is an investment in Cohere,
not in the merger itself.
And the €11 to €13 billion sometimes mentioned concern a German data
center campus operated via StackIT, with up to 100,000 chips: this is a
separate infrastructure project, not a contribution to the merged entity.
These distinctions matter: they avoid overestimating the merger's
financial perimeter, which is about $20B in combined valuation, and they
show that the German ecosystem is investing in parallel in compute — via
StackIT — without it being the same operation.
For an analyst, the lesson is to always verify what a figure applies to
before adding it to another.

As a textbook case of sovereignty, the Cohere–Aleph Alpha merger
deserves a closer look, because it crystallizes three underlying trends.
First, AI sovereignty is becoming a product: it is no longer just
political rhetoric, it is a feature sold to clients — data jurisdiction,
compute localization, non-American governance.
Second, scale is becoming the condition of sovereignty: at ~$20B and
more than 1,000 employees, the entity reaches a critical size that lets
it compete on R&D, where isolated national champions remained
undersized.
Third, the non-American transatlantic axis is emerging as a third way:
neither Washington nor Beijing, but a Toronto–Berlin–Heidelberg corridor
offering Western democracies an AI "aligned" with their legal frameworks.
The comparison with Mistral is instructive: where the French company
secures American compute (Microsoft, Vera Rubin GPUs) on European soil,
the Cohere–Aleph Alpha pair bets on total capital and legal independence
— two philosophies of sovereignty, one "hosting-based", the other
"ownership-based".
Both can coexist; they answer different client segments, depending on
whether the requirement is about data localization or about the absence
of American control.

In summary, Cohere in September 2026 plays a double game: a solid,
open enterprise model (Command A+, Apache 2.0, native citations, 48
languages), and a corporate operation that could make the merged entity
the standard-bearer of sovereign Western AI.
If closing happens at end of 2026 as planned, the competitive landscape
will count a new ~$20B actor explicitly positioned against American
extraterritoriality — a positioning neither American nor Chinese labs
can occupy.
This is, with Mistral's record raise, the other big European fact of
the period: European AI sovereignty is no longer decreed, it is financed
and merged.
