# Meta–Scale AI and Beijing's block of the Manus acquisition

<a id="s08-10"></a>
### 8.9. Meta–Scale AI: $14.3 billion and the leadership reshuffle

In February 2026, Meta invests $14.3 billion in Scale AI and takes 49%
of the AI data company's capital. The operation comes with a double
leadership move: Alexandr Wang, Scale AI's founder, becomes Meta's
Chief AI Officer; Yann LeCun, the historic AI figure at Meta, leaves
the company. A capital deal, a people deal, and a change of era.

The context is data. Scale AI has established itself as the
indispensable supplier of annotated data and evaluations for model
training: without quality data, no frontier models. By taking 49% of
Scale AI for $14.3 billion, Meta secures privileged access to the
most critical — and most underestimated — link in the AI value chain.
The percentage is significant: 49%, just under a majority, means
de facto control without accounting consolidation nor, Meta hopes,
competition authorities' veto.

The human side is just as structural. Alexandr Wang's arrival as Chief
AI Officer puts a data entrepreneur, not an academic researcher, at
the helm of Meta's AI — a strong signal about priorities: execution,
products, industrial scale. Yann LeCun's departure, deep-learning
pioneer and embodiment of open research at Meta, marks the end of an
era: the one in which Meta's AI defined itself through its
publications and open models. The new era is defined by $14 billion
deals and unified leadership positions.

The implications are threefold. For Meta, it is acceleration: data
secured, leadership tightened, course set on mass-market AI products.
For Scale AI, it is consecration — and increased dependence on a 49%
shareholder that is also its biggest potential customer. For the
industry, it is confirmation that the AI battle is now fought as much
on training data as on architectures: whoever controls the data
pipelines controls the labs' pace. And for open research, LeCun's
departure sounds like a symbol: in 2026, power in AI has passed from
university labs to the giants' balance sheets.

<a id="s08-11"></a>
### 8.10. Beijing blocks Meta's acquisition of Manus: the first Chinese veto

In February 2026, China blocks Meta's acquisition of Manus — a
$2 billion operation. It is the first forced dismantling of an AI
acquisition by Beijing: never before had Chinese authorities scuttled
this way the takeover of an artificial intelligence player by a
foreign giant. The amount, $2 billion, is almost anecdotal against
the deals that precede it in this section; the political scope, on
the other hand, is historic.

The context is Manus: a Chinese AI agent that had caused a sensation
with its autonomy capabilities, seen as one of the showcases of the
Chinese agent ecosystem. That an American giant — Meta, at the very
moment it invests $14.3 billion in Scale AI — seeks to grab it for
$2 billion says how strategic these agent technologies are. That
Beijing opposes it says the other side: AI agents are now classified,
in China, among the sensitive technological assets whose exit from
national territory is no longer a mere commercial formality.

The blocking mechanism deserves emphasis: this is not an upstream
authorization refusal, but a forced dismantling — the acquisition is
undone by public authority. It is one notch above a preventive veto:
the Chinese state asserts its capacity to undo what was signed, even
when the acquirer is one of the world's largest market
capitalizations. The precedent is set, and it applies to all future
operations: any acquisition of a Chinese AI player by a foreign group
will now proceed under the Damocles' sword of a political review —
even a cancellation.

The implications are considerable for acquisition strategies. For
American giants, China becomes a high regulatory-risk buying zone:
Manus's $2 billion shows that even modest amounts can trigger the
veto when the technology is deemed strategic. For Chinese AI
startups, the message is double: valuation protected from foreign
predation, but also a reduced exit horizon — the foreign acquirer is
no longer a reliable exit door. For investors, it is a geopolitical
discount factor to integrate into any investment thesis on Chinese
AI.

Finally, this blockage fits into a sequence: while Z.ai raises ~$9
billion in two months with orchestrated financing mechanisms and
Moonshot prepares its Hong Kong IPO, Beijing locks the exit door.
China's 2026 strategy appears in relief: massively fund national
champions, open domestic and Hong Kong capital markets to them, and
forbid their absorption by foreigners. The "first forced dismantling"
is no accident: it is the birth certificate of a doctrine.
