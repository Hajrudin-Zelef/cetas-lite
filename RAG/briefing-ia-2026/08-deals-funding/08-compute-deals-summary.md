# The structuring compute deals and the deals summary table

<a id="s08-14"></a>
### 8.13. The structuring "compute" deals: the sinews of war

Behind the raises and mergers, 2026's most structuring deals may be
those concerning computing capacity. Three agreements, signed between
April and July, lock in gigawatts of power and tens of billions in
crossed commitments between labs and infrastructure providers. It is
the sinews of war: without compute, no frontier models.

First agreement: Anthropic–Amazon, April 20–21, 2026. Anthropic
commits to spending over $100 billion on AWS over ten years — 5
gigawatts of capacity, with an upgrade of Trainium chips (from
Trainium2 to Trainium4) and Graviton processors, AWS becoming its
primary cloud. In mirror, Amazon invests $5 billion immediately in
Anthropic, plus up to $20 billion more under conditions. The apparent
asymmetry — a $100 billion commitment on one side, a $5 (+$20) billion
investment on the other — tells the deal's nature: Anthropic buys
compute on credit against its future, Amazon buys a share of that
future. It is the largest cloud agreement in AI history, and it binds
for ten years the destiny of a frontier lab to that of a hyperscaler.

Second agreement: AMD–Anthropic, July 22, 2026. AMD will supply
Anthropic with up to 2 gigawatts of its MI450-series accelerators,
deployed in Helios racks, with a first gigawatt from the first half
of 2027. And AMD invests up to $5 billion in Anthropic. The pattern
is the same as with Amazon — silicon for capital — but the stakes
differ: for AMD, it is the offensive against Nvidia's domination,
with a first-class reference customer for its new accelerator
generation. For Anthropic, it is source diversification: after
AWS-Trainium, AMD's MI450s reduce dependence on a single supplier and
create permanent negotiating leverage.

Third agreement: Microsoft × Mistral, July 21, 2026. Several billion
dollars, thousands of Vera Rubin GPUs deployed in Mistral's European
data centers. Here the direction of flow is reversed: it is the
American hyperscaler investing in the European lab's infrastructure,
installing its next GPU generation there. For Microsoft, it is a
foothold in sovereign Europe and an outlet for Vera Rubin; for
Mistral, it is the securing of its compute capacity without diluting
its capitalistic independence — the data centers stay European, the
chips deploy there in partnership.

Read together, these three deals draw the grammar of compute in 2026:
multi-year commitments with nine- or ten-figure sums, crossed
counterparts (capacity for capital), precise industrial timelines
(first gigawatt in H1 2027), and a geography that matters — United
States, Europe. They also explain the giant raises of section 8.2:
when you commit to spending over $100 billion in ten years, you need
$30 billion Series G rounds and $65 billion Series H rounds. Compute
is the expense line that eats all the others, and these agreements
are its contractual translation.

At the end of this overview, one conviction imposes itself: 2026 is
the year AI became a heavy-capital industry. The amounts — $1,250
billion for a merger, $65 billion for a round, $75 billion for an
IPO, over $100 billion for ten years of cloud — belong to the
vocabulary of extractive industries and major infrastructure
programs, not to that of software. The winners of this sequence are
those who managed to convert capital into capacity: secured
gigawatts, acquired developer platforms, talent retained at
billion-dollar cost. The potential losers are those who, lacking a
balance sheet, will have to rent their future at a high price — or
sell it. And above it all, the states: Washington arming its
champions, Beijing locking down its own, Brussels and Berlin financing
theirs. 2026's deal season is not only financial: it is the staff
map of the decade opening up.
