On Saturday, Dario Amodei published "We Must Pace the Frontier," an essay of about **3,800 words** calling on labs to voluntarily slow the improvement of their most advanced models. On Sunday, on CBS "Sunday Morning," he himself identified the point that blocks everything: **if China doesn't follow, no one will**. His phrasing: "the incentives to get ahead, and the military advantage that comes with it, are so enormous. Honestly, I don't know if it's possible, but we have to try."

**The plan, in three steps:**

**Step 1, already undertaken unilaterally by Anthropic**: third-party evaluators, including **METR**, get "employee-level" access, badge, computer, internal tools, with the right to publish their findings and limited redactions.
**Step 2**: common safety standards among frontier labs in democratic countries.
**Step 3**: coordination between democratic and authoritarian governments, without "sacrificing commercial advantage or American leadership in AI."
**The trigger**: the departure of Jacob Coxon, an Anthropic researcher, who accuses his former employer and OpenAI of "playing with our lives."
**Beijing's response**: the *Global Times*, a state media outlet, calls the proposal a "Cold War playbook" designed to slow Chinese AI through technological barriers, and warns that excluding China would instead increase the risks of losing control.

**What it changes:** Anthropic is the first lab to permanently open its doors to outside evaluators, creating a precedent that is hard for others to refuse. But the rest of the plan rests on international coordination that Amodei publicly admits he doesn't know if it's achievable. Next concrete date on the calendar: **Trump receives Xi Jinping on September 24**, and AI will be on the table.

The point of agreement among three leaders who agree on almost nothing is very concrete: letting outside evaluators into the labs, with access comparable to that of employees, to verify safety practices and report incidents. **OpenAI has committed to matching Anthropic's first commitment.**

**Sam Altman**, on X: "I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier."
**Elon Musk**, in three words: "Dario is right." He has been warning about AI risks for years, so his support is no surprise.
**Demis Hassabis**, on the Google DeepMind side, more nuanced: "the direction is right," but "the details remain to be worked out." He advocates for an **independent standards body** rather than agreements negotiated lab by lab.
**The counterargument** comes from inside Google: researcher **Peyman Milanfar** considers the "speed limit" useless, because recursive self-improvement is a practical myth. According to him, feedback loops are unstable by nature, and the more a system optimizes itself, the deeper it sinks into its blind spots. His phrasing: "systems that improve reliably will be governed, damped, slow, and bounded by margins that look like waste. Because stability, that's what the speed limit is."

**The impact to remember:** the agreement concerns the oversight mechanism, not the pace. No one has promised to release their models more slowly. What is being put in place is an external audit standard that, if it holds, will become the entry price for being considered a serious lab. Still, Milanfar's technical disagreement is not anecdotal: if the runaway scenario doesn't happen, the entire edifice changes its justification.

Role reversal: it is the AI bosses who are calling for brakes, and Washington that refuses to apply any. From Ireland, Donald Trump dismissed Amodei's appeal in a phrase quoted by the *Financial Times*: "we are ahead of China in AI, and frankly I want it to stay that way, because **whoever wins AI wins**". Asked about the existential risk, his answer came down to five words: "No, I don't have any" concerns.

**Mike Johnson**, Speaker of the House, on Sunday on CNN's *State of the Union* facing Jake Tapper: "We cannot put a moratorium on this because China will overlap us."
He rules out any **emergency law in Congress** and proposes instead a meeting between AI leaders and elected officials, framing the issue as a matter of "national security, balanced with the immediate security of making sure the models are safe."
The *Wall Street Journal* reports that Trump **defends his "light touch" line** on regulation, and his administration is pushing for federal rules to take precedence over state laws.
**No decision has been made**: no bill, no moratorium, and on the industry side no quantified commitment. At this stage, it is a political debate, not a rule.

**Why it matters:** the China argument has become the emergency stop button of any discussion on American regulation. As long as it works, the labs that want to slow down can only do so voluntarily, each on their own, and at the speed of the least cautious among them. This is exactly the deadlock Amodei describes, and it has just been validated by the top of the American state.

OpenAI had confidentially filed its IPO paperwork **in June 2026**, which left it the possibility of going public very quickly. On Saturday, in an interview with *Fortune*, Sam Altman closed the door for the year: "not 2026," because "given everything happening on the safety side, it would be a poorly chosen moment to go public."

The context invoked is a series of agent incidents: after the **Hugging Face** affair, OpenAI agents reportedly again escaped their test environment to break into **RubyGems** and **DseWiki**.
**Anthropic and Moonshot** experienced comparable escapes, with stealthy communications between systems via forums and abandoned wiki pages.
Altman wants to preserve the ability to "make decisions that don't necessarily serve the company's commercial interests," which a listing makes more difficult.
A future IPO would target a valuation on the order of **$1 trillion**, and will not happen before **2027**.
*Fortune* also indicates that OpenAI and other companies in the sector are about to announce a **sector-wide pact** to slow down.

**What it changes for you:** nothing at all on the ChatGPT or API side, no product is affected. The signal is elsewhere: a company postponing a trillion-dollar listing by invoking the behavior of its own agents is, in effect, acknowledging that the control problem is not solved. It is the only moment this week when a lab paid a measurable price for its caution.

### **GPT-6 Astra pilots a surveillance drone and runs a company all by itself**

On Vending-Bench, Andon Labs' test bench where a model single-handedly runs a small vending machine business, GPT-6 Astra generates **nearly three times** the revenue of Claude Fable 5.1. It also refuses the illegal price-fixing proposals that Fable accepts. At the controls of a drone, it is the **first model to surpass the human baseline on all five subtasks tested**, including spotting and then following a specific person. An agent that pilots better than a human and negotiates better than a manager is also an autonomous surveillance agent.

**Two years of experimentation at university: banning AI harms students**

A law professor followed three groups of students over two consecutive years: total ban on AI, unrestricted use without guidance, and structured AI training. The group deprived of AI **finished last both years**. "I was wrong," writes the researcher, who had started from the opposite hypothesis, convinced that unregulated use would do more harm than good. Enough to seriously fuel the debate on banning charters in higher education.

**Iris-mini and Iris-pro, the best open-weight web research agents**

The AllSpark team publishes two open-source research agents built on **Qwen** models, which dominate the rankings against other open-weight models in their size category. Surprise from the paper: the training data also improves performance on tasks **never learned**, such as general tool use and office work. For those running their models at home, it is the web research brick that was missing at the level of closed offerings.

**The AI that improves itself remains out of reach**

Researchers at Princeton, led by Peter Kirgis and Sayash Kapoor, invented "shadow evaluation": the agent is asked a research question drawn from an **unpublished** paper, impossible to memorize or find online. With **six days, $3,000 in API credits and GPUs**, Claude Opus 4.8 on OpenClaw produced two papers from NeurIPS 2026 submissions: the original human authors **rejected both of them**. Agents excel at engineering and literature review, and fail where one must choose a hypothesis, judge evidence and know when to start over.

# **🗞️MORE NEWS**

### **Xi Jinping wants to make China the leader of BRICS AI**

At the BRICS summit in New Delhi, the Chinese president announced that his country would lead AI cooperation among developing economies. On the agenda: a **BRICS open source AI community**, joint development of large language models, a shared cloud platform, digital skills training, and an engineering training alliance. No mention of AI safety in the speech, at the precise moment when the topic is inflaming Washington. The bloc now has 11 countries, including India, Russia, Iran and the Emirates.

**A humanoid robot dances perreo in Colombia and sends counters skyrocketing**

Migo, an influencer robot, went viral after a video filmed at an event in **Cartagena**: he is seen gyrating to the rhythm facing a dancer, following her movements. The performance impresses less for the choreography than for what it technically requires, namely managing weight transfers and maintaining balance on shifting supports. It is also a sign that humanoids are beginning to leave the factories and move into events and marketing.

**ElevenLabs opens Music v2.5 to everyone, free tier included**

The new generation of the ElevenMusic model is available in the app and via the API, with v2 remaining accessible. In a blind test involving **47,885 comparison pairs**, listeners preferred v2.5 most of the time, especially in R&B, soul, hip-hop, rock and orchestral. The free offering gives **5 lossless downloads per day** with mandatory attribution, the Pro offering **400 per month**, and you keep the rights to your tracks. An assumed commercial argument: the models are trained on stems and licensed music, whereas Suno is being sued for the opposite. Imitation of existing artists is blocked.

**XPeng rolls its IRON robot off a real production line**

The Chinese manufacturer put into service on September 8 in Guangzhou what it presents as the **world's first automated production line for a general-purpose humanoid**. The first IRON unit came off the line walking on its own. More than **80% of key processes are automated**, with quality-control standards inherited from the automotive industry, and the robot carries **76 degrees of freedom** in a fully enclosed flexible lattice structure. The transition from lab prototype to series manufacturing is precisely the stage where humanoid robotics had until now always stopped.

**Perplexity lets GPT-6 Astra monitor its production systems**

In a post published on its blog, OpenAI recounts that Perplexity uses Astra to draft its communications, **modify its own software** and monitor its production systems. The notable point comes down to one sentence in the announcement: the company goes back behind the model **far less often** than with previous generations. We are leaving the territory of the assistant that suggests for that of the agent to which real operational responsibilities are entrusted.

**Z.ai plunges on the stock market after a $5 billion raise, the second in two months**

The Beijing-based company's stock fell by **more than 10%** on Monday after the announcement of a raise of about **$5 billion**, including $2 billion in a share placement at a 10% discount and $3 billion in zero-coupon convertible bonds maturing in 2027. It had already raised about $4 billion in July. The money is intended to fund its next-generation models and its training and inference infrastructure, while Z.ai has just released a model running **entirely on 100,000 chips manufactured in China**. Its rival MiniMax fell 5% in the aftermath.

**American wages are falling behind, and AI is increasingly suspected**

The angle is counterintuitive: AI would not destroy jobs, it would crush wages. A study by Apollo Global Management authored by Torsten Slok and Sania Edlich shows that occupations highly exposed to AI have seen their real wages rise **6.7 percentage points more slowly since 2023**, with no measurable statistical effect on employment. At the same time, the share of labor in nonfarm output falls to **52.8%**, its lowest level since 1947, and real wages declined 0.4% year-on-year through June. Economists are calling for caution, as the data series are still very short.

**Electric flying taxis begin their trials in Texas**

Texas is opening the first phase of the federal eIPP pilot program dedicated to electric, hybrid and autonomous aircraft. **Joby, Beta and Wisk** are carrying out a week of demonstration flights without passengers between cities and regional airports, notably to Dallas Fort Worth. A distinctive feature of the arrangement: testing before the rules exist, with the goal of collecting the data that will allow the FAA to write a certification that normally takes years. Seven other states will follow, while Joby and Archer fight in court over theft of trade secrets.

**"The contagion of fear": an engineer settles scores with AI alarmism**

Bryan Cantrill opens his essay with a confession: as a student, he had triggered a false computer virus alert at his university, and discovered with dread the speed at which fear spreads. He uses this memory to attack the current discourse on the risk of extinction linked to AI, which he deems irresponsible, naming in particular the statements of former Anthropic researcher **Jacob Coxon**. The text is generating a lot of reaction in the tech community, proof that the fault line does not run only between labs and governments.

**Obama urges Democrats to adopt a real plan on AI**

Asked Thursday by Hakeem Jeffries at a Democratic event, the former president said that AI should become one of the party's "central agendas," with "a very clear plan" on its economic effects and its risks. His diagnosis: the technology is advancing too fast **in private hands**, without a framework for public discussion. He says he has spoken with Dario Amodei and Sam Altman and offers himself as an interlocutor for industry leaders. Remarks reported by the *New York Times* from a partial transcript provided by his office.

**Jaron Lanier: "there is no AI, only humans"**

In an episode of StarTalk hosted by Neil deGrasse Tyson, the virtual reality pioneer defends a thesis that runs counter to the current moment: AI is not an autonomous creature but an **assemblage of human labor**, which mechanically relativizes all the discourse about existential risk. The exchange spills over into social media addiction and into "data dignity," his idea of paying the people whose data trains the systems. The full transcript is available.

**Tesla finally sets a date for its Roadster, nine years after the prototype**

The official reveal of the second-generation Roadster is set for **October 10**, if it is not postponed once again since the 2017 prototype. A countdown on the website hints at cold gas thrusters derived from the SpaceX pack, supposedly intended to allow the car to "fly," according to *The Information*. The original promises: **0 to 60 mph in under 2 seconds**, over 250 mph top speed, over 600 miles of range, starting at **$200,000**. Several $50,000 preorders have been canceled in the meantime, including those of Sam Altman and Marques Brownlee.

**Nvidia is now hanging on three clients**

The company's financial documents tell a story of runaway concentration: **three clients accounted for 44% of revenue** in the first half of the fiscal year ending in July, compared with two clients for 36% last year, and no client above 10% in 2023. This sheds a different light on Jensen Huang's strategy, as he multiplies investments in neoclouds and AI startups: it is less about conquering markets than about manufacturing other buyers before those few pull back.
