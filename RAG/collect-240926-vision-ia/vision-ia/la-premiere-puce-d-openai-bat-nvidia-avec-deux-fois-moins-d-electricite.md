OpenAI presented "Jalapeño" on Tuesday at the Hot Chips conference, its very first in-house accelerator, designed with Broadcom. SemiAnalysis's independent tests give it the win against Nvidia's GB200 and GB300: **1.5 to 1.9 times more throughput per kilowatt**, latency **1.7 to 3.6 times lower**, and **700 watts** of consumption versus **1,400 watts** for a GB300. The entire tech press picked up the information during the day.

**What Jalapeño actually does:**

- It's an **inference-only** chip: it runs already-trained models, it doesn't train any. It's optimized for the two real bottlenecks of LLMs, the prefill phase and communication between chips.

- SemiAnalysis benchmarks (InferenceX suite) on **GPT-OSS 120B, DeepSeek R1 670B and Kimi K2.5 1T**, with about **1,400 tokens per second** on GPT-OSS 120B.

- It handles **2.1 to 4.1 times more interactive load** than a GB200 or a GB300, with **50% more HBM memory per watt**.

- It didn't face Vera Rubin directly, but it exceeds the throughput-per-megawatt figures published in July by Nvidia and CoreWeave for Rubin.

- Design started mid-2024, frozen in November 2025. For now only **engineering samples** exist: first units late 2026, volume ramp-up in 2027.

A first-generation chip that beats the market leader almost never happens. "Usually, first-generation chips aren't competitive, and here OpenAI beats Nvidia's Blackwell and even Rubin," summarizes Dylan Patel, head of SemiAnalysis. You'll never buy a Jalapeño, but you'll see its effect: every watt saved on inference is less cost on OpenAI's products. The company is simultaneously maintaining its agreements with Nvidia, AMD and AWS, which mostly looks like a nice negotiating position.

OpenAI announces it has dismantled a network of Russian accounts that used ChatGPT to fuel a Western influence operation, built around an entirely fictitious think tank, the "International Burke Institute." Of the **36 articles** published by this institute between September 2025 and May 2026, **34 were plagiarized**.

**The key points:**

- The IBI website, registered in February 2025, claimed to be based in Israel and cited **Francis Fukuyama and Noam Chomsky** as intellectual endorsements, without the slightest real connection to them.

- The operators used **VPNs** to bypass OpenAI's geographic blocking. ChatGPT was used to write, translate, produce summaries in Russian for a second operator, and above all to erase the linguistic markers that betray a Russian pen.

- The institute published an in-house "sovereignty index," which unsurprisingly ranked Russia ahead of Western democracies.

- Distribution on X, LinkedIn, Facebook, Substack and Telegram, including a German-language channel named "Lahme Ente," hostile to the EU, Ukraine and the German government, with 10,000 to 20,000 subscribers.

- Target countries: Germany, United States, France, Poland, Turkey. OpenAI classifies the operation as **category 3 out of 6** on the Brookings scale: credible infrastructure, tiny real audience.

The cost of manufacturing propaganda has just dropped to zero, but the bottleneck hasn't budged an inch: you still need someone to read it. An operation capable of producing in five languages, with a fake institute, a fake tip and fake intellectual sponsors, ended up with an audience of a confidential blog. The most interesting detail remains this one: it wasn't a state or a social network that blew the final whistle, but the model provider, which sees the prompts go by.


Anthropic merged on Tuesday Claude's memory (the chat) with that of Claude Cowork, its office agent that handles documents, code and tasks. What you talk about in a conversation, the agent already knows at the moment it takes action. The rollout took place the same day, **on the Free, Pro and Max plans**, on web, desktop and mobile.

**In detail:**

- A single memory shared between the two products. Anthropic's example: you prepare a conference in the chat (number of participants, city, speakers), then Cowork drafts the agenda without you having to re-enter anything.

- The memory fills up **as the conversation goes**, and no longer via a summary at the end. You can therefore switch from the chat to the agent mid-way.

- Everything Claude has retained becomes **viewable, editable and deletable, topic by topic**.

- By default, Claude records neither health data, nor ethnic origin, nor gender identity, nor religion, nor political opinions. An "include sensitive topics" setting reactivates them, and the app warns on every sensitive recording. Never stored, however: identity documents, social security numbers, criminal records, immigration status.

- The limit: it's impossible to partition the chat's memory from Cowork's, unless you open two separate accounts. Claude Code, for now, keeps its memory separate. App update required on iOS and Android.

Permanent rebriefing is what makes most agents painful on a daily basis: you spend more time re-explaining the context than retrieving finished work. It's also the right moment to go look closely at this memory setting, because a single memory means a single file on you, which now follows the assistant everywhere. At least it's finally readable and erasable line by line.


SpaceX has filed with the FCC a dossier named Starmind: a constellation of data centers in low orbit (500 to 2,000 km) that could reach **one million satellites**. Each unit carries **72 Nvidia chips**, the equivalent of a complete server rack, and the first unit, AI1, is targeting a launch in the last quarter of 2027.

**A few key figures:**

- AI1 runs on the **Vera Rubin NVL72 "Space-1"** platform, Rubin GPU plus Vera CPU. Nvidia claims up to **25 times** the AI compute of an H100.

- Sun-synchronous orbit, facing the Sun **98% of the time**. The solar panels produce around **210 kW**, for **175 kW** of average compute power per satellite.

- Heat escapes into the vacuum through liquid radiators of around **1,700 square feet** (nearly 160 m²), the most fragile point of the dossier.

- The results come back down via **laser links** relayed by Starlink. Stated goal: free xAI from the electrical grid, land and terrestrial cooling constraints, and train Grok 5.

- At this stage, it's a filed dossier, not an authorization: no FCC decision, no public budget. Eventually, the project is to rely on Terafab, the chip factory at **119 billion dollars** set up with Tesla.

The irony is neat: the chip that Jalapeño outclasses on paper goes off to conquer orbit. Musk calls the NVL72 the "best architecture" available, but the specialists interviewed by Engadget point out that embedding the most recent and most power-hungry architecture is precisely the hardest way to start: thermal dissipation, cost, radiation, and zero possibility of going to replace a faulty card. The 2027 timeline is to be taken for what it is, a Musk timeline.

# 🧠 **RESEARCH**

**An mRNA adjuvant that melts tumors in mice**

Anti-cancer vaccines often trigger an immune response that is too weak, and boosting it with cytokines causes severe side effects. The team of Daniel Anderson (MIT), with Harvard and the University of Houston, designed an mRNA adjuvant encoding two genes that activate immune cells through other pathways. In mice, it slows or eliminates bladder, colon, lung and melanoma cancers, alone or combined with a targeted vaccine or checkpoint inhibitors. Combined with flu and covid vaccines, it multiplies the T-cell response by 10 to 15.

**Four weeks of chatbot, and you spot fake news less well**

The MIT Media Lab had pairs of headlines and images evaluated over a month. Assisted by a chatbot, participants were initially 21% more accurate at distinguishing true from false. By the fourth week, deprived of AI, they were 15% worse than before the experiment, while a quarter of them declared themselves on the contrary sharper. AIs that ask questions instead of giving the answer clearly limit this effect, at the cost of speed.

**A model compressed to 4 bits that beats its original version**

Multiverse Computing presents Quantization-Aware Healing, a repair method for models that have undergone structural compression (removal of layers, heads, neurons) in addition to quantization, a case that conventional techniques handle poorly. Result on a GPT-OSS 120B pruned to 60B then quantized to 4 bits: the final model outperforms the bfloat16 version it came from on 7 out of 9 benchmarks, including reasoning, math, and code. In other words, a smaller model, cheaper to run, and yet better.

**An MIT AI invents weather disasters that never happened**

Current risk models can only replay what is already in the archives. The Extreme Event Aware method (η-learning), by Kai Chang and Professor Themis Sapsis, generates maps of extreme events that are statistically possible but never observed in a given region, with an estimate of duration, intensity, and affected area. Published in Nature Communications on August 20, it targets insurers, urban planners, and power grid managers who must anticipate a "hundred-year Katrina."

**A sonar to pilot a robot in murky water**

When an underwater vehicle lands or digs, it stirs up a cloud of sediment and its cameras go blind. Amy Phung and Richard Camilli, of the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, first have the environment mapped by sonar, whose resolution is low but which works as well in clear water as in murky water, then bring the craft close enough for the cameras to take over. An image-matching algorithm estimates the depth of each pixel to keep up with real time. Intended applications: exploration, underwater maintenance, and demining.

**Faraday reproduces scientific papers with 27 billion parameters**

Inherent, a London laboratory founded by former Google DeepMind employees, claims that its agent Faraday outperforms Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 on a specific task: independently rediscovering the results of a published study, without knowing the answer in advance, the equivalent of a standard doctoral exercise. All on Qwen 3.6, a model with 27 billion parameters, far smaller than its competitors. Inherent's bet is on reinforcement learning, to create a researcher's "instinct" rather than follow rules.

**Detecting an AI's lies: EleutherAI's assessment**

EleutherAI publishes its retrospective on Aletheia's Quest, a month-long competition organized by Cadenza Labs and the National Deep Inference Fabric. Finishing 2nd out of 19, the team tested hundreds of detector variants on more than 30 datasets and three families of open-weights models ranging from 27B to 120B. Main surprise: black-box methods, which observe only the outputs, often rival white-box methods that read internal activations, the latter proving highly context-dependent. The datasets are released.

**Ukraine opens its five million combat images to the British**

The United Kingdom becomes the first country to gain access to Avengers Labs, the Ukrainian platform that gathers around five million annotated combat images, raw material for training military AIs. Three British startups are already running pilot projects. The agreement marks a quiet shift: real wartime data is becoming a diplomatic bargaining chip for developing autonomous weaponry.

**Students prefer Gemini for writing their essays**

Out of 6,851 blind votes collected by StudyArena, Gemini wins for writing with 39.6% of preferences, ahead of Claude (31.8%) and ChatGPT (29.2%). Two takeaways more useful than the ranking: long answers win significantly more often (37% longer on average), and increasing the reasoning setting does not improve writing quality. The authors' advice: use AI as a proofreader, to hunt down generic passages and missing details, and write the final text yourself.


# **🗞️MORE NEWS**

**Chinese hackers double their pace thanks to DeepSeek**

Groups linked to the Chinese state carry out more than twice as many attacks since they integrated open source models into their tools, reveals Taiwanese company TeamT5 in a report relayed by Bloomberg. Their model of choice is DeepSeek, for two very prosaic reasons: it costs almost nothing and its guardrails are particularly lax. Researchers have, however, never yet encountered Kimi K3, which is more expensive, in an attack. The downside of open source that people rarely think about when installing a model at home.

**ChatGPT's task scheduling arrives on free accounts**

Until now reserved for Plus, Pro, Business, and Enterprise subscriptions, ChatGPT's scheduling menu is opening up to everyone. Concretely, you schedule a prompt that triggers itself at the time or pace of your choice (morning news roundup, weekly follow-up, Friday recap), and you can now share a scheduled task with other users. Paid accounts additionally gain event-based triggers: a prompt that fires when something changes in Gmail, Slack, or GitHub.

**OpenAI reinstates the 5-hour limit on Codex for Plus subscribers**

After several weeks of a purely weekly quota, OpenAI restored on August 25 a usage limit per 5-hour block on Codex and ChatGPT Work for Plus accounts. The official argument from Thibault Sottiaux, head of engineering for Codex: smooth the load on the servers, and prevent an occasional user from emptying their weekly quota in a single session without realizing it. The Pro plans at $100 and $200 remain exempt for the coming months. The subject is generating a lot of reaction among heavy users.

**Google buys 34 years of Spirit Airlines data, flight attendants protest**

The American low-cost airline went bankrupt in the spring, but its archives are still worth something: Google won the auction at $10 million (against $7.5 for Mercor) for 34 years of invoices, flight operations, wifi sales, personnel files, and crew schedules. Google assures that customer data is excluded and that no personal information will be transmitted to it. The AFA union, which represents 5,500 former crew members, has filed an objection: employee records are being transferred without consent, and pretty much nothing in American law prohibits it.

**Meta launches Hatch, its paid agent at up to $199.99 per month**

According to internal documents obtained by The Information, Meta will release in the coming weeks Hatch, the paid consumer version of its OpenClaw agent, with tiered pricing that climbs up to $199.99 per month. The agent connects to DoorDash, Etsy, Reddit, Yelp, or Outlook and offers a customizable dashboard, from fitness tracking to travel planning. A new model dubbed Watermelon is expected in October. Meta is also preparing a platform on WhatsApp allowing third-party agents to be connected to it.

**Waymo will launch its robotaxis in Munich in 2027**

The Alphabet subsidiary is starting phased testing in Munich in the coming weeks, with a public opening planned for late 2027. It is its third city outside the United States after London and Tokyo, and the first in the European Union. Waymo already operates in 11 American cities, has announced 19 more, and claims nearly 221 million miles driven in autonomous mode. The first real European regulatory test of the consumer robotaxi.

**Einride deploys 500 Tesla Semis for Amazon**

The Swedish company Einride will run 500 Tesla Semi electric trucks driven by its Saga AI optimization platform, for Amazon and other North American customers. It is the largest deployment of Tesla Semi in the world, third-party financed, spread over 24 months starting in September 2026 and distributed across five American states. The operation triples the electric fleet deployed by Einride, whose platform already claims more than 19 million optimized electric miles.

**BlackBerry, which has quietly become a robotics player**

The former king of the keyboard smartphone now makes its living with QNX, its embedded system present in 275 million vehicles, and its CEO John Giamatteo tells CNBC that robotics is one of its most dynamic segments, on an order book of about $950 million. The target is not the humanoid but industrial physical AI: warehouses, autonomous forklifts, medical instruments. An expanded partnership with Nvidia was announced in April to bring QNX into these markets.

**A voice bracelet for seniors born from an MIT AgeLab study**

Having come to take part in an MIT AgeLab study on technologies to assist the elderly, Don Yansen mostly heard participants recount how unusable smartphones and smartwatches are for them. He founded Kiwi Health in October 2024 to offer a screenless, voice-controlled bracelet whose AI is trained to understand vocal variations related to age. The device measures health parameters, detects falls, and automatically alerts caregivers.

**Applying has become so easy that no one can recruit anymore**

Three years ago, Andrew Stockwell, head of HR at Vendr, received a few dozen applications per listing, a hundred on good days. Today, recruiters take in more than a thousand, boosted by generated resumes, optimized cover letters, and auto-fill extensions. The result is paradoxical: everything looks the same, much is exaggerated or fake, and spotting a good profile becomes almost impossible. Platforms like Greenhouse are now considering deliberately reintroducing friction into the process.

**Is agentic AI just automation in disguise?**

A consultant applies a simple test to the agent trend: does the system, during execution, make a decision that no one had anticipated at the outset? He then walks through a detailed use case, a maintenance agent on an Airbus A320 that diagnoses a fault, queries documentary databases, and recommends a repair, only to find that the whole thing fits into a classic flowchart. A salutary read before signing an "agentic" quote.

**Successful agent projects are the ones that rein in their agents**

Gartner predicts that more than 40% of ongoing agentic AI projects will be abandoned by 2028, weighed down by costs, unclear return on investment, and insufficient risk controls. The McKinsey 2026 survey points in the same direction, with average maturity in AI governance at only 2.3 out of 4, and 30% of organizations at a level deemed sufficient. The underlying reason is mechanical: the more autonomous an agent is, the less traceable its decisions are after the fact, which becomes disqualifying in financial compliance or industrial quality control.

**OpenAI loses its head of data centers**

Chris Malone, who had been leading OpenAI's data center strategy since March 2025, left the company last week, with no official reason given. Having previously worked at Meta and then Google, he arrived shortly after the launch of the Stargate project. According to Business Insider, this is the thirteenth senior executive departure at OpenAI in 2026, following those of former COO Brad Lightcap and chief product officer Fidji Simo. Three other executives continue to oversee the infrastructure portfolio, now under the responsibility of Sachin Katti.

**SpaceX announces a $100 billion spaceport in Louisiana**

The site, located in Vermilion Parish, is to become the primary launch base for Starship and "support thousands of launches per year" according to the Louisiana economic development agency. Musk promises "probably 10,000 exciting jobs." The announcement comes as SpaceX faces lawsuits and environmental fines at its Texas site in Boca Chica, which promises lively local debates.

**Stability AI raises $76 million from the entertainment industry**

The company behind Stable Diffusion closes a $76 million Series B, bringing its total funding to $232 million. The interest of the round lies less in the amount than in its composition: Universal Music Group, Sony Music Group, Warner Music Group and Electronic Arts, with which Stability already has content licensing agreements, alongside AMD Ventures and Pacific Alliance Ventures. After years of lawsuits between rights holders and image generators, the majors are moving from the plaintiffs' camp to the shareholders' camp. The funds will go to the creative production suite for image, music and video.
