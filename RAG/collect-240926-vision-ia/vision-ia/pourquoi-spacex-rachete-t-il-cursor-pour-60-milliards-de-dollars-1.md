No one saw it coming. SpaceX announced this morning the acquisition of **Cursor**, the AI-powered code editor that has become developers' darling, for **$60 billion** paid entirely **in shares**. The deal comes just days after Cursor's blockbuster IPO, and the entire tech press is already talking about it.

Key takeaways:

- An XXL amount: **$60 billion**, paid **100% in SpaceX shares**

- Spectacular timing: the announcement comes **just days after** Cursor's **IPO**

- The official motive: beefing up **SpaceX's AI division, deemed to be struggling**

- The underlying bet: SpaceX claims to be targeting an addressable market of **$26 trillion** in AI

- A media firestorm: the story led coverage on **TechCrunch, The Verge, and Hacker News**

Why it matters: that a space player would shell out such a sum for a software development tool says a lot about the current fever. Tech giants are willing to pay top dollar to avoid missing the AI train. If the deal goes through, it reshuffles the deck in the developer tools market and propels SpaceX, already sprawling, to the center of the AI ecosystem. One nagging question remains: can a "struggling" AI division really be fixed with tens of billions of dollars, or is it mostly buying time against rivals who are moving fast?

Anthropic finds itself in court. The company is the target of a lawsuit over the **usage limits imposed on Claude Max subscribers**, its premium offering. At issue: the commercial terms and service restrictions applied to the heaviest users, the very ones paying the most.

The key points:

- The lawsuit directly targets **Anthropic** and its premium plan **Claude Max**

- The heart of the dispute: the **usage limits** applied to a subscription presented as premium

- The underlying issue: **transparency of the terms** of AI subscriptions

What it changes: usage caps have become a recurring friction point for power users, who sign up for an expensive plan expecting near-unlimited access, only to hit limits that aren't always clearly disclosed. The issue goes far beyond Anthropic: most AI providers juggle shifting quotas to control colossal computing costs. If the court sided with the plaintiffs, the entire "unlimited, up to a point" AI subscription model would need to become clearer, with more explicit terms about what the customer is actually buying. A case to watch, as it could set a precedent for the entire industry.

The figure is dizzying. Over the last fiscal year, OpenAI spent **$34 billion**, far more than the previous year. A sum that, on its own, illustrates the intensity and cost of the race for cutting-edge AI.

A few key figures:

- Spending over the year: **$34 billion**

- Trend: **sharply up** compared with the previous fiscal year

- Main items: **infrastructure**, computing power, and model development

Why it matters: training and running cutting-edge models costs a fortune, between buying chips, renting data centers, and paying the salaries of highly sought-after engineers. This level of spending tells the story of a deliberate strategy: build fast and big to keep the advantage, even at the cost of deepening losses. But it also raises the question of sustainability: how long can such a pace last, and by when will these investments need to translate into commensurate revenue? OpenAI is not alone in this situation; all the major labs are injecting record amounts, but this figure is a reminder that generative AI remains, for now, an industry that consumes far more cash than it produces. Enough to fuel the debate about a possible bubble.

Meta continues to infuse AI everywhere. Facebook is rolling out **starting today** a new search option called **"AI Mode"**, which no longer returns simple links but answers generated from the **public content posted on Meta's platforms**.

In detail:

- "AI Mode" appears **alongside the usual modes** ("People", "Marketplace") in Facebook search

- The answers rely on Meta users' **public posts**, not just links

- Ability to ask **follow-up questions**, as in Meta's new Forum app

- Rolling out **starting today**, accompanied by other AI new features (photo presets, collage suggestions)

- Worth reading: Meta's official announcement

The impact to remember: by turning the public posts of billions of users into raw material for its answers, Meta is seeking to catch up with Google and conversational engines on the search front. For the user, it's more convenient: you ask a question, you get a summary rather than a list of links. But it also raises a question about the use of our content: what you post publicly now feeds an AI, and shapes what others see when they search. One more step in Meta's strategy to make AI unavoidable at the heart of its consumer applications.

**A man paralyzed by ALS becomes the first "power user" of a brain implant for speech**

Casey Harrell, afflicted with ALS and paralyzed, has been using for nearly **3 years** a brain-computer interface that transforms his neural activity into speech. In 22 months, he has accumulated more than **3,800 hours** of use, largely independently, demonstrating for the first time the reliability of these implants in daily life. The study, published in **Nature Medicine**, relies on an algorithm capable of decoding the **39 phonemes** of English.

**David Sinclair launches human trials to reverse aging of the entire body**

Harvard researcher David Sinclair will test on volunteers **SL-100**, an oral drug that promises to reverse aging not of a single organ, but of the **entire body**. The molecule uses **chemical reprogramming** to mimic embryonic genes and circulates through the blood, unlike conventional gene therapies. Stated goal: to win the **$101 million XPrize for rejuvenation**.

**Stanford's AI jobs index reframes the panic over layoffs**

Despite **40,000 layoffs** in tech in May 2026 (a two-year record), new data from the **Stanford Digital Economy Lab**, drawn from **25,000 companies**, shows that AI has not triggered a general wave. It mainly hits certain profiles, and often serves as a **convenient pretext** for overstaffed companies to scale back.
