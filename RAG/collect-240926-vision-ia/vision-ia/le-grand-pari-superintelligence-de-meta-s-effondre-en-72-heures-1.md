Zuckerberg had made it the showcase of his ambition for a "personal superintelligence." Three days were enough to pack it all up. According to TechCrunch, Meta removed **Muse Image**, the very first product from its **Superintelligence Labs**, barely **72 hours** after its launch. Deployed on Instagram on **July 8**, the feature allowed AI filters to be applied to any photo with a single tap, including images posted by other people. And it was **enabled by default**.

Launched on **July 8**, Muse Image was removed after only **72 hours**
It transformed **any photo** with a tap, including those posted by other users, without explicit consent
The actors' union **SAG-AFTRA** condemned the tool for its obvious potential for abuse
It was the **first product** to come out of Superintelligence Labs, Zuckerberg's flagship division
Meta eventually acknowledged that the feature "had missed its target"

What this changes: for a division meant to embody the company's future, a withdrawal in three days looks like a scathing disavowal. The problem wasn't the technical feat, but an elementary blind spot: enabling by default a tool that lets others' photos be retouched. The episode sums up the tension of the moment, between the dizzying promises of "superintelligence" touted by the labs and products that stumble over consent issues a intern could have anticipated. For Zuckerberg, who invested colossal sums and poached at gold-plated prices to build these labs, the first contact with the public turns into a fiasco. And it serves as a reminder that in the era of generative filters, the real red line isn't what AI can do, but what it allows itself to do without asking.

It's an internal letter, translated and attributed to **Zhipu AI** founder Tang Jie, that has set things ablaze. The document reveals a new strategy dubbed **"Touch High"**: concentrating the bulk of investments over the **next two years** on artificial general intelligence, rather than on short-term commercial revenue. Going against the monetization race driving most labs, one of China's AI champions chooses to aim for the summit, even at the cost of immediate profitability.

The four pillars of the strategy:

AI systems capable of accomplishing **extremely long and complex tasks**
**Groups of autonomous agents** working in concert
Systems capable of **training and improving themselves** (self-improvement)
A **strengthening of safety** as capabilities increase

The context: since the letter is a leak, caution is still warranted regarding the details. But the signal itself is clear. While Meta stumbles over an Instagram filter and American giants trade barbs through press releases, a Chinese player lays out a two-year roadmap entirely focused on AGI. The choice of the four pillars is telling: long tasks, autonomous agents, and above all self-improvement outline the trajectory the entire sector whispers about without always publicly owning it. If Zhipu holds this course, the race to AGI won't be played out only between San Francisco and London. The trillion-dollar question remains: declaring ambition is one thing, delivering is another, and two years pass quickly.

The takeaway: the most striking thing is the wide gap between the proclaimed urgency and the available data. On one side, sixteen Nobel laureates speaking of a window that is closing; on the other, work that still measures no massive upheaval in employment. One can see in this a salutary precautionary principle, or yet another communication exercise. But one fact deserves to be noted: seeing OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic, fierce competitors on every front, sign the same text is rare enough to be highlighted. When those three agree on a message, it generally means it is worth listening to.

Why it matters: behind the ego clash lies a real industrial question. The explosion in compute needs is pushing giants to imagine increasingly exotic infrastructures, from dedicated nuclear power to servers in orbit. Distinguishing serious engineering from the appealing slide deck designed to raise funds is becoming a daily exercise for anyone financing this sector. And when two of the most influential figures in AI publicly spar over the feasibility of a project, it is also a signal: the race for computing power has become so tense that it now plays out as much in press releases as in server rooms.
