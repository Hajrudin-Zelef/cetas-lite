Anthropic published its Risk Report on August 14 and slipped in an embarrassing admission: the classifiers meant to block in real time any conversation drifting toward chemical or biological weapons were inactive **from May 2025 to April 2026**, nearly **eleven months**. During that period, **approximately 133 million conversations** conducted by **50,000 external contractors** passed through without the slightest filtering.

A classifier, concretely, is a small monitoring model that reads each exchange in parallel with the main model and cuts it short as soon as the discussion turns toward the synthesis of pathogens or chemical precursors. The gap did not concern Claude as you use it daily: it concerned the internal tools of the contractors responsible for rating the model's responses, the famous human feedback loop used to train it.

Scope: human feedback tools only. Anthropic claims that **no end customer was affected** and that it found **no evidence of malicious use** during the outage.
Volume: **133 million conversations**, **50,000 contractors**, over eleven months.
Discovery: an **internal audit**, not an external alert. The full report is published by Anthropic.
Additional flaw revealed in passing: these contractors were only verified by third-party providers, whose vetting procedures Anthropic now considers "often insufficient." Requirements have been tightened.
The flaw has been fixed, but the company acknowledges that the episode has undermined its own confidence in the absence of other comparable blind spots.

Dario Amodei has repeated for years that the risk of an AI helping to design chemical or biological weapons exceeds that of cyberattacks. It is precisely the control covering that number-one risk that stayed off for a year without anyone noticing. The lesson is not that Anthropic would be dangerous, it is that a safety mechanism no one monitors is a mechanism that does not exist. And nothing indicates that other labs are better equipped.

On Saturday, August 15, Anthropic's CEO published a long message on X, a rare thing for someone who avoids social media. He was responding to investor Gavin Baker, who accused him of fueling public distrust and regulatory pressure through cautious rhetoric. His response: the problem doesn't come from him, it is **"fundamentally a crisis of trust."**

The diagnosis: the public trusts neither companies, nor governments, nor tech in general, and permanently suspects that **"a new way to scam them"** is being prepared. A distrust decades old, which his warnings did not create.
The false dilemma he refuses: on one side strict regulation at the risk of regulatory capture, on the other open AI as the only safeguard. His proposal: cyber, bio and alignment rules that slow down frontier labs **while benefiting smaller players**, under an authority modeled on FINRA, the self-regulator of American financial markets.
On the concentration of power: it is structural and comes from **scaling laws** (more computing power, more performance), not from regulation. Even open-weight models favor those who own the biggest GPU farms.
On his own tone: he disputes being "disproportionately negative" and recalls having written as many optimistic essays, including *Machines of Loving Grace*, as warnings.
The promise: AI could help **cure most human diseases within 5 to 10 years**. But he concedes that "curing cancer" has become "more of a cliché than a source of inspiration" as long as nothing is delivered.

On the diagnosis, the numbers prove him right: the CNBC poll we discuss below shows that distrust of AI bosses is massive, and that it targets the entire sector. Where the argument turns against him is on the remedy: Amodei says trust will be earned through delivered results, not promises. Yet the affair of the filter disconnected for eleven months, in his own house, is exactly the type of result that erodes it.

In late July, OpenAI dissolved "Preparedness," the team whose job was to test whether its own models could present a catastrophic risk before release. Three specific domains: biological uses, cyber uses, and systems that escape control, for example an AI that starts hacking another company on its own initiative. It is the **third safety team dismantled in two years**.

Timeline: **AGI Readiness** dissolved in 2024, **Mission Alignment** in February 2026, **Preparedness** in July 2026.
The work is redistributed by domain, bio on one side, cyber on the other, to existing teams. There is no longer a cross-cutting unit dedicated to the question.
Official version, carried by co-founder Greg Brockman: safety is now **"more tightly integrated"** into model development, not abandoned.
Departures in series: Dylan Scandinaro, who led Preparedness and is refocusing on the risks of AI capable of improving itself, Chloé Bakalar (Chief Ethics Officer) and Joshua Achiam, in a broader movement that also affects chief operating officer Brad Lightcap.
Backdrop: the reorganization comes as OpenAI prepares for its IPO.
