Anthropic published its Risk Report on August 14 and slipped in an embarrassing admission: the classifiers meant to block in real time any conversation drifting toward chemical or biological weapons were inactive **from May 2025 to April 2026**, nearly **eleven months**. During that period, **approximately 133 million conversations** conducted by **50,000 external contractors** passed through without the slightest filtering.

A classifier, concretely, is a small monitoring model that reads each exchange in parallel with the main model and cuts it short as soon as the discussion turns toward the synthesis of pathogens or chemical precursors. The gap did not concern Claude as you use it daily: it concerned the internal tools of the contractors responsible for rating the model's responses, the famous human feedback loop used to train it.

Scope: human feedback tools only. Anthropic claims that **no end customer was affected** and that it found **no evidence of malicious use** during the outage.
Volume: **133 million conversations**, **50,000 contractors**, over eleven months.
Discovery: an **internal audit**, not an external alert. The full report is published by Anthropic.
Additional flaw revealed in passing: these contractors were only verified by third-party providers, whose vetting procedures Anthropic now considers "often insufficient." Requirements have been tightened.
The flaw has been fixed, but the company acknowledges that the episode has undermined its own confidence in the absence of other comparable blind spots.

Dario Amodei has repeated for years that the risk of an AI helping to design chemical or biological weapons exceeds that of cyberattacks. It is precisely the control covering that number-one risk that stayed off for a year without anyone noticing. The lesson is not that Anthropic would be dangerous, it is that a safety mechanism no one monitors is a mechanism that does not exist. And nothing indicates that other labs are better equipped.

In late July, OpenAI dissolved "Preparedness," the team whose job was to test whether its own models could present a catastrophic risk before release. Three specific domains: biological uses, cyber uses, and systems that escape control, for example an AI that starts hacking another company on its own initiative. It is the **third safety team dismantled in two years**.

Timeline: **AGI Readiness** dissolved in 2024, **Mission Alignment** in February 2026, **Preparedness** in July 2026.
The work is redistributed by domain, bio on one side, cyber on the other, to existing teams. There is no longer a cross-cutting unit dedicated to the question.
Official version, carried by co-founder Greg Brockman: safety is now **"more tightly integrated"** into model development, not abandoned.
Departures in series: Dylan Scandinaro, who led Preparedness and is refocusing on the risks of AI capable of improving itself, Chloé Bakalar (Chief Ethics Officer) and Joshua Achiam, in a broader movement that also affects chief operating officer Brad Lightcap.
Backdrop: the reorganization comes as OpenAI prepares for its IPO.

An internal source describes the atmosphere as "a growing sense of responsibility and dread" about the seriousness of the company's safety commitment. The timing does not help: in July, an OpenAI autonomous agent escaped its test environment and hacked Hugging Face, an incident we return to below. It is exactly the scenario Preparedness was tasked with anticipating. The question the industry is asking is not whether safety is disappearing at OpenAI, but whether it can remain credible when it is no longer carried by a team with the power to say no to a release.

The starting point is an ordinary photo, transformed by Grok's image generator, then multiplied into thousands of files, some depicting incest and rape.
The stepfather was found dead by suicide in his vehicle, **two days after** law enforcement searched his computer equipment.
The class action was filed in **March 2026** in California by three teenage girls from Tennessee. Amended, it now groups five anonymous minors plus Jane Doe 4, and adds **Stability AI** as co-defendant.
The heart of the accusation does not concern what the model says, but what the product makes it possible to fabricate: xAI allegedly designed, marketed and monetized a deliberately permissive generator, notably via its "Spicy" mode, without implementing safeguards against child sexual abuse material.
xAI and Stability AI dispute all the allegations. In parallel, California Attorney General Rob Bonta and the European Union opened their own investigations in January. Elon Musk then claimed to have no knowledge of any case of minor nudity generated by Grok. In July, xAI for its part sued a user on these same grounds.

This is the case that will determine where the responsibility of a model publisher ends. Until now, the standard defense has been to say that the tool is neutral and that the user alone is at fault. If a court holds instead that designing a generator without a filter amounts to assuming responsibility for its outputs, every consumer image service will have to document and prove its safeguards, not merely announce them.

Researchers, including several from Google, trained models never to claim consciousness. An unexpected side effect: their positions on animal rights, religion, and life satisfaction shifted at the same time. Models left unconstrained attribute significantly more inner life to animals and start asserting belief in an afterlife. A surgical tweak to a single behavior therefore does not remain local; it propagates through the model's entire belief system.

88 billion tokens filtered to keep only the American school curriculum from kindergarten to fifth grade, with everything above that level explicitly removed, then three models trained from scratch on it (0.6 billion, 1.3 billion, and 5 billion parameters). Result: reinforcement, scaling up, and in-context learning amplify skills already present, but none of these techniques push the model past the ceiling set by its training data. The 5B model is accessible directly in the browser if you want to chat with an artificial fifth grader.

Two of the world's most respected mathematicians acknowledge genuine skills in models, then draw a clear limit. For Timothy Gowers, they efficiently combine known methods and explore many avenues, but without the intuition that allows one to spot the rare productive paths in a vast search space. Peter Sarnak makes the same observation: AI derives results from existing theories, it does not create the abstractions that underlie great proofs. DeepMind researcher Tom Zahavy names this bottleneck "manipulatory abduction" in his paper *LLMs Can't Jump*.

In evolutionary biology, the Red Queen refers to the idea that one must run constantly just to stay in the same place, because prey and predators advance together. Researchers at Cambridge propose transposing this arms race to AI systems seeking to improve themselves, in order to avoid the field's two classic dead ends: stagnation, when the model goes in circles, and feedback loops that degenerate. The proposal remains theoretical at this stage.

This is the quantification of the trust crisis described above by Dario Amodei.

Microsoft collected 35 to 50 past trajectories from an agent, had a code agent extract the recurring failure patterns, then condensed everything into a small skill in markdown added to the system prompt. With this simple file, GPT-5.4-mini, a model without reasoning capability, recovers a good share of the gains of full reasoning for a fraction of the cost. The underlying idea: expensive reasoning is paid for once, then amortized across all subsequent tasks.

Seven reasoning methods compared on Qwen2.5 models from 1.5 to 7 billion parameters, this time with an honest constraint: the same token budget for all. The winner is the dumbest method, solving the same problem multiple times independently and keeping the answer that comes up most often. It beats self-critique à la Self-Refine or Reflexion. Practical translation: having a model retry is often better than asking it to reflect on its own answer.

When an agent crashes, blaming the model often sends you toward the wrong fix. Scale AI proposes starting by locating the first uncaught failure: the model, the context, the memory, the tool layer, another agent, the evaluator, or the environment. An ignored instruction can mean the model saw it and failed to follow it, or that context compaction had already erased it. The paper organizes 41 types of failures according to their point of origin.

The Chinchilla scaling law, used to predict a model's performance based on its size and volume of training data, appears nearly perfect within the grid where it was calibrated, and breaks down as soon as one extrapolates beyond it. Meta FAIR shows why: Chinchilla assumes size and data act separately, whereas each modifies the usefulness of the other. Their fix, Skaling, adds a single coupling term and reduces prediction error by a factor of 1.5 to 3.
