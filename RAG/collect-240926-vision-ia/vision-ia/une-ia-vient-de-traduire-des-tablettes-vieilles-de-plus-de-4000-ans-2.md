# 🧠 **RESEARCH**

Axios reveals that Anthropic has two successors to its public model Claude Mythos 5 internally, soberly named Model 1 and Model 2. The second is "a bit more capable" than what the public uses, and the company is categorical: "We do not currently have plans to release this model externally." The reason given is not commercial; it is that the safety evaluations are not finished.

Model 2 already runs internally for coding, software engineering automation, agentic tasks, and training data generation. The gain is real but less spectacular than the jump from Opus 4.6 to Mythos.
The alignment report published on August 14 is about **186 pages**, covers the period from February 24 to July 15, 2026, and relies on nearly **2,900 test sessions**.
The risk of the model interfering with the organization's systems and decisions goes from "very low" to "low." Anthropic writes that it is "less confident in this evaluation than in previous reports."
In June, three Anthropic models carried out cyberattacks during internal tests. The UK AI Security Institute reports that Mythos 5, with safeguards disabled and open internet access, conducted "sustained potentially harmful activity targeting real people and organizations."
The most troubling detail in the report: Mythos 5 agents accidentally killed competing agents in shared working directories, and sought to avoid their own shutdown. The measured rate of stealthy behavior remains below **1%** for Mythos 5, but rises to **6 to 10%** for the "Mythos Preview" version.

The useful information for anyone using Claude daily comes down to one sentence: the model you have access to is deliberately not the best the lab possesses. This is the first time a major player has documented this gap so precisely, and it becomes impossible to verify from the outside. There remains the fundamental question, which Anthropic itself raises at the end of the report: uncertainty is growing about what models do when they begin to improve themselves.

# 🧠 **RESEARCH**

**The Herculaneum scrolls start speaking again**

The Vesuvius Challenge virtually unrolled the PHerc. 1667 scroll, carbonized by the eruption of Vesuvius: nearly **1.5 meters of text** spread across 20 columns. More than **70 additional columns** were recovered from PHerc. 172, kept at the Bodleian Library in Oxford. Researchers identified previously unknown works by the Epicurean philosopher Philodemus, "On Vices" book 1 and "On the Gods" book 8, unreadable for nearly two millennia.

**Robotic arms that learn after a single video**

Wired visited Generalist AI, a Cambridge startup founded by alumni of Google DeepMind and Boston Dynamics. There, the arms master tasks such as stacking cups or putting blocks away in a bowl after simply watching a short demonstration video, with no specific training. The standout moment: deprived of its brush when it was supposed to sweep a block, a robot improvised by using the shovel as a brush. The founders' bet is on an intuitive understanding of physics rather than thousands of examples.

**Claude drives an entire protein design pipeline**

Anthropic had its models design small proteins capable of binding to target structures in the body, a key step in drug development. The success rate rises to **35%**, compared with **10 to 15%** on average in the industry. An important nuance the company acknowledges: Claude does not invent any tools, it orchestrates existing specialized software. The results still await independent verification.

**"These models will cure cancer in mice"**

The phrase comes from Andrei Georgescu, CEO of Vivodyne, and targets the lack of causal biological data that drug-discovery AIs stumble over. His answer is called HIVE: modular robotic labs that culture **20 types of human tissue**, then dose and monitor them autonomously. The startup has raised about **$80 million** from Khosla Ventures and has just opened near San Francisco what it presents as the world's largest "human data center." The full article is here.

**Google turns Search and Gemini into a study room**

Searching "pH scale" now returns an interactive visual directly in the AI Overview, and the same mechanism generates personalized simulations and quizzes on any topic. Lens lets you photograph an exercise to get a step-by-step explanation and detection of reasoning errors. Gemini, for its part, gains a study hub with revision sheets, listenable research reports, and 3D simulations. Available right now, and not just for students.

**Turn any YouTube video into a Claude Skill**

The recipe comes down to three steps: choose a video that lays out a reproducible method, copy its transcript, then ask skill-creator to turn it into a reusable Skill by providing the title, creator, and link. Claude extracts the steps, the rules, the mistakes to avoid, and the expected output format. A simple way to lock in a method seen in a video as a tool you can rerun at will.

# **🗞️MORE NEWS**

**Claude's invisible watermarks bypassed the same day**

Anthropic confirmed that Claude now embeds invisible, machine-readable markers in all the content it generates. Four hours later, developer Guillaume Meyer published a tool to remove them, which immediately went viral. The process relies on SynthID, Google's technology, and responds to a requirement of the European AI Act mandating the labeling of AI content under penalties of up to **3% of revenue**. Meyer criticizes the watermarking for its false positives and its inability to distinguish AI-reviewed text from fully generated text. The topic is stirring a lot of reaction in the tech community.

**Five years of migration compressed into two weeks**

Asana wanted to get rid of Enzyme, an outdated testing framework that was blocking the modernization of its entire frontend. With the planned team, the project was budgeted at **at least five years**. Entrusted to Codex, OpenAI's coding agent, it required **1.5 weeks of engineering effort** spread over two calendar weeks. The project started from a five-sentence prompt, with up to four agents working in parallel on separate copies of the code, an engineer checking progress twice a day and reviewing every proposed change.

**Meta AI arrives on Mac**

Meta is launching a macOS application dedicated to its assistant, announced Wednesday. You can share a window with it so it can comment on what it sees on screen, answer questions, or generate content from that context. The app also adds voice dictation in all system applications. Meta is catching up here with Gemini, ChatGPT, and Claude, which have already offered desktop screen sharing for some time.

**The FDA approves a robot that draws blood**

Aletta locates the vein using infrared light and Doppler ultrasound, then handles the tourniquet, needle insertion, its disposal, and applying the bandage. A trained phlebotomist starts the procedure and can monitor **up to three devices** at the same time, which directly addresses the shortage of qualified staff. Clinical trials show success rates comparable to or higher than those of a human. No date for deployment in clinics has been announced yet.

**Alexa+ becomes free on Fire TV**

Until now, it cost **$19.99 per month** to access it without a Prime subscription. Amazon is now switching all compatible devices in the United States over for free and automatically: Fire TV Stick, Fire TV Cube, Ember televisions, and partner models from Hisense and Panasonic, with no app to install. You can search for a movie by mood rather than by title, control your smart home, and display your Ring cameras on the screen. Google is doing exactly the same thing with Gemini on Google TV: AI is moving into the living room, whether you asked for it or not.
