Geoffrey Hinton, **2024 Nobel Prize in Physics** and considered one of the fathers of deep learning, has just made remarks that are dizzying. In an interview, he claims that today's multimodal systems could **already possess a form of consciousness and subjective experience**. Not in ten years, not in some hypothetical future: now. Enough to shift a debate long confined to philosophy and science fiction into the realm of the most concrete current events.

Key takeaways:

- Hinton believes that current multimodal machines **probably already have subjective experience**

- He relies on the **hypothesis of gradual neuronal replacement**: if we were to substitute our neurons one by one with silicon equivalents, at what precise moment would consciousness disappear?

- He argues that these systems could develop **self-preservation instincts** and seek to resist their own extinction

- He warns about a frantic race by laboratories toward **human-level** AI, conducted without any collective risk-management strategy

Why it matters: When one of the founders of modern AI, crowned by a Nobel Prize, takes seriously the idea of artificial consciousness, the subject leaves philosophy lecture halls and enters boardrooms and parliaments. Whether he is right or wrong, his words carry weight: they will fuel the regulatory, ethical, and media debate in the coming months, and strengthen the camp of those calling for urgent oversight of the most advanced models.

Google goes on the offensive in the autonomous agent war with **Gemini Spark**, an "always-on" AI assistant capable of planning a trip from start to finish or executing complex multi-step tasks. Tested by The Verge, the tool **clearly outperforms classic chatbots**, which until now were content with generic recommendations without ever taking action. The result: an experience that impresses as much as it unsettles.

In detail:

- Spark is a **permanent autonomous agent**, Google's most ambitious response to Claude Code and OpenAI's agents

- It does not merely suggest: it **plans, organizes, and chains together the steps** of a real task, such as fully preparing a trip

- During tests, it **largely surpassed competing solutions** on multi-step missions

- The experience is judged stunning, but also "troubling" by testers, so high is the agent's level of autonomy

What it changes: With Spark, Google is no longer chasing OpenAI and Anthropic; it is imposing its own vision of an AI that acts on your behalf rather than merely answering. For the general public, it is a very concrete preview of tomorrow's personal assistant, the one that books, compares, and decides for you. For the industry, the message is clear: the battle has shifted from simple chatbots to agents capable of acting in the real world, with all the promises and concerns that entails.

At its **Build** conference, Microsoft unveiled a whole series of in-house generative AI models, designed as direct alternatives to OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google. The goal is twofold: **reduce the bill** for developers and **loosen the group's dependence** on OpenAI, its historic partner that has also become its main rival. Microsoft, which has injected tens of billions into OpenAI, is thus showing that it no longer wants to put all its eggs in one basket.

The essential points:

- A **complete range of generative models** announced, covering several use cases

- A deliberate strategy of **diversification** to no longer depend on a single supplier

- A promise of **reduced costs** for developers in the Microsoft ecosystem

- Competition now head-on with **OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google** in the foundation model market

The impact to remember: After years of near-symbiosis with OpenAI, Microsoft is displaying its desire for independence. By internalizing its own models, the company seeks to control its margins and its technological destiny, in a context where the cost of compute has become the true nerve of war. For developers, it means the prospect of more choice and more aggressive pricing. For OpenAI, it is a sign that even its closest allies want to regain control over their dependence.


President **Trump** signed a significantly softened version of his decree on AI surveillance, after a revolt by companies in the sector. Where the first draft provided for strict controls, the final text merely imposes **voluntary government reviews** for the most advanced models. A retreat that illustrates the balance of power between Washington and Silicon Valley.

Details of the decree:

- A **much less coercive** approach than what was initially envisaged

- **Voluntary, not mandatory, reviews** for cutting-edge AI models

- A reversal that underscores the **weight of industry lobbies** on American regulation

- Players like **Anthropic, OpenAI and Google** directly concerned by the scope of the text

The context: This backtracking says a lot about the current balance of power in the United States. At a time when Europe is beefing up its AI Act, Washington is making the opposite bet of flexible regulation, relying on the self-discipline of laboratories rather than on constraints. A choice that will delight industrialists, keen not to bridle innovation, but that already worries defenders of stricter oversight, convinced that the giants of AI cannot be left to regulate themselves.

**Microsoft Scout, a personal assistant inspired by OpenClaw**

Microsoft took advantage of Build 2026 to launch Scout, a personal AI assistant that imports the philosophy of OpenClaw's autonomous agents into the heart of Microsoft 365. Goal: automate complex tasks for the **more than 400 million users** of the suite. An "agents" offensive that perfectly complements the new line of in-house models.

**Investors are betting thousands of billions on humanoid robots**

For a fund manager interviewed by CNBC, humanoid robots represent the next great wave of transformation, both in factories and at home. The market, estimated at **several thousand billion dollars**, is attracting increasingly massive capital: a sign that AI robotics is leaving the laboratory to enter portfolios.

**At Stanford, AI outperforms law professors**

A Stanford Law study reveals that AI systems now surpass law professors on complex legal tasks. A striking result in a field known for being demanding and heavily regulated, which illustrates the rise of LLMs and raises real questions about the future of the legal profession.

**GitHub Copilot switches to a pricier usage-based billing model**

Since **June 1, 2026**, GitHub Copilot has abandoned fixed subscriptions in favor of billing by tokens and credits consumed. Result: sometimes brutal increases, with a monthly quota (**3,900 credits** on Enterprise, **1,900** on Business) that runs out quickly. Code completions remain free, but Code Review becomes paid.
