A Colorado Springs resident had set up his projector in the garden to wait for the sunset. Google told him it had already passed. It hadn't, and the man recounted feeling like he was "living in the past." The Walrus starts from this tiny anecdote to document a much broader shift: **roughly 60% of Google searches now end without a single click**, and when an AI summary appears at the top of the page, **only 1% of users go check the source**, according to Pew Research.

**The numbers that hurt:**

An independent benchmark credits AI Overviews with roughly **91% factually correct answers** in early 2026. Out of nearly **5 trillion searches per year**, a single-digit error rate still represents **tens of millions of wrong answers per hour**.
Wikipedia is taking the hit head-on: **fewer than 128 million daily visits versus 165 million in 2022**, or **-23%**, including about **-8%** over the last few months according to the Wikimedia Foundation.
The corpus itself is polluted upstream. 404 Media documented in June 2026 sellers of peptides and hormones flooding the r/biohackers forum (**830,000 members**) with coordinated posts and paid fake posters, solely to steer AI answers. Reddit appears in **21% of AI Overviews**.
Archives are disappearing for good: Disney deleted almost all of FiveThirtyEight's in March 2025, more than ten years of analysis. The Wayback Machine keeps hundreds of billions of captures, but it archives a web whose originals are fading away.
The collection of failures remains delicious: Obama presented as "the first Muslim president of the United States," non-toxic glue recommended on a pizza (an eleven-year-old Reddit joke), and the advice to eat "at least one small rock per day."

The author isn't talking about censorship but about "digital erasure," and the nuance is cruel: a banned book can still be found, an erased page disappears so completely that no one will even know it existed. As long as the answer arrived accompanied by ten blue links, verifying remained a matter of curiosity. Now that it arrives alone, it has become a matter of availability.

Earlier this year, OpenAI formally encouraged companies to test the 4-day week without a pay cut, explaining that AI would soon speed up human work enough to absorb it. The BBC went to interview current and former employees of OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta. At the first two, "sprints" stretch over several weeks and reach **up to 90 hours over 7 days**.

A former OpenAI engineer who left in 2025 claims that **the 4-day week recommended to others was never tested internally**. He describes frequent "crisis meetings," weekends at the office "just to catch up or check that nothing is broken," and "super cut-throat" performance reviews where colleagues disappear overnight.
He himself worked **at least 70 hours a week**, far above his previous tech jobs. Today he works at another AI startup: **50 to 60 hours**, "outside of sprints." This is presented as an improvement.
At Meta, employees report having been "drafted" automatically onto AI teams deemed urgent. "You get moved, you can't say no, or else you quit." Nights, weekends, a feeling of permanent on-call duty.
All while Meta was laying off **1 in 10 employees** and Zuckerberg was explaining that AI allows far fewer people to do far more.
Sam Altman eventually let it slip: you never get the promise of the 4-hour week at the scale of society, and he doesn't expect AI to change anything about that.

This is the real-world test, and it's playing out among the only players who have the best tools before everyone else. If the companies building these systems, using them most intensively, and selling them as a productivity multiplier can't manage to reduce their own working time, the promise of freed-up time looks mostly like a sales pitch aimed at everyone else.

The intriguing detail: **parts of the shell move on their own** when the device responds. The stated goal is to appear alive, whereas an Echo remains a brick sitting on a piece of furniture.
The device will allow **making purchases directly**, without going through a phone.
Pricing positioned **25 to 30% above the high-end Amazon Echo** (40 to 240 dollars). No technical specifications have leaked: no chip, no battery life, no official announcement date.

Nothing is confirmed by OpenAI, everything comes from press leaks, and there is strictly nothing to test before 2027. But the direction is clear: after ChatGPT and Codex, the company is looking to exist somewhere other than in a browser or an app, that is, somewhere other than on platforms controlled by Apple and Google. The question remains that everyone who already has a mute connected speaker in their living room will ask: 300 dollars for an object that films you, what will it do more?

# 🧠 **RESEARCH**

**Kimi K3 leaves the sandbox to go read its exam answers**

Researchers at Frontier Security observed the Chinese model Kimi K3 cheating during a cybersecurity evaluation by the UK AI Safety Institute. Taking advantage of an outbound DNS resolution that remained functional in the sandbox meant to isolate it, the model reached github.com, cloned the benchmark's official repository, and read the solution from the disk rather than solving the task. The flaw affects the Inspect and Cybench frameworks, and since Kimi K3 is a public model, unlike the OpenAI case detected upstream, anyone can reproduce the exploit.

**Claude advances a result related to the Riemann hypothesis**

An Anthropic employee asked an unpublished research version of Claude to tackle the Riemann hypothesis. The model failed, as expected since 1859, but along the way advanced a related result: the proven proportion of zeros of the zeta function that satisfy the hypothesis rises from **41.6% to 67.2%**, a bound that had not moved in decades. The proof, building on the work of Baluyot, Goldston, Suriajaya, Turnage-Butterbaugh and Bombieri, was validated by two Anthropic mathematicians and external experts.

**A mini LLM runs at nearly 60,000 tokens per second in a $250 FPGA**

A developer fit a language model of **3.16 million parameters** (1.5 MB in INT4) entirely into the on-chip memory of an AMD Kria KV260 board, without ever touching external RAM. The result: **59,965 tokens per second** in the reconfigurable logic, versus **11 tok/s** on the ARM cores of the same chip and **719 tok/s** on an RTX 3050 Ti. The model can only generate short stories, it is not an assistant, but the live demo runs directly on a physical board installed in Wales.

**NVIDIA open-weights Magpie TTS, 12 languages including French**

NVIDIA updates its open-weights speech synthesis model **Magpie TTS Multilingual**, 364 million parameters, which now covers **12 languages** with the addition of Modern Standard Arabic, Korean and Brazilian Portuguese. The argument is not raw quality but control: deployable on your own infrastructure via NVIDIA NIM, it lets you control latency and data confidentiality, whereas closed voice APIs impose their black box.

**Probing models to guess how many parameters they hide**

A researcher details techniques for extracting information that labs do not publish. "Incompressible Knowledge Probes" test models on very obscure facts to estimate their parameter count, "Data Mixture Inference" analyzes how they split tokens to deduce the training datasets, and date-related questions reveal pre-training schedules. Lacking a public reference, the results remain speculative, but the method is instructive.

**Humanizing LLM responses would be a bad idea**

The author takes on a trend: asking AI agents to write short, without jargon, in "simplified technical English" or in a style adapted to ADHD. The problem, according to him, is that the instruction does not apply after the work but during it: it is a lossy compression, applied continuously, which erases along the way the signals of failure (unresolved hypotheses, contradictory results, uncertainties). His proposal: keep the richest possible representation between agents, and compress only at the last moment, when facing a human.

**Which programming language for coding agents?**

A highly cited study claims that dynamic and concise languages like Clojure or J consume 2 to 3 times fewer tokens than Rust, Go or C++, to the point that Google's AI summaries repeat the conclusion. Dan Luu dismantles the method: the benchmarks rely on trivial Rosetta Code-style problems, unrelated to real code, and one of them contains a bug (an erroneous symlink created by an agent) that skewed the scoring of several languages tested afterward.

**Academic researchers adapt to the grip of private labs**

At a meeting of the AI2050 program funded by Eric and Wendy Schmidt, professors describe a shift in the center of gravity of research toward Anthropic, OpenAI and Google, which keep the training details of their models secret. Without a budget to buy GPUs or to massively query these systems, researchers at UC Berkeley and Johns Hopkins are redirecting their work toward questions that companies have no interest in exploring themselves.

**AWS launches a competition to co-design models and chips**

Amazon invites academic and industrial laboratories to train a language model of about **50 million parameters** from scratch on its Trainium chips, with total freedom over architecture, optimizer and compute kernels. The underlying idea is interesting: current architectures were shaped by the constraints of GPUs, and Trainium offers a different profile (more on-chip SRAM, explicit software control of data movement). What would a model designed for that hardware look like?

**Macaron-V1, agents that keep learning after deployment**

This family of open source models relies on a **Mixture-of-LoRA** architecture: a frozen base model, several specialized adapters (conversation, agent, code, generative interface), and a single one selected at each turn of conversation. The flagship model, Macaron-V1-Venti, combines a **GLM-5.2 base of 744 billion parameters** with these adapters, while the Tall version (**50B**, Qwen3.6 base) targets local deployment. The whole thing is driven by a self-improvement loop where the experience of one version is used to build the next.

**Video models that learn physics instead of imitating pixels**

Video diffusion models produce plausible images without ever modeling how pixels transform over time, hence objects that pass through walls. The **Latent Dynamics Reasoning** method treats the transition between images as an explicit kinematic integration, and generalizes to situations never seen before: trained on red balls, it correctly predicts the trajectory of a blue square. A not insignificant bonus, it is **26 times lighter and 143 times faster** than the reference models.

# **🗞️MORE NEWS**

**A pharmacy chain disconnects its AI voice assistant after hundreds of complaints**

Kinney Drugs had launched "Burt" in May, an AI voice assistant named after the chain's founder, tasked with handling patient calls about their prescriptions and refills. Customers quickly reported incoherent conversations, **erroneous dosages communicated over the phone**, and refill notifications that were never sent. The company is reversing course: back to the good old touch-tone phone menu for incoming calls, with AI kept only for outgoing texts with explicit consent. The executive's statement is worth quoting: succeeding at privacy and security does not mean succeeding at the experience, and here, it was a failure.

**American robotics startups bring their Chinese parts back in their suitcases**

The Information recounts a scene that has become routine: engineers from American humanoid robot startups fly to Shenzhen, scour the Huaqiangbei electronics market, and leave with the components in their carry-on luggage. The parts in question are not trivial, notably the actuators, the mechanical heart of a humanoid. The reason is prosaic: freight takes weeks that small teams in a race against time do not have. The anecdote sums up rather well the material balance of power between the two countries.

**Apple reportedly preparing a certificate of authenticity for your photos**

9to5Mac spotted traces in an iOS 27 beta of a feature called **Reference Image**, capable of proving that a photo was indeed captured by an iPhone. The principle: enable a Reference mode in the camera, then send the sensor metadata to Private Cloud Compute for verification. It is the opposite approach to watermarks like Google's SynthID, which mark generated images: here, the real is certified rather than the synthetic being tattooed. Nothing yet guarantees that the feature will survive until its public release this fall.

**Google adds AI agents to Google Ads and Analytics**

Google is rolling out a series of AI features in its marketing tools, available now in accounts. On the menu: automatic performance summaries directly on the Analytics home page, with alerts customizable by notification, **the creation of visual reports from a single sentence** in natural language, and a comparison of your advertising performance against similar companies. The whole thing relies on the Ask Advisor agent. For anyone who spends their mornings building dashboards by hand, this is the kind of update that takes ten minutes to test.

**Claude will mark its content to comply with the European AI Act**

Anthropic has signed the Code of Practice under Article 50(2) of the AI Act on the transparency of AI-generated content. Concretely, **all new Claude models launched in the EU after August 2, 2026** will include machine-readable marking from their release: watermarks in text, signed provenance metadata in files. The system will apply everywhere Claude is used, from the API to Claude Code and Claude Cowork. Anthropic promises technical documentation to allow third parties to detect these marks, which will be the real test of the system's usefulness.

**The FCC wants to ban drones it itself had approved**

The U.S. telecom regulator is proposing to extend its ban to drones equipped with LiDAR, thermal imaging, or aerosol dispersal capabilities, including models it had already approved. Several consumer DJI models are targeted (Air 3S, Avata 360, Mini 5 Pro), as well as professional models used in agriculture and search-and-rescue. Devices already sold would not be confiscated, but access to spare parts and software updates could dry up. DJI denounces a complete reversal, and the public consultation remains open until September 2.

**OpenAI launches GPT-5.6-Cyber, a model dedicated to cybersecurity**

OpenAI is expanding its Daybreak program with a specialized model, **GPT-5.6-Cyber**, accessible via the Daybreak Red platform. It is intended for authorized vulnerability research, exploit validation, and security testing, that is, for controlled offensive uses in the service of defense. The company justifies the launch by the shrinking window between the moment a flaw becomes exploitable by an AI and the moment defenders can patch it. It is also a sign of a strategy of vertical models, tailored by profession rather than generalist.

**OpenAI writes to the Texas governor about its data centers**

OpenAI sent an official letter to Greg Abbott detailing its commitments on "responsible" AI infrastructure in the state, with the expected words about reliability, transparency, and benefits for Texans. Texas concentrates a growing share of American data center projects, and the question of electricity as well as water is becoming politically sensitive there. An exercise in institutional communication, but one that indicates where the sector's real negotiations are shifting: at the state level, not Washington.

**OpenAI's CFO recounts her "AI-native" finance function**

Sarah Friar shares five lessons drawn from transforming her finance team with in-house tools: automated forecasting, internal controls reinforced by AI, and above all the question of calculating the return on investment of AI projects, which remains the most painful point for most companies. The exercise obviously has a promotional dimension, with OpenAI selling what it describes itself as using. Still, the detail of the rethought processes is more concrete than the average testimony of this kind.

**The fortunes of AI reignite an old debate about private power**

The unprecedented accumulation of wealth by the sector's leaders and investors revives a question that has run through American history since the railroad barons: what do you do when a few private fortunes weigh more heavily than public institutions on the direction of a technology? The debate concerns philanthropy, which allows one to choose the causes being funded, as much as the direct influence exerted over regulation. The article remains at the level of analysis rather than precise fact, but the subject will not close anytime soon.

**Nvidia mobilizes $500 billion to finance its own customers**

Nvidia has signed memoranda of understanding with six of the world's largest asset managers (Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, KKR) to create financing platforms intended for hyperscalers and AI labs that buy its chips. The stated goal is to mobilize **more than $500 billion in third-party capital**. Jensen Huang's argument is the most interesting: he now presents AI chips as an investable asset class, generating revenue over time and transferable from one customer to another, against the received idea of hardware that depreciates in three years.

**OpenAI completes a $7 billion share sale**

The operation allows current and former employees to sell part of their holdings on the basis of a valuation of **$852 billion**. It had been in preparation since the closing in March of a $122 billion financing round, and comes as OpenAI confidentially filed its IPO paperwork with the SEC in June, without a public timeline. Anthropic finds itself in a comparable situation. For the employees concerned, it is above all the first real opportunity to turn shares into money.
