**Why it matters:** In a sector where enterprise trust is hard-won, finding yourself in the crosshairs of power can, against all expectations, strengthen the image of independence of an AI lab. Many decision-makers see it as the sign of a provider that doesn't bow to pressure, a strong argument at a time when choosing a model commits strategic data. For Anthropic, the confrontation with Trump thus becomes a reputational asset as much as a risk. It remains to be seen how long this goodwill capital will hold if the regulatory vise tightens.

The next humanoid robot may have neither head nor legs. That's the bet of **Genesis AI**, a French startup backed by **Eric Schmidt**, the former Google boss, which has just unveiled **Eno**: a versatile machine mounted on a wheeled mobile base, capable of folding up like a beach chair. The company's mantra comes down to one sentence: "humanoid robots don't need to look like humans."

Eno forgoes the classic human silhouette: **no head, no legs**, but a wheeled mobile base
It is designed "around human **capabilities**" rather than human **appearance**
Conceived as a **generalist** robot, rather than specialized on a single task like folding laundry
The only concession to anatomy: its **hands**, designed to "correspond exactly to the form and function" of the human hand
Project backed by the financial support of **Eric Schmidt**

**What this changes:** For years, humanoid robotics has chased an ever more faithful resemblance to humans, even at the cost of sacrificing efficiency on the altar of aesthetics. Genesis takes the opposite stance: if the ultimate goal is usefulness, why impose a human form that is costly and complex to master? By keeping only what truly matters, starting with dexterous hands, the startup is betting on robots that are simpler to produce and deploy. A philosophy that could, if it convinces, bring closer the arrival of genuine versatile assistants in our homes, and reshuffle the cards in a race toward humanoids until now obsessed with realism.

France is turning its back on **Palantir**. According to The Guardian, the country is abandoning the American company's data analysis tools, regularly criticized, in favor of a national provider: **ChapsVision**. A switch that looks like a manifesto of digital sovereignty.

Transition of AI data processing tools from **Palantir** to **ChapsVision**, a French player
Stated issue: **digital sovereignty** and technological independence over sensitive data
A strong political signal concerning so-called critical infrastructures
Topic relayed and discussed within the Hacker News community

**The context:** Palantir has for years crystallized European concerns about dependence on American solutions for processing sovereign data. By entrusting these tools to a local player, Paris sends a clear message: on the most strategic building blocks, autonomy comes before convenience. The move is not isolated. It echoes the Dutch initiative **GPT-NL**, unveiled the same day, and more broadly a European wave that wants to regain control over its critical technologies. The question of maturity remains: can a national champion really compete, in the short term, with the power and lead of American platforms? That is the whole challenge of the coming months.

Is prompt engineering already outdated? According to several figures in the sector, we no longer "prompt": we design loops that drive agents. Within a few days, Peter (from **OpenClaw**) and Boris (from **Claude Code**) both claimed to no longer write prompts. **Addy Osmani**, at Google, immediately dubbed this practice "**Loop Engineering**", the fourth paradigm after Prompt, Context and Harness.

Agents are no longer guided by isolated prompts but by **loops** designed to make them work autonomously
The claimed lineage: **Prompt, Context, Harness, Loop**
That's **four "engineering" disciplines** invented in barely two years
Each was THE essential skill, until the next one made it optional

**The impact to remember:** Behind the buzzword lies a deep-seated trend: the half-life of AI skills is melting before our eyes. The expertise everyone was scrambling for six months ago can become secondary overnight. For professionals, the real capital is therefore no longer mastery of the tool of the moment, but the ability to reinvent oneself at the frantic pace of agents. A lesson that goes far beyond the circle of developers: as autonomous agents gain ground, it's our very way of working with machines that is being redefined, again and again.
