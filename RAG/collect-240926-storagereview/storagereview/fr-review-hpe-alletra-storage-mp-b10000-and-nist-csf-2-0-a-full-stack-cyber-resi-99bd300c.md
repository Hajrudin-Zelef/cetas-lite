# fr-review-hpe-alletra-storage-mp-b10000-and-nist-csf-2-0-a-full-stack-cyber-resi-99bd300c

<!-- source: https://www.storagereview.com/fr/review/hpe-alletra-storage-mp-b10000-and-nist-csf-2-0-a-full-stack-cyber-resilience-architecture -->

HPE has designed a coordinated cyber resilience architecture built around the Alletra Storage MP B10000 storage system. This architecture extends the platform's native security capabilities with an integrated software suite including virtualization with Morpheus and VM Essentials, continuous data protection with Zerto, long-term backup retention with StoreOnce, and observability through integration with a vendor-independent security information and event management (SIEM) system. The overall architecture is designed to be fully compliant with the NIST 2.0 cybersecurity framework, with the B10000 serving as the operational hub for all framework functions. The goal of this design is to answer the two fundamental questions that every security expert asks about any infrastructure: is it still under our control, and is the data it contains still protected?
Enterprise storage is traditionally overlooked in broader security discussions. In most organizations, a chief information security officer (CISO) concerned about a misconfigured router in a branch office or an unpatched laptop on the corporate network pays little attention to data center storage arrays. These are protected by multiple layers of perimeter security, accessible to only a handful of administrators, and largely invisible to the rest of the IT department. On paper, they are one of the most secure assets in the building.
That assumption is no longer valid. Modern ransomware attacks have learned that the storage array is the most valuable target in the data center. Endpoints and servers can be reinstalled. Primary storage contains the data, and an attacker who gains administrative control over it can encrypt the data, delete the snapshots that would enable recovery, and destroy backups in a single coordinated operation. At that point, the organization is no longer experiencing a mere inconvenience; its survival is at stake. The tools available to attackers, including AI-assisted variants that adapt faster than signature-based defenses, are increasingly effective at hitting this target.
The stakes are no longer just operational. In the United States, the European Union, and the United Kingdom, regulators have transformed infrastructure security from a best practice into a legal obligation. Regulatory frameworks such as the European Digital Operational Resilience Act and the NIS2 directive require demonstrable controls for detection, recovery, and incident reporting, with liability extending to the executive level. The organizations subject to these obligations are precisely those that manage enterprise storage at scale: banks and financial services firms, hospitals and healthcare networks, energy and critical infrastructure providers, government agencies, and the cloud service providers that host all of these systems. For them, neglecting infrastructure security represents not only a business risk but also a compliance failure with serious consequences.
Cyber resilience sits at the crossroads between storage and security teams, which report to different managers, measure success differently, and rarely share the same operational language needed for coordination during an incident. Storage administrators master throughput, capacity, and recovery time objectives. Security teams, for their part, understand attack chains, attack vectors, and security posture management. Most organizations only discover this gap after being forced to. The architecture implemented by HPE is designed to bridge it, and the following sections analyze each NIST function to assess its effectiveness, constantly returning to the two essential questions of control and protection.
Key takeaways
- Full-stack resilience: HPE combines the Alletra MP B10000 storage system with VM Essentials, Zerto, StoreOnce, and a vendor-independent SIEM integration, mapping the coordinated stack to every NIST CSF 2.0 function.
- Fast block-level detection: The B10000's built-in ransomware engine, based on entropy and validated against more than 100 strains, detected a simulated encryption attempt in 4 to 5 minutes and automatically captured a forensic snapshot at the moment of detection.
- Immutability that withstands administrator compromise: Virtual Lock snapshots enforced by the array remain read-only for the entire retention period; even a compromised administrator account cannot delete them, and any attempt becomes a logged event visible to the SIEM.
- Tiered recovery: Four tiers (Zerto for the lowest RPO, VME snapshots, Virtual Lock promotion, and StoreOnce Catalyst for long-term retention) were tested in the lab, with clean restorations from Virtual Lock and Catalyst.
- Standards-compliant telemetry: Structured security syslog normalized to the Elastic Common Schema feeds Elastic Security, CrowdStrike Falcon, or any modern SIEM, supporting DORA/NIS2 evidence and automated SOAR playbooks.
Architecture overview
In order to provide a practical demonstration environment rather than a large-scale enterprise deployment, the HPE team set up a compact but functional backup and recovery infrastructure in its Fort Collins lab. At the heart of this environment is a three-node HPE VM Essentials (VME) cluster, running on HPE ProLiant DL325 Gen 11 servers, which provides the compute layer for the demonstration infrastructure. These hosts are interconnected via the local IP network, which also provides connectivity to the NAS layer used within the environment.
HPE Zerto virtual machines run within the VME cluster, configured similarly to how many organizations currently deploy Zerto in a traditional VMware environment. Although the underlying infrastructure uses HPE VME, the operational flow and disaster recovery capabilities remain familiar to administrators accustomed to VMware-based disaster recovery procedures.
A secondary Zerto environment connected to the WAN is available in the Bristol, UK lab. Firewall rules and network paths are configured to support replication from Fort Collins. This topology targets multi-site recovery: continuous replication and disaster recovery orchestration across geographically distinct sites. Thus, protected workloads in Fort Collins can fail over to Bristol in the event of primary site unavailability.
During this engagement, HPE ran Zerto replication locally in Fort Collins due to bandwidth and distance constraints, and because a second parallel VME cluster was not available to test the full inter-site flow. The Bristol infrastructure is documented here as configured and available, rather than as an inter-site failover performed during this session.
Storage connectivity is split between IP and Fibre Channel networks, depending on the workload. The Fibre Channel SAN infrastructure directly connects the VME hosts to the HPE Alletra MP B10000 platform, providing shared access to enterprise storage within the cluster. The B10000 also presents Catalyst via Fibre Channel to the StoreOnce Virtual Storage Appliance (VSA), which ensures application-consistent backups between the array and the backup target. Virtual Lock snapshots play a central role in ransomware resilience and platform immutability; their operation is detailed in the Protection and Recovery sections.
For its backup infrastructure, HPE deployed a StoreOnce Gen5 VSA solution running on a single lab server. The VSA solution offers the same essential StoreOnce capabilities as the dedicated appliance. Deduplication, backup target presentation, replication behavior, and integration with the HPE data protection suite remain fundamentally identical, whether deployed as a VSA or on dedicated hardware. The VSA solution is perfectly suited for labs, branch offices, test environments, and small-scale production use cases, making this demonstration compact and easy to deploy.
The physical StoreOnce appliance has an advantage that goes beyond mere scalability and throughput, and that proves particularly relevant in the ransomware context analyzed in this article. A VSA runs as a virtual machine on a hypervisor. If an attacker compromises the virtualization layer, all workloads running on it, including a VSA backup target, become vulnerable. A physical StoreOnce appliance, by contrast, does not have this dependency. It runs on dedicated hardware, outside the hypervisor, which the attacker would have to traverse, thus ensuring that the backup of last resort relies on an uncompromised infrastructure. For production deployments where StoreOnce constitutes the long-term retention tier in a cyber resilience architecture, the physical appliance represents the most sensible choice, precisely for this reason.
The HPE Alletra MP system and the StoreOnce VSA were both integrated into the HPE Data Services Cloud console, providing centralized, cloud-based management and visibility of the entire environment. Through this console, the infrastructure can be monitored, managed, and integrated into HPE Data Services workflows from a single interface, bringing together storage, backup, and recovery operations.
Govern: Implementing your strategy
HPE does not write your governance plan. It is your organization, your insurer, and your legal department that define security policy. HPE provides the interfaces (APIs, command-line interfaces, Data Services Cloud console, and published security hardening guides) that allow you to implement that policy, attest to its compliance, and conduct audits of it.
This distinction is crucial, because most storage vendors quietly assume the opposite. They offer subjective default configurations presented as best practices and treat configuration drift as a support issue rather than a governance warning signal. The B10000 reverses this approach. Every administrative action is logged. Every security policy-relevant setting is accessible via the API. The security syslog stream is structured to feed the same SIEM where the organization's other governance evidence is already stored. When an auditor asks who changed the password policy on a specific date, the answer is accessible in the same way as for all other managed systems in the infrastructure.
Two governance disciplines deserve particular attention. The first is regulatory compliance. The B10000's audit logging and SIEM integration architecture supports the operational controls required by the European Digital Operational Resilience Act and the NIS2 directive, covering detection, response, and recovery activities, incident reporting workflows, and the visibility expected by management for management control. Centralizing audit and security telemetry in the SIEM makes these obligations concrete. The controls expected by the auditor are not superfluous additions. They are the same controls used daily by the security operations center (SOC) for platform operations.
The second point concerns executive oversight. Regulatory frameworks increasingly require executives to demonstrate consolidated and up-to-date visibility into information and communication technology (ICT) risks. A storage array that hides its audit history behind a vendor-only support portal cannot satisfy this requirement. A storage array that streams audit and security events directly into the customer's SIEM, however, can. The B10000 was designed to meet this second requirement.
The platform offers several concrete features. Documented security hardening guides define initial configuration. Role-based access control and dual authentication mechanisms ensure separation of duties. Multi-factor authentication is built in. DISA STIG (Defense Information Systems Agency Security Technical Implementation Guide) security hardening recommendations are published for environments that require them. The security syslog stream turns every governance-relevant action into a queryable event. The plan is the customer's responsibility. The instrumentation is the platform's responsibility.
Identify: Trust in the platform
Within the NIST CSF 2.0 framework, identification consists of understanding what the organization owns, where it comes from, and what risks it entails. For a storage platform, this translates into supply chain integrity, secure development practices, and verifiable initial configuration.
HPE's hardware and software supply chain is the foundation. Components come from audited circuits, firmware is signed, and platforms are shipped in tamper-evident packaging and seals that the receiving team can verify using shipping manifests. Supply chain assessment is a documented process used by the HPE Cybersecurity Center of Excellence to ensure that a B10000 platform leaving the factory one week has the same provenance as a B10000 platform leaving the factory another week.
Secure development practices extend this security chain to software. The B10000 operating system undergoes structured penetration testing, including internal red team exercises and third-party assessments, the results of which are integrated into the release cycle. Identification is not a one-time operation performed at installation. It is a continuous verification that the trusted platform of day one is still the one present on day 400. The B10000 provides the elements (signed firmware, hash-verified downloads, security hardening guides, secure development documentation, and audit log streams) that enable this verification. Using these elements is, again, solely the decision of the security team.
Protect: Maintaining posture
Protection of the B10000 relies on four mechanisms: immutable Virtual Lock snapshot scheduling, a deliberate separation between array snapshots and hypervisor snapshots, replication to a second site via the array's native Remote Copy and Zerto, and continuous drift detection via the audit log stream.
Immutable Virtual Lock snapshot scheduling
Virtual Lock snapshots are created according to a schedule defined at the array level and enforced directly by the B10000 storage platform. These snapshots remain immutable and read-only for the entire duration of their configured retention period, preventing any modification or deletion until expiration. Administrative actions performed on protected snapshots are recorded in the audit log. These logs can be forwarded to external SIEM platforms, providing visibility into policy violation attempts, including attempts to delete protected recovery points.
B10000 snapshots versus VME snapshots
In this environment, snapshots come from two different sources, and policy development depends on maintaining this distinction.
B10000 scheduled snapshots are immutable and read-only, and their retention is governed by the Virtual Lock mechanism. VME snapshots, created by HPE Morpheus VM Essentials at the hypervisor level, are modifiable and read-write accessible for virtual machine operational workflows.
If an attacker gains access to the hypervisor, VME snapshots become accessible. Virtual Lock snapshots, however, do not. The Virtual Lock schedule defines the minimum retention duration. VME snapshots complement the upper operational layer.
Replication: native at the array level and at the application level
A Virtual Lock snapshot on the primary site does not protect against site-level events. The architecture offers two distinct replication paths to address this. The first is native to the array: B10000 Remote Copy asynchronously replicates volumes to a second B10000 array located at another site. Combined with Virtual Lock snapshot schedules on the target, it creates an immutable and physically separate vault, independent of the hypervisor and any application software. The second, Zerto, operates at the application level with near-synchronous replication and continuous journaling, providing the granular low recovery point objective (RPO) recovery path described in the "Recover" section.
Both solutions are complementary rather than redundant. Remote Copy preserves the immutability guarantees enforced by the storage array across sites, while Zerto provides checkpoint granularity and orchestrated failover at the virtual machine level. As part of this project, the topology was configured to support Zerto replication from Fort Collins to the HPE Bristol, UK site. However, during the test session, replication ran locally in Fort Collins due to bandwidth and parallel cluster constraints. The Bristol site is deployed as an available topology and for follow-up activity, not as an inter-site disaster recovery demonstration.
Configuration drift detection
The security hardening guide defines a baseline configuration. The continuous audit log stream allows a SIEM to verify, in near real time, that this configuration has not drifted. Changes to password policies, account lockout, creation of new accounts, role elevations, schedule modifications, and replication target changes all appear as queryable events. Since the lab environment did not have a SIEM, drift detection was performed at the B10000 audit log level rather than using a centralized correlation tool. At a customer with a SIEM, these same audit events are forwarded to the SIEM via the syslog integration described in the "Detect" section.
Detect: Visibility and early warning
The B10000 includes a ransomware detection engine that monitors storage I/O operations in real time and generates an alert as soon as a statistical signature of large-scale encryption is detected during writes to the array. The HPE Cybersecurity Center of Excellence has validated this engine against more than 100 of the most significant ransomware strains.
Detection is enabled per data store on the VME side. When a data store is created or modified in VME on a B10000 storage server, the ransomware detection option is enabled by default for each volume provisioned in that data store. Per-volume customization is possible after creation. This feature requires B10000 operating system version 10.5.0 or later. In the Fort Collins lab, the ftc-vme-mg1 data store was configured to connect to the FTC-AMP-5 B10000 server via Fibre Channel, with ransomware detection enabled. This configuration is the one used for the ransomware attack simulation described later in this section.
The B10000 also supports system-wide ransomware detection policies directly on the storage array, allowing administrators to define global detection sensitivity and automatic snapshot retention behavior on protected volumes. As shown below, the Fort Collins environment had the array-level ransomware detection policy enabled with a low-confidence sensitivity profile and automatic snapshot retention configured. This provides an additional layer of protection beyond per-datastore enablement in VME, ensuring that the detection engine remains consistently applied at the platform level, regardless of how individual volumes are provisioned.
The detection logic, for both types of data, relies on entropy. Random data, which corresponds to the appearance of encrypted data at the block level, exhibits high entropy. Legitimate workload data, even compressed, has a structure that the engine recognizes. The engine uses a CuSum entropy calculation to measure the disorder of incoming writes relative to an established baseline value. This baseline value is learned during a training phase, when a new volume is first protected, and then maintained by a rolling one-hour I/O profile, to prevent legitimate workload variations from generating false positives. When entropy deviates from the baseline in a statistically significant way, the engine flags the volume.
For the SOC, the main idea is that this encryption detection engine constitutes a last line of defense for the environment, not a substitute for endpoint protection. It is at the endpoint level that ransomware must be detected first. The B10000 detects what the endpoint let through.
SIEM integration
The integration is deliberately vendor-independent. HPE's role is to provide the infrastructure and tools necessary for the B10000 to integrate audit and security log data into any SIEM already in place within an organization, rather than steering customers toward a preferred HPE tool. Detection alerts and audit events are exported via the security syslog stream. The Fluentd-based aggregator, published by HPE on GitHub, takes the raw syslog message, normalizes it to the Elastic Common Schema format, and tags the event with fields such as "event.kind: alert" and "event.severity: high," so that the receiving platform's existing rules engine can process it without custom parsing. The major difference is that this data is not only compatible with modern SIEMs; it is active. It is structured to trigger automated alerts and downstream responses, instead of being archived and consulted after the fact. Elastic Security and CrowdStrike Falcon Next-Gen SIEM are platforms explicitly validated by HPE, and the open syslog format means that Microsoft Sentinel and other modern SIEMs can be integrated with comparable effort.
Ransomware simulation on a Windows host
During remote testing conducted on the HPE Fort Collins lab, a simulated ransomware workload was executed on a Windows host connected to a Virtual Lock-protected volume on the B10000. The simulation ran a controlled encryption script that produced the high-entropy write pattern characteristic of a ransomware encryption event, without using actual malware.
The B10000 detection engine triggered an alert four to five minutes after the encryption script began writing to the volume. The alert appeared on the B10000 management console, with identification of the affected volume.
The four-to-five-minute window is short enough that immutable snapshots taken at the time of detection, or shortly after, capture a stable state before the attacker has finished traversing the volume. This method is faster than most behavioral analyses performed at the application level, because the B10000 detects writes at the block level as soon as they occur.
Respond: From alert to action
When the B10000 ransomware detection engine triggers, the platform takes automatic protective actions, even before any external system intervenes. Upon detection, the array captures an immutable snapshot of the affected volume and reports its state as degraded. This alert snapshot is not a clean recovery point and should not be used for data restoration, as it may contain encrypted data. It is intended for forensic analysis, preserving the state of the volume at the time of attack detection. It is important to note that the array does not block access to the volume. The data remains accessible, a deliberate design choice. The array triggers the alert and preserves the evidence. However, it leaves it to operators to decide on the course of action, as they are best positioned to determine whether it is a genuine attack or a false positive, such as a large legitimate encryption or compression operation.
This autonomous behavior represents the minimum, not the maximum, of response capability. Since it runs directly on the storage array, it does not depend on a SIEM rule triggering, a syslog stream functioning properly, or an external automation tool being available. Even if all other layers fail, the snapshot is preserved. This independence is essential: the most important response is triggered, whether the rest of the stack is operational or not.
Beyond this level, the active SIEM integration described in the Detect section enables orchestrated response. Since B10000 alerts are structured to trigger action rather than being archived, the customer can create automated scenarios in their existing SIEM or SOAR (Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response) platform. These scenarios can include extending snapshot retention on affected volumes, isolating the host, opening an incident ticket, or notifying the response team. HPE provides the active and structured signal. The orchestration logic relies on the tools chosen by the customer, ensuring that the response complies with the procedures and approval workflows already established by the security team.
An important clarification is needed: the interface can be structured in layers. The B10000 alert offers a data analysis option, but the system itself does not search for or identify specific malware. This option assumes that the customer has their own analysis and digital forensics tools to identify affected data. The B10000's role is detection and data preservation, not deletion.
Recover: Tiered restoration and validation
Recovery is a tiered process that consists of making decisions: which recovery point, from which tier, in which environment, and by which process validation is performed. The architecture developed in the Fort Collins lab supports a four-tier recovery model, each tier justified by the need to solve a different problem.
Tier 1: Zerto for continuous protection. Near-synchronous replication with continuous journaling offers the lowest RPO in the stack. Granular restoration allows the incident response team to restore a virtual machine or file to a checkpoint prior to the encryption event. This is the preferred tier when the alert is triggered quickly and the attacker's window of opportunity is narrow.
Tier 2: Native VME protection for daily virtual machine-level restoration. The HPE Morpheus VM Essentials snapshot flow provides operational recovery at the virtual machine level. This tier handles routine restoration operations and serves as a fallback if Tier 1 is unavailable.
Tier 3: B10000 immutable Virtual Lock snapshots for volume-level restoration. This tier is crucial in the event of a ransomware attack. Virtual Lock snapshots are protected against deletion and modification, and the promotion operation is native to the B10000, with no dependency on a third-party software vendor.
Tier 4: StoreOnce Catalyst restoration for long-term retention and cross-array recovery. Tiers 1 through 3 protect against incidents for a few hours or a few days. Tier 4 protects against incidents for a few weeks or a few months, particularly when the attacker established persistence well before encryption was triggered and when the only valid recovery point predates the retention period of any primary storage snapshot. Catalyst stores are deduplicated, immutable, and can be replicated across multiple StoreOnce systems or detached to Cloud Bank object storage. Catalyst Copy and Cloud Bank Detach together constitute Tier 1 of the 3-2-1-1 model. It is also at this tier that the distinction between physical infrastructure and VSA, mentioned in the architecture overview, becomes most important. As a last-resort recovery option, Tier 4 must be able to withstand a hypervisor compromise. This is why it is recommended to deploy StoreOnce on a dedicated physical appliance rather than on a VSA in production.
Final assessment
The two essential questions a security expert asks about a storage platform are: does the infrastructure remain under the organization's control, and does the data remain protected? After analyzing the architecture in the Fort Collins lab, it appears that the B10000 provides more complete answers to these two questions than a simple storage array, because the answer is not limited to the array itself.
The platform logs every administrative action, exposes all relevant settings via its API, and forwards audit and security telemetry data to the SIEM already used by the organization. The customer remains responsible for drafting the governance plan, but the tools needed to implement it, document it, and have it validated by an auditor are available and accessible, not hidden behind a vendor portal. Thanks to immutable Virtual Lock snapshots, even a compromised administrator account cannot discreetly destroy recovery points; any attempt to do so is logged and can be queried.
In terms of protection, the detection and recovery loop at the heart of the architecture is the element that demonstrated the greatest reliability. The B10000 detected the simulated encryption workload in four to five minutes at the block level and captured a forensic snapshot upon detection, without waiting for an external system. The affected volume was then restored without issue, both through a scheduled Virtual Lock snapshot promotion and through a StoreOnce Catalyst restoration. It is this sequence that is crucial during an incident, and it ran without any third-party product in the dependency chain.
What sets this architecture apart is not so much that the B10000 is significantly more secure than competing arrays taken individually. It is rather that HPE is one of the few vendors to offer storage, virtualization, replication, backup, and observability within an integrated system, and to test that system as a whole against real ransomware in its own lab, instead of validating each component separately. For an enterprise that owns its infrastructure but is subject to a separate security team and an external regulator, this coordination makes all the difference between a set of high-performing products and a resilience strategy capable of working under pressure.
The architecture is evolving rapidly. HPE maintains a regular release cadence for the entire technology stack, with deeper integration between VM Essentials and the B10000, ransomware detection extended to data types beyond block volumes, and a complete published reference architecture, all elements planned in its near-term roadmap. This evolution is moving toward coordination increasingly managed natively by the platform and toward a clearer design for implementing the type of resilience architecture analyzed in this article.
For years, the storage array was considered one of the safest elements in the building, mainly because no one paid much attention to it. This architecture demonstrates that the array should be quite the opposite: not a neglected box in a corner, but a key player in detecting and protecting against an attack, provided the organization takes the necessary steps to turn these capabilities into a security plan.
