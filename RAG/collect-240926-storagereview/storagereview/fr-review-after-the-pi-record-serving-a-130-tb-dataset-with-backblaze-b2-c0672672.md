# fr-review-after-the-pi-record-serving-a-130-tb-dataset-with-backblaze-b2-c0672672

<!-- source: https://www.storagereview.com/fr/review/after-the-pi-record-serving-a-130-tb-dataset-with-backblaze-b2 -->

Earlier this year, we set a new record by computing 314 trillion digits of Pi . The computation, which ran for months on a single Dell PowerEdge R7725 server, pushed modern processor and storage infrastructure to its limits, while demonstrating the considerable progress made by enterprise computing hardware. Once the computation was complete, the record itself turned out to be only part of the story. The computation produced a colossal dataset consisting of 628 files of approximately 206 GB each, for a total of more than 130 TB. These files represent the entirety of the computation's result and are often the most interesting part of a project of this kind. Mathematicians, developers, and data scientists often want to explore these datasets further, whether to validate certain parts of the computation, experiment with new analysis methods, or work with exceptionally large numerical datasets.
This poses a practical problem that goes far beyond the scope of Pi computation. Modern computing workloads increasingly generate enormous datasets that must remain accessible long after the computation is finished. Scientific simulations, genomic analyses, and large-scale AI training all produce results that can reach tens or even hundreds of terabytes. While a laboratory environment is perfectly suited to generating this data, it is rarely the most appropriate for hosting it indefinitely for a global audience.
For our Pi project, keeping more than 130 TB of results permanently stored in the StorageReview lab was not the right solution. Researchers had already begun requesting it, and making the dataset available on a large scale required infrastructure capable of storing it reliably and distributing it efficiently.
That is where Backblaze came in. The company hosts the entirety of the Pi data on its Backblaze B2 cloud storage platform, making the results accessible to researchers and enthusiasts who wish to use them. Providing durable storage and global access to a dataset of this magnitude represents a significant commitment, and we thank Backblaze for its valuable help, which allows the scientific community to fully benefit from the results of this record-breaking computation.
This collaboration also illustrates an increasingly observed trend in modern infrastructure: the concrete benefits of hybrid workflows that combine on-premises computation with cloud storage and distribution. The Pi computation was entirely executed in our lab on a single system, but once the work was finished, the cloud emerged as the ideal solution for hosting and sharing the results.
Before that, the data had to be transferred from our lab to Backblaze. Transferring more than 130 TB of results represents a major challenge, but thanks to a high-performance network connection, the transfer was completed in less than two weeks, with a steady throughput of 2 Gbit/s for most of the process. Large datasets remain resource-intensive, and when transferring hundreds of gigabytes at a time, bandwidth quickly becomes the limiting factor.
Now that the data is securely stored on Backblaze B2, the results of the 314 trillion digit computation can be shared beyond our lab. Researchers can download the files, test them, verify certain parts of the computation, or explore the digits for their own projects. As Pi Day 2026 approaches, this is the ideal time to make this data accessible.
Backblaze B2: Enterprise cloud storage for large-scale data
To make the Pi dataset widely accessible, infrastructure capable of reliably storing and distributing very large volumes of data was essential. Backblaze B2 Cloud Storage is an enterprise object storage platform designed precisely for this type of workload. Object storage makes it possible to store large collections of files in scalable object buckets, rather than constraining them to traditional file hierarchies. It is thus perfectly suited to large datasets, backups, and modern data pipelines. B2 also supports S3-compatible APIs, allowing organizations to interact with the service using familiar tools and workflows, without having to rethink their existing applications or data processes.

Hosting the dataset in Backblaze B2
Once the PI computation (314 trillion digits) was complete and the data transferred from the R7725 to NAS storage, the question of making it publicly available required careful consideration. Streaming 130 TB directly from the lab was not feasible. Our WAN connection, limited to 2 Gbit/s in upload and download throughput, is shared among all lab operations. Continuous and large public downloads would have disrupted daily operations, and bandwidth throttling would have made large-scale simultaneous downloads slow and unreliable for any user wishing to access the data.
The initial idea was to distribute the dataset via BitTorrent. Although technically possible, this solution is not the most accessible to most users. Downloading a 130 TB dataset via torrent requires a client, some familiarity with how this type of download works, and patience with a process that is less simple than a direct download. For a dataset intended to be widely accessible to researchers and the scientific community, it was preferable to avoid these difficulties.
Backblaze B2 solved both of these problems elegantly. The dataset is hosted entirely in the cloud, which allows public downloads to bypass the lab's wide area network and any load on its operations. Connections to B2 are made via HTTPS directly from Backblaze's infrastructure, ensuring secure, consistent downloads independent of the availability of third-party servers. The entire dataset is now stored in the pi-314-trillion bucket in the form of 628 objects totaling approximately 132 TB, organized under a single data path and accessible to everyone, without impacting the lab.
The transfer from the lab's NAS was carried out using a simple Rclone configuration, routed through our UDM Pro Max, which serves as a gateway, at a steady throughput of just over 2 Gbit/s for the entire duration of the transfer. At that rate, transferring 130 TB required approximately 10 days of continuous transfer. The graphs below illustrate this transfer time, per minute and cumulatively.
WAN usage during the transfer
The lab's UniFi network dashboard confirms the upload characteristics during the transfer period. The WAN interface indicates that Backblaze is the dominant application in terms of traffic volume, with an upload throughput of 2.27 Gbit/s and monthly WAN data consumption of 90.9 TB recorded during the period. The connection remained stable throughout the transfer, with no significant packet loss.

Final bucket layout
Backblaze created the pi-314-trillion bucket, which contains all 628 files and whose confirmed size is 132,210.5 GB. This bucket is configured as private, retains all file versions, and is accessible via the S3-compatible endpoint at s3.us-west-004.backblazeb2.com. Object storage simplifies the management of a dataset of this magnitude. Each file is individually addressable, the complete listing can be retrieved programmatically, and there is no file system hierarchy or volume limit to work around.
Accessing the dataset
Researchers are asking us to access this data; in fact, a project is already underway. Michael Kleber is a senior software engineer at Google, but he has been studying the digits of pi since earning his doctorate in mathematics in 1999. Mathematicians consider pi to be a normal number , so it is reasonable to ask: "Among the 10^d sequences of d digits, which one takes the longest to appear in pi, and how many digits are needed?" Kleber conducted the research up to d = 7, and when Fabrice Bellard computed 2.7 trillion digits of pi in 2009, Kleber encouraged him to extend his research to d = 11, the limit of what was possible at the time. "With 314 trillion random digits, there is about a 79% chance of finding all sequences of length 13," Kleber explains, "so I hope we get lucky!" We expect this to happen much more often now that Backblaze has made the data accessible to everyone.
The PI dataset is hosted on Backblaze B2 and can be downloaded by anyone who wishes to use it. Access is via a request link to obtain credentials or download instructions to retrieve the files. Backblaze will host the dataset to ensure its availability for research and verification for the duration of the hosting.
Download options
Users can retrieve individual files or the entire 130 TB of data, depending on their needs. The bucket is structured so that each object can be accessed and downloaded directly, without having to retrieve the entire dataset. For those who wish to retrieve everything, a full bucket sync can be performed using the tools described below. This option requires 135 TB of free space.
Recommended tools
- Rclone is the recommended tool for accessing the dataset. It integrates seamlessly with Backblaze B2 and allows users to adapt the download process to their bandwidth needs.
- S3-compatible APIThus, any S3-compatible download tool can be used to retrieve the data. The only condition is that this tool allows the default S3 endpoint URL to be modified to point to the B2 endpoint rather than AWS.
A concrete example of hybrid infrastructure
Our computation of Pi to 314 trillion digits perfectly illustrates what hybrid infrastructure looks like in practice. The computation was entirely executed on a single Dell PowerEdge R7725 server in the StorageReview lab. However, because the system needed to be reassigned to other projects and tasks once the computation was complete, permanently keeping 130 TB of results within the lab was not a viable solution.
The desire to make the data available to those who wish to use it, whether for rigorous scientific work or simple curiosity, is very real. However, hosting a dataset of this size within the lab quickly becomes a significant burden on operations. Bandwidth is saturated, infrastructure is constantly stressed, and the lab's daily tasks are disrupted by every download request.
A solution like Backblaze B2 eliminates all of these obstacles. The data resides in cloud infrastructure designed specifically for data volumes of this magnitude, with scalable throughput, multiple redundancy points to ensure integrity, and the security and operational expertise specific to enterprise storage platforms. The compute resources were hosted on-premises because the project required it. The storage is in the cloud because that is simply the most suitable solution for what comes next.
