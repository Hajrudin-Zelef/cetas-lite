# fr-review-boosting-data-center-efficiency-with-solidigm-ssds-and-liquid-cooled-s-43f0153a

<!-- source: https://www.storagereview.com/fr/review/boosting-data-center-efficiency-with-solidigm-ssds-and-liquid-cooled-servers -->

As data centers strive to improve energy efficiency, especially due to the demands of AI workloads, many are turning to liquid cooling to optimize performance and manage power consumption. Liquid cooling can efficiently handle the heat generated by high-performance servers, allowing them to operate at full capacity without the energy-intensive costs associated with traditional air cooling. Solidigm's high-density SSDs are perfectly suited for these environments, delivering exceptional efficiency in terabytes per watt.
While AI is forcing many data center operators to consider liquid cooling, its impact goes far beyond that. In a previous report, we examined the effect of liquid cooling on a Dell PowerEdge R760 2U server. CoolIT's direct liquid cooling (DLC) system significantly reduced the server's energy consumption by lowering fan speeds, saving 200 watts. That test was entirely focused on processor performance; this time, we wanted to take a more storage-centric approach to understand the impact of SSDs on server energy consumption.
What are NVMe active power states?
NVMe power states are predefined states into which an NVMe device can transition to optimize its power consumption and performance. The NVMe specification allows for up to 32 power states, each characterized by a maximum power consumption, an entry latency (ENLAT), an exit latency (EXLAT), and relative performance. These power states are divided into operational and non-operational states. Operational states (or P states) allow the device to handle I/O operations. Non-operational states (or F states) are used when the device is idle and not handling any I/O operations.
Managing these power states is essential for optimizing the energy efficiency of NVMe devices, particularly in environments where power consumption is a major concern, such as edge devices and specialized applications like SSDs on the International Space Station. For example, the NVMe specification includes features such as Autonomous Power State Transition (APST), which allows the device to automatically transition between power states based on current usage and thermal conditions. This helps balance performance with power consumption, ensuring reliable operation in remote or constrained environments. Runtime D3 (RTD3) support allows the device to enter a zero-power sleep state, saving even more energy when the device is not in use.
NVMe power states are particularly beneficial when energy efficiency and thermal management are paramount. In edge devices, for example, the ability to quickly transition to lower power states when idle can significantly reduce energy consumption, which is essential for devices operating in remote or harsh environments with limited power availability. This is made possible through features such as PCIe Active State Power Management (ASPM) and low-power states such as L1.1 and L1.2, which reduce power consumption to minimal levels. Power and thermal management on the ISS is crucial due to the limited and controlled environment. NVMe power states can help limit SSD power consumption to manage thermal design power (TDP) and optimize the overall energy budget, ensuring SSDs operate efficiently without overheating.
In these specialized environments, NVMe power states provide a flexible and efficient way to manage the power consumption of NVMe devices. By leveraging these states, devices can balance performance and energy efficiency, making them suitable for a variety of applications, from edge computing to space missions. The ability to dynamically adjust power states based on real-time conditions ensures that NVMe devices can meet the varying requirements of different environments while optimizing energy efficiency and thermal management.
In addition to NVMe power states, the concept of composite temperature and touch temperature plays a crucial role in managing the thermal performance of NVMe SSDs in new enterprise SSDs. Touch temperature represents the external temperature of the SSD enclosure. Solidigm is a leader in adopting new higher touch temperature standards. The factory-set touch temperature for the Solidigm D5-P5336, for example, is 80 °C. This higher touch temperature limit allows SSDs to be cooled with lower airflow or to operate at higher ambient temperatures. This flexibility allows data centers to optimize cooling strategies and improve overall thermal management, potentially reducing cooling costs and improving SSD reliability and longevity.
Managing NVMe active power states
In a Linux test environment running Ubuntu 22.04, we can use the NVMe toolkit to query the drive to display and modify the power states of the D5-P5336. As you can see below, the drive supports states 0, 1, 2, and 0, with step 2 being the least restrictive and step XNUMX the most restrictive.
For the Solidigm 61.44 TB D5-P5336, PS0 is 25 W, PS1 is 15 W, and PS2 is 10 W. The drive idles at about 5.5 W, so with each increase in power mode, the SSD has less and less power overhead to devote to NAND read and write operations. Write operations are the most affected, as they consume more energy to write to NAND than to read from it.

Impact of power states on performance
To measure the impact on power consumption and performance of power states on the 61.44 TB Solidigm D5-P5336 SSD, we equipped a Dell PowerEdge R760 with 24 SSDs. By running Ubuntu and the FIO workload generator, we were able to easily run a consistent workload across all SSDs and update the power mode on the fly.
We used Dell's embedded power monitoring inside the server's iDRAC9 embedded management system to monitor system-level power.
We focused on sequential read and write bandwidth workloads, using a 128 KB block size on each drive, and then measured overall performance across the 24 SSDs. It is worth noting that this particular Dell PowerEdge R760 configuration with 24 NVMe bays uses a PCIe switch compared to direct-attach NVMe bays. Thus, the total measured bandwidth saturates the available PCIe switch lanes before reaching the drives. This impacts the total read performance we measured compared to the Soldigim P5536 datasheet, but the overall write speeds were all below this limit.

Going back to our article on the benefits of converting an air-cooled platform to direct liquid cooling, we saw a slight increase in processor performance, but we also saved 200 W of power. Power is a precious commodity in the new wave of AI-centric servers that often devote all available resources to GPUs and high-end processors. In a data center with a limited power budget or close to the air-cooling limit, switching to DLC buys a power budget that allows the server to be filled with more SSDs for the same power footprint as an air-cooled server.
A power savings of 200 W can be very useful in terms of storage density. This savings allows you to double the storage footprint from 12 to 24 SSDs in a liquid-cooled server compared to an air-cooled server if you have workloads oriented toward read-intensive workloads. With the Solidigm D5-P5336, this 24-bay server increased its storage capacity from 737 TB to 1,474 TB thanks to the liquid loop. If the workload is write-heavy, you can equip the server with about eight additional SSDs. However, these figures are for baseline power modes, so if you are willing to reduce high-end write performance, you can easily equip your server with 24 SSDs with a write-heavy workload at reduced performance.
Conclusion
Through our testing of Solidigm D5-P5336 SSDs, we were able to see that managing NVMe power states can have a significant impact on energy efficiency without significantly affecting performance. Data center operators looking to maximize energy efficiency can leverage these power states to achieve greater storage density or reduce operational costs, especially in AI-centric environments where power is paramount. Solidigm's high-density SSDs are well positioned for this, delivering excellent terabyte-per-watt efficiency, especially with modern liquid cooling technologies.
Our results reveal that even slight adjustments to power states can generate significant energy savings, which can be crucial in environments limited by power availability. Optimizing overall server power consumption improves storage density and promotes more sustainable data center operations.
Power management is becoming increasingly critical as modern servers are pushed to their limits, especially in AI-driven workloads. The combination of liquid cooling and efficient SSD management options offers a path forward for data centers looking to scale performance and storage density without exceeding power budgets.
