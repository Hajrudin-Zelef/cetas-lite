# fr-review-coolit-5d9994f0

We partnered with CoolIT Systems to introduce liquid cooling into our lab. As part of this effort, we developed a mini liquid cooling platform and upgraded a Dell PowerEdge R760, converting it from air cooling to liquid cooling. We are just beginning our liquid cooling journey, but we have already made significant discoveries about the benefits of direct liquid cooling (DLC).
Liquid cooling, in one form or another, will be necessary to support modern workloads. The math no longer works for air-cooled servers with massive CPU and GPU thermal design power (TDP). DLC delivered via cold plates is the most common solution, with every server vendor offering at least one option. As for Dell, they partnered with CoolIT Systems to provide liquid cooling across the entire PowerEdge lineup.
Our lab, like most data centers, was not originally designed to take advantage of liquid cooling. But like many data centers, we are finding that the most powerful servers require liquid cooling in one form or another, and if we want to take advantage of these systems, we need to adapt. This is a story we hear a lot from businesses these days, as data centers invest in AI, and most of these systems will soon require some kind of liquid loop to operate.
In our case, we decided to start by upgrading one of the Dell PowerEdge R760 servers in the lab. To be clear, when customers want liquid-cooled servers, they order them that way from Dell. Dell handles the integration with CoolIT and customers receive a server with cold plates installed and tubing connected for liquid cooling. PowerEdge DLC systems have a few nuances that set them apart from air-cooled servers. We ventured into relatively unexplored territory with this work. The iDRAC card is different, for example; the DLC version has a cable for leak detection. We successfully completed the conversion process, but installing your own cold plates is not a supported activity.
CoolIT DLC Kit
CoolIT equipped us with a mini system that is typically used for a small proof of concept while its customers work through the process of adding liquid cooling to their data centers. That said, this system can scale up to 10 kW, so it is a great way for those new to liquid cooling to gain some experience with about half a rack. There are three key components in this setup: the cold plates, the rack manifold, and the coolant distribution unit (CDU).
The cold plates are designed for specific use cases in terms of TDP and fit perfectly to the processor or graphics card to be cooled. Their appearance is deceptively simple, and although they contain no pumps or moving parts, their engineering is complex due to steadily increasing TDPs. For comparison, CoolIT recently launched a new line of cold plates capable of supporting up to 1,500 W. The processors in our R760 are relatively modest: the Intel Xeon 8580 processors have a TDP of "only" 350 W each.
Installing the cold plates is very straightforward; the blocks even come with pre-applied thermal paste, making it a very simple kit to install. As noted earlier, there is a different iDRAC card for DLC systems that has the connection point for the leak detection cable coming from the cold plates. The tubing is routed to the rear of the R760, via a different bracket provided with the DLC iDRAC kit.
The cold plates are connected to the manifold via labeled hot/cold connections. The manifold itself is made of stainless steel and the fittings are drip-free quick disconnects. It takes a few seconds to connect the server to the manifold, which is pre-filled. Additionally, our manifold is placed at the rear of the rack, but it can be configured at the front if needed. We have a mini manifold for this use case; a more traditional DLC rack would have a manifold spanning the entire rack. The manifold connects directly to the CDU.
In this loop, the cooling unit (CDU) handles most of the thermal processing; we are using the CoolIT AHx10 here. This is a 5U liquid-to-air CDU capable of handling a 7 kW load at an ambient temperature of 25 °C. CoolIT offers an extension kit to increase its capacity to 10 kW. The chassis incorporates a liquid-to-air heat exchanger and redundant pumps. The CDU, like the manifold, comes pre-filled. We placed it relatively low in the rack, but its location is flexible and depends on the rack configuration.
The AHx10 has a maximum power consumption of 750 W, which contributes to the overall economic conversation around energy savings. The system has an intuitive touchscreen that provides support for remote access. Aside from initially setting the pump pressure, there really is not much to do with the CDU; it is pretty much set it and forget it, and ours has been running for a few weeks without additional intervention.
It is important to note that with this CoolIT system, we are not solving the heat problem itself. We are actually transferring it from the R760's processors to the heat exchanger located inside the coolant distribution unit (CDU). The lab's cooling remains unchanged, as we do not have a domestic hot water system to remove heat from the lab. That said, a small system like this is perfectly suited to a few liquid-cooled servers and could be ideal for a business with a small AI deployment; a server like the Dell PowerEdge XE9640 would be perfectly appropriate.
Even though we still have to deal with the heat from the DLC R760 in our lab, moving to liquid cooling offers several advantages.
Benefits of DLC
When moving from air cooling to liquid cooling, the most important and most obvious benefit is the reduction in fan usage. The R760 still needs airflow for system components such as DRAM and storage, but they do not need to spin as fast. While this makes the server quieter, the best aspect of the DLC loop is the reduction in power consumption. The other thing we found was a bit surprising: the DLC R760 performed slightly better than when it was air-cooled.
To more precisely analyze the R760's power consumption, we set up our Quarch QTL2843 power analysis module. We tested the server with its original air heatsinks, then with the CoolIT cold plates. In order to heavily stress the processors, we performed a Pi calculation to 50 billion decimal places, which places a very heavy load on the processor and DRAM. Our goal was to push the processors to the max in order to force the fans to operate at full speed.
The impact of implementing DLC was immediately obvious. When running the R760 in the air-cooled configuration, the fans spin at 100% during the workload, as expected. With the DLC configuration, the R760 chose to run the fans at 32%, a dramatic drop. This equates to a savings of 200 watts on a single server. It is not just fan speed that stands out; the processors themselves reported about half the temperature with DLC, 41/42°C versus 88/89°C when air-cooled.
But it is not just energy savings that stand out from liquid cooling. We found a small performance improvement that we were not expecting. Thanks to the cold plates providing better cooling, the processor can run at maximum. In the air-cooled configuration, the R760 completed the 50 billion Pi calculation in 369 seconds. In the DLC configuration, the R760 went a bit faster, delivering the calculation in 347 seconds. That represents a performance gain of about 6%, allowing us to get a little more out of the Intel processors.
Final Thoughts
We are just beginning liquid cooling in the lab and are thrilled to have worked with CoolIT on this first effort. The cold plates work perfectly on the PowerEdge R760 and the manifold and CDU assemble and "just work" without any worries or ongoing tinkering. For those who are afraid of introducing liquid into the data center, continued simplicity is essential. We also had no leaks or other more catastrophic events, which was expected: this is enterprise equipment with an extremely low failure rate.
