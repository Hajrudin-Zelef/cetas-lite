# fr-review-ubiquiti-enterprise-nas-review-16-bays-dual-25gbe-and-zfs-for-3999-25cfd5e9

<!-- source: https://www.storagereview.com/fr/review/ubiquiti-enterprise-nas-review-16-bays-dual-25gbe-and-zfs-for-3999 -->

The Enterprise NAS (ENAS) is Ubiquiti's first attempt at offering a lighter enterprise storage solution. This 3U chassis includes 16 hot-swappable drive bays, an eight-core Arm Neoverse N2 processor, 64 GB of ECC memory, two SFP28 25 GbE ports, and two 550 W hot-swappable redundant power supplies, all for a suggested retail price of $3,999. Note: the Ubiquiti store is currently applying a memory surcharge at checkout due to rising memory prices. The final price is therefore higher than the list price until component costs stabilize. The file system used is ZFS, the bays accept all drives, and all software is included. No licenses, no feature unlocks, and no support contracts are required.
The price of a bare NAS may seem high at first glance, but it offers many advantages. Dual 25 GbE connectivity as standard is a major asset that the competition cannot match: Synology's 16-bay RS4021xs+ ships with two 10 GbE RJ45 ports and incorporates 25 GbE connectivity via a PCIe expansion card. The observation is similar for rackmount NAS units of equivalent capacity, where network connectivity above 10 GbE is a paid option. Add redundant power and ECC memory to that, and Ubiquiti meets the criteria that traditionally distinguished SMB NAS from entry-level enterprise arrays. ENAS is available now directly from Ubiquiti (affiliate link).
The ENAS crowns a lineup expansion carried out at a remarkable pace. Ubiquiti has built a solid reputation with its access points and gateways, and its networking offering continues to grow. In the past year alone, we tested the UniFi E7 and E7 Campus WiFi 7 access points, the Cloud Gateway Fiber gateway, and the Dream Router 7 router. Storage is a more recent area. The lineup began with the UNAS Pro, a 2U seven-bay enclosure sold for $499, aimed primarily at loyal UniFi customers. It was then expanded with the compact UNAS 2 and the UNAS Pro 8, which added redundant power and NVMe cache. The ENAS is the first model in the family to include ZFS, ECC memory, native iSCSI, and SAS expansion ports. It is important to note that the file systems differ enough that Ubiquiti specifies that UNAS data cannot be restored directly onto an ENAS; only users, groups, and settings are transferred.
The segment ENAS is entering is not empty. Synology and QNAP, among others, have dominated the SMB NAS market for twenty years, and their platforms offer far more comprehensive software access: application ecosystems, containers, surveillance suites, and high-performance backup tools that UniFi Drive does not currently offer. However, the established players have paved the way for new competitors. Synology spent most of 2025 enforcing a compatibility policy that effectively limited its 2025 Plus series units to only validated drives, primarily Synology-branded, before reversing its decision with DSM 7.3 in the fall. In this context, a 16-bay ZFS system advocating drive openness and with no software licensing represents a perfectly positioned product.
The ideal buyer profile is easy to identify: a small or medium-sized business, or the managed service provider operating it, that already has a medium-to-large UniFi deployment. In these environments, ENAS integrates into the same console as switches, gateways, access points, and cameras, inherits Site Manager for multi-site visibility, and integrates into the same identity model, with UniFi Endpoint managing remote file access. Storage is no longer a separate vendor with a separate user interface and renewal. For businesses that need simple, reliable file and block storage and are already familiar with the UniFi interface, operational continuity relies as much on the product as on the hardware.
A clarification regarding our test unit: we have had the ENAS in the lab since November 2025, and the production hardware revision changed the rear I/O configuration, moving from 10 GbE to the dual 25 GbE interface available today. Everything presented and tested here corresponds to the hardware currently being sold, and the software has evolved rapidly since we received our unit; we will revisit this.

Build and design
The ENAS is designed to different standards than the rest of the UniFi storage lineup, resembling the rackmount servers used in labs more than the UNAS Pro from which it is derived. The differences begin at the drive bays. The ENAS bays use a true eject button with an integrated activity LED, replacing the push-to-eject system of the UNAS Pro. This small change greatly simplifies hot-swapping when the rack is plunged into darkness and a drive needs to be removed. A cover in the upper right of the front panel conceals a connector that we will discuss later in this section.
The aluminum handles on the rackmount brackets serve a dual function: they allow the mounting latches to be unlocked and hold the optional side panel in place. Ubiquiti ships the ENAS with King Slide rails, identical to those used on servers from major manufacturers, and their installation in the rack is tool-free. Installing the inner rail on the chassis is tricky and requires a screwdriver and the screws located underneath the unit. Once this is done, the ENAS slides into place and locks using the latches at the top of each handle.
It is also at the drive trays that the cost savings are apparent. The release lever is plastic, unlike that of the UNAS Pro, which appears to be aluminum; a less judicious choice for a part handled every time a drive is moved. Installing 3.5-inch drives is tool-free, with an optional locking screw. However, 2.5-inch drives still require four screws through the bottom of the tray. If you install SSDs in all 16 bays, that is 64 screws that will prevent you from installing all your drives in the enclosure.
The rear panel features an RJ45 console port, two 25G SFP28 ports, and two SFF-8644 connectors, better known as external Mini-SAS HD. Each Mini-SAS port provides 24 Gbit/s of throughput per bay, supporting up to two UACC-ESE-16 enterprise storage expansion modules, a simple and efficient 16-bay expansion chassis. Ubiquiti describes this bay as a 3U unit with one SFF-8644 port and the same 550 W hot-swappable power supply modules used in the ENAS. It is also compatible with the ENVR-Core. This module was available on Ubiquiti's website when we were finalizing this test, but was not yet available in the store; we were therefore unable to examine it in the lab. With two bays, the system reaches 48 bays, which explains Ubiquiti's claim of raw capacity exceeding one petabyte.
A blank panel to the right of the expansion ports provides access to two M.2 slots. The trays are not included; installing them requires adding Ubiquiti's UACC-SSD tray (affiliate link). UniFi Drive uses these slots as read-only SSD cache, not as a general-purpose fast storage tier. Ubiquiti offers a read-write cache mode on the UNAS lineup, but it is not supported by the ENAS, which is based on ZFS.
Further to the right are the hot-swappable redundant power supplies, two 550 W CRPS modules. Nothing new under the sun for anyone who works on servers, and that is precisely the point: this is the first UniFi storage enclosure whose component compartment integrates seamlessly with the rest of the rack. Each module detaches via a side locking tab and is removed by a retractable handle.
For a $3,999 chassis, the rear panel is very comprehensive. Console, dual 25G port, and SAS expansion on a single plate: a configuration typically found one or two tiers higher, and it is the clearest sign that the ENAS is not simply a bigger UNAS.
Finally, the small port mentioned on the front at the beginning of this section is a magnetic USB Type-C connector for the optional UniFi 3U panel. According to Ubiquiti, this panel features a 4.7-inch touchscreen and RGBW lighting consuming less than 4 W. If screens and indicator lights do not interest you, but you want to conceal your drive bays, Ubiquiti offers the UACC-3U-Bezel-Lite blank panel. Both panels are listed on Ubiquiti's website but are not yet available in the store as we write this; we were therefore unable to test them.
This bezel is not just a decorative element, because the ENAS has no power button. Shutting down the system from the interface is easy; turning it back on, however, is another story. Without a front-panel control, the only way to power on the device near the rack is to unplug both power cords and plug them back in. Remotely, the UniFi interface also does not offer a power control, leaving Wake-on-LAN as the only option. For an enclosure intended for cabinets and small racks that may never be used with a KVM switch, this is important to know before shipping it to a site an hour away.
This enclosure has the usual characteristics of Ubiquiti products: a clean design and well-thought-out practical features: tool-free rail mounting, tool-free 3.5-inch bays, redundant power, and 25 GB of built-in RAM. The Mini-SAS ports are the game-changer, as they transform a 16-bay enclosure into a 48-bay enclosure without replacing any existing components. The lack of a power button is the only real flaw.
ENAS Management
The ENAS is managed via UniFi Drive on UniFi OS. Thus, anyone who has already used a UniFi device will find a familiar user experience. The system appears in the same console as the rest of the UniFi deployment, and Site Manager provides global visibility across all sites. A managed service provider (MSP) can thus monitor the capacity and status of a client's ENAS systems, as well as those of its switches and access points. Local storage administration includes creating ZFS pools in Mirror, RAID-Z1, RAID-Z2, or RAID-Z3 mode, sharing folders via SMB (including Time Machine targets for macOS clients) and NFS, as well as iSCSI LUNs for block workloads. Snapshot scheduling, replication between UniFi storage devices, and backups to third-party cloud services such as Amazon S3, Backblaze B2, Wasabi, Google Drive, OneDrive, and Dropbox complete data protection. Finally, AD/LDAP integration allows user import for businesses that already have directories. Disk encryption is supported, as are ZFS inline compression and deduplication, although Ubiquiti wisely warns that deduplication is resource-intensive and is best reserved for SSD-based pools.
The two external M.2 slots serve as SSD cache rather than primary storage. Ubiquiti offers a read-only cache and a read-write cache on the UNAS lineup, whereas for the ENAS, the SSD cache is read-only and can be equipped with one or two SSDs. The cache deliberately avoids sequential processing of large files, since transferring them via the SSDs would unnecessarily consume endurance. The benefit therefore lies in simultaneous random accesses, office file sharing, and database-type workloads, rather than in large media file transfers. We performed tests with and without cache, and the results below confirm this behavior.
The downside is that the features remain limited compared to DSM or QTS. There is no application ecosystem, no container or virtual machine hosting, and no equivalent to Synology's Active Backup suite; the ENAS is a storage appliance, not yet an application platform. On the other hand, the platform is evolving very rapidly. Since we received our unit, Ubiquiti has regularly deployed UniFi Drive updates, the most recent being version 4.3.10 on July 30. The company has also publicly committed to providing immutable snapshots, multi-site backup orchestration, and expansion bays that, according to Ubiquiti, will allow a single system to exceed one petabyte of raw capacity via the two SFF-8644 ports. Buyers should evaluate the ENAS based on its current features, but it is important to consider its evolution, and the gap is narrowing quarter after quarter.
The UniFi Drive home page presents the entire system in a single view. A status bar on the left displays the following information: network address, uptime, firmware version (UniFi OS 5.1.19 and Drive 4.3.6 during our test), CPU temperature, and a three-position slider for fan speed adjustment. The central panel shows real-time disk throughput, while the lower panel details the usage of each share and offers a column for snapshot scheduling. Each share is accessible in one click for protection. Our RAID-Z2 pool showed 132.76 TB used out of 170.57 TB available, with reserves for deduplication and iSCSI indicated on the capacity bar.
It is at the storage pool level that ZFS decisions are made, and Ubiquiti provides more extensive guidance than most vendors. Illustrated cards at the top of the interface explain the trade-offs between a single RAID group, multiple RAID groups in the same pool, multiple pools with different protection levels, and global spare drives; all useful guardrails for an administrator new to ZFS. Below, our test pool is configured with a single RAID-Z2 group of eight 30 TB 7,200 rpm hard drives, providing protection against two drive failures, with bays 9 through 16 left free.
Block storage has a dedicated interface. LUN creation is facilitated by a quick wizard, and the list shows at a glance the number of hosts, mapping status, usage per LUN, and the underlying pool. Initiator access is controlled by host groups rather than individual LUN configuration; we provisioned two 25 GB LUNs mapped via a single host group for our iSCSI tests. Although more minimalist than a dedicated SAN interface, this interface covers the Proxmox/VMware/Hyper-V shared storage needs for which the ENAS is intended.
The Services page centralizes all necessary protocols: web access, SMB protocol with ready-to-use paths for macOS and Windows, NFS exports with per-host permissions and compression settings, as well as a predefined mount command, and options for Time Machine and Rsync. Note: file services use their own credentials, separate from UniFi accounts. A banner reminds you that they are required for SMB mounting and Time Machine operation.
Day-to-day administration is done in the file management interface, where shared drives are listed with the number of active users, usage, pool assignment, and protection status. This is where our 314 TB share, containing most of the 125 TB of test data, was located alongside two smaller shares. Personal drives are assigned to each user via the "Administrators and Users" section, which allows separating end-user storage from departmental shares without additional configuration.
Performance testing
To test the ENAS on its standard 25 GbE interfaces, we built a single RAID-Z2 group composed of eight Seagate IronWolf Pro 30 TB CMR drives, installed in bays 1 through 8. With the rear half of the chassis left empty, we generated load from a Dell PowerEdge R740 server running Ubuntu Server, equipped with a dual-port NVIDIA ConnectX-4 25 GbE network card, directly connected to the ENAS's SFP28 ports. Our tests consisted of FIO sweeps of 4 KB random transfers and 1 MB sequential transfers, in read and write, across a matrix of queue depths and job counts, to determine the peak of each protocol and its saturation behavior.
We ran tests on six configurations: NFS with asynchronous mounts, NFS with synchronous mounts, and iSCSI, with and without M.2 cache. The cache drives are 1 TB M.2 units provided by Ubiquiti, identified as Kingston OM8SEP41024Q-A0; this is a TLC NVMe component from Kingston's OEM catalog, not an enterprise SSD. For a read-only cache, this is a judicious choice, since the device handles reads and never has to endure sustained writes. The graphs below compare the six configurations; peak values are indicated in the text.
Rand 4K Read IOPS
4 KB random reads show similar performance regardless of NFS configuration, peaking between 89,500 and 90,900 IOPS, regardless of sync mode or cache. With a working set of this size and 64 GB of ECC memory, ZFS handles most of these accesses via ARC; the observed ceiling therefore reflects the network path and protocol stack more than the performance of the underlying hard drives. iSCSI peaks at 40,400 IOPS (39,700 with cache filled), just under half of NFS performance.
Rand 4K Read Latency
Latency confirms this observation. At low queue depth, each configuration responds to 4 KB reads in 0.13 to 0.15 ms, a performance level attributable to memory and network, not disk. Response times evolve predictably as queue depth increases, and NFS configurations remain performant up to moderate depths before experiencing a sharp increase when the system reaches saturation at the extremes of the matrix. Practical conclusion: the ENAS has sufficient headroom for simultaneous small-file accesses generated by an office.
Rand 4K Write IOPS
4 KB random writes reveal notable differences between configurations. Asynchronous NFS peaks at 9,600 IOPS (10,100 with cache), which is respectable for a RAID-Z2 pool of hard drives. On the other hand, by forcing synchronous writes, throughput collapses to a few hundred IOPS, with average latencies on the order of ten milliseconds; each commit is waiting on the pool. UniFi Drive does not offer a dedicated ZFS write logging device (SLOG), which is the usual solution to this type of behavior. Moreover, since the ENAS SSD cache is read-only, the current platform has no mechanism to absorb synchronous writes. iSCSI reached 4,500 IOPS in baseline configuration and 15,200 with the cache filled, a surprisingly large gap for a read cache. The difference between these two results should therefore be interpreted with caution.
Rand 4K Write Latency
The write latency graph reflects the IOPS results: asynchronous NFS shows sub-millisecond latency at the low end of the range and degrades progressively, while synchronous configurations start around 20 ms and then increase. Nothing surprising for ZFS on hard drives without a logging device. However, businesses planning workloads, databases, or NFS datastores with forced synchronization and intensive synchronous write use should examine the asynchronous and iSCSI results and adapt their infrastructure accordingly.
1M Sequential Read GB/s
Large sequential reads are the ENAS's main strength. Asynchronous NFS throughput peaked at 4,170 MB/s, and all four NFS configurations remained above 4,100 MB/s, far exceeding the capabilities of a single 25 GbE link, confirming that the system can simultaneously exploit both SFP28 ports. For a $3,999 enclosure equipped with hard drives, sustained read throughput above 4 GB/s represents remarkable performance, capable of handling backup verification, media extraction, and multi-client streaming with comfortable headroom. iSCSI sequential reads are the weak point: target throughput peaked at only 257 MB/s (227 MB/s with cache) and degraded as load increased, a behavior consistent with system saturation rather than gradual throughput limitation. It appears that a firmware update could resolve this issue; in the meantime, iSCSI on the ENAS is better suited to random-access workloads than to large sequential streams.
1M Sequential Read Latency
Sequential read latency remains stable on NFS, settling around 1.7 ms and evolving as expected with queue depth. The iSCSI curve is the exception, with response times ten times higher at equivalent load, corresponding to the same saturation signature observed on the throughput graph.
1M Sequential Write GB/s
Sequential writes via asynchronous NFS reached 2,567 MB/s (2,654 MB/s with cache), approximately the limit one would expect from the RAID-Z2 pool. Synchronous NFS configurations show the same results as with 4K data, peaking between 137 and 142 MB/s. iSCSI writes showed significantly higher performance than iSCSI reads, at 1,432 MB/s, confirming that the anomaly observed in reads is due to a software issue rather than a hardware limitation.
1M Sequential Write Latency
Write latency concludes this assessment without surprise: asynchronous NFS shows latency below 10 milliseconds at reasonable depths, synchronous NFS falls in the tens-of-milliseconds range, and iSCSI falls between the two. Ultimately, these data indicate that the ENAS is an excellent machine for sequential operations and performant for asynchronous random operations, with synchronous write workloads being the only area requiring planning.
Conclusion
The hardware value is undeniable. For $3,999, the ENAS offers 16 open bays, ZFS with ECC memory, two built-in 25 GbE interfaces, redundant power, and expansion ports that can ultimately reach more than a petabyte of storage. At this price, existing solutions do not offer such a combination without expansion cards and, sometimes, without compatibility restrictions with certain drives. Our tests confirm this claim: sequential NFS read throughput above 4.1 GB/s, write throughput of 2.6 GB/s, and about 90,000 IOPS in 4K random read with cache offer performance far exceeding the needs of most small businesses.
The decision comes down to software. Businesses using Synology or QNAP application ecosystems (containers, surveillance, turnkey backup suites) will find UniFi Drive spartan in comparison. Virtualization designers will need to take into account the iSCSI sequential read anomaly and the synchronous write penalty before opting for this storage solution. But that is not really the target audience for this enclosure. For SMBs with a medium-to-large UniFi deployment that need simple, reliable file and block storage, managed from their usual console, with snapshots, replication, cloud backup, and directory integration included, with no license fees, the ENAS is the most performant storage solution Ubiquiti offers, and the first that established players must take seriously. Given the pace of UniFi Drive updates since we received our unit, we expect the software gap to continue narrowing, and we will reevaluate the platform as immutable snapshots and expansion bays become available.
