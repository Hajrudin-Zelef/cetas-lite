# fr-review-dell-poweredge-xe7740-inside-the-architecture-of-enterprise-ai-inferen-a8c6e9c2

<!-- source: https://www.storagereview.com/fr/review/dell-poweredge-xe7740-inside-the-architecture-of-enterprise-ai-inference -->

The AI infrastructure market is not moving in a single direction; it is splitting into two distinct universes. On one side, cutting-edge training clusters, designed to develop large-scale foundation models, tightly coupled to proprietary architectures and a limited number of accelerators. On the other, the rapidly expanding reality of enterprise inference, where organizations deploy models to serve users, process data in real time, and generate measurable business value. The Dell PowerEdge XE7740 is designed specifically for this second universe.
Key takeaways
- The PowerEdge XE7740 is built for enterprise inference. with a dual-zone cooling system, a structured PCIe Gen5 topology, and a scalable network architecture suited to real production workloads.
- System balance is intentional, Combining the density of 6 Xeon cores, high memory bandwidth, and PCIe Gen 5 E3.S NVMe to support KV cache offload and orchestration.
- Silicon flexibility is fundamental, supporting a wide range of PCIe Gen5 accelerators without requiring infrastructure redesign.
- The platform scales easily over time. from partial GPU installation in a single chassis to distributed inference across multiple racks using eight dedicated Gen5 x16 network slots at the rear.
At the heart of the XE7740 is a strong commitment to chip diversity. Rather than basing the platform on a single accelerator roadmap, Dell designed a system that adapts to availability, cost, and the organization's level of readiness. The XE7740 supports a range of PCIe Gen5 accelerators, including NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000, H100/200, L40S, L4, and A16 GPUs for organizations prioritizing broad compatibility with their ecosystem, as well as Intel Gaudi 3 for teams seeking a more economical and immediately available inference solution. Gaudi 3 accelerators are available today, enabling organizations to move from planning to deployment without the supply lead times that often characterize acceleration strategies.
As inference becomes the dominant AI workload, availability and cost are essential factors. Most enterprises do not train models at the cutting edge of technology. They run inference pipelines, manage medium-sized language models, power retrieval-augmented generation workflows, and deploy computer vision in production. In this context, Gaudi 3 positions itself as one of the most affordable modern inference accelerators on the market, offering a contemporary architecture with high-bandwidth memory and horizontal scalability via Ethernet, without the cost of high-end training GPUs. Within the XE7740, Gaudi 3 aims less to replace existing solutions than to enable sustainable inference deployments.
The platform surrounding the accelerators was designed with the same care. The XE7740 is built on Intel Xeon 6 processors and, in systems dedicated to inference, the processor remains an essential component. The high core count and increased memory bandwidth provide the headroom needed for schedulers, tokenization, preprocessing, and orchestration tasks that sit directly on the critical path of inference. E3.S NVMe storage at the front supports local data storage and KV cache offload, reducing the load on accelerators and improving overall system efficiency. This balanced design reflects the conviction that inference performance depends on the entire system, not just the accelerators.
The XE7740 is designed for seamless scalability. Enterprises can start with a modest configuration, for example two or four accelerators, and benefit immediately without saturating the chassis. As needs grow, the same platform can scale vertically or transform into distributed inference. Eight PCIe Gen5 x16 slots at the rear provide dedicated bandwidth for high-speed networking, enabling the XE7740 to serve as the foundation for building horizontally scalable inference clusters. Optional DPU support further strengthens this flexibility by offloading network and communication tasks as deployments evolve.

Design and manufacturing of the XE7740
Dual-zone architecture: separation of CPU and GPU
One of the most striking features of the XE7740 is its physical separation into two distinct zones for thermal management and power. The upper 1U section houses the processor zone, which includes the two Xeon 6 processors, the 32 DIMM slots, storage, and the DC-SCM management module. This zone is cooled by four high-performance dual-fan modules (40 × 40 × 56 mm) providing an airflow of 47.4 CFM.
The lower 3U section is dedicated to GPUs and includes all acceleration slots with their own cooling system, as well as the PCIe baseboard (PBB), the rear PCIe expansion slots, and OCP NIC connectivity. The GPU zone is equipped with twelve larger high-performance fans (60 × 60 × 56 mm) delivering significantly higher airflow (up to 122.2 CFM per fan) than the processor fans. All fans are hot-swappable. This dual-zone cooling system makes it possible, in a standard 19-inch rack, to ensure that the thermal needs of high-TDP accelerators (up to 600 W per card) do not affect the cooling of the processor and memory, and vice versa.
Dell paid particular attention to optimizing the airflow of the XE7740. Systems with high accelerator density inherently require substantial internal cabling, including GPU auxiliary power cables, PCIe signal cables between the HPM board and the PBB board, as well as ventilation board connections. On the XE7740, these cables are routed along the side walls of the chassis using dedicated brackets and cable covers. Each cable is manufactured to the exact required length; no superfluous cable bundles are found inside the system. By keeping cabling out of the central airflow channel, the design preserves a clear front-to-back airflow and minimizes impedance in the CPU and GPU cooling zones.
In a chassis that can accommodate up to eight 600 W accelerators, even small obstructions in airflow can create localized hot spots and force fans to run at full speed, thereby increasing power consumption and noise levels. Dell's cable management system clears the center of the chassis for direct and optimal airflow over the components that need it most.
Topology and data flow of a PCIe switch
The PCIe subsystem of the XE7740 is built around four PCIe Gen 5 switches on the PCIe baseboard (PBB), designated SW1 through SW4. These switches form the backbone of the system's I/O architecture, connecting accelerators, networking, and storage to the two Xeon 6 processors in a carefully organized topology.
The 16 internal GPU slots are divided into two groups of eight, each group served by a pair of PCIe switches, each connected upstream to a processor. Within each group, pairs of adjacent double-width GPU slots are interleaved between the two switches. On the CPU0 side, switch SW1 serves GPU slots 21 and 25, as well as rear PCIe slots 8 and 9, while switch SW2 serves GPU slots 23 and 27, as well as rear PCIe slots 6 and 7. On the CPU1 side, switch SW3 serves GPU slots 29 and 33, as well as rear PCIe slots 3 and 4, while switch SW4 serves GPU slots 31 and 35, as well as rear PCIe slots 1 and 2.
Each CPU domain therefore has four double-width accelerator slots, four rear NIC/I/O slots, and four of the eight front E3.S NVMe storage bays. This switching topology has important implications for data flow, particularly for RDMA traffic. With each switch hosting both accelerator slots and rear NIC slots, an accelerator and a network card on the same switch can perform RDMA transfers entirely within the switching fabric. Data never needs to transit through the root processor, eliminating the CPU bounce buffer that would otherwise be required when a PCIe device on one root port communicates with a device on another. This reduces latency, avoids consuming valuable processor memory bandwidth, and frees up CPU cycles for other tasks.
Communication within the domain of the same processor, between its two switches, transits through the processor root complex but remains local to that socket. By contrast, inter-processor communication must use the UPI links between the two Xeon 6 processors. The 6787P provides four UPI 2.0 links at 24 GT/s each, offering significant inter-socket bandwidth. However, traffic between an accelerator in the switch bank of processor 0 and a network card in the switch bank of processor 1 will inherently exhibit higher latency than local transfers within the same switch or the same socket.
The switches themselves are not directly interconnected. All inter-switch traffic transits through the main processor; it is therefore important to understand the relationships between GPU slots, NIC slots, storage, and CPU sockets. To simplify this complexity for enterprises, Dell offers validated and optimized configurations for the most common accelerators.
Dual-zone power
The power supply of the XE7740 reflects its thermal architecture with a rather unusual dual-zone power design. The system supports up to eight hot-swappable power supply units, divided into two zones: zone 1 (CPU zone) houses power supplies 1 and 2, while zone 2 (GPU zone) houses power supplies 3 through 8.
The system requires at least one power supply per zone to ensure BMC power and backup power. In the event of a power loss in either zone while the system is operating, the system shuts down immediately to prevent data loss. The zones are interdependent for their operation, even though they are physically and electrically separated. For full redundancy, Dell recommends a 1+1 configuration in the CPU zone and a 3+3 configuration in the GPU zone, meaning that all eight power supply slots must be occupied for a fully redundant deployment.
Dell AIOps, management, and enterprise reliability
Dell's PowerEdge platform has built a solid reputation in the industry for its reliability and ease of maintenance. Enterprise customers regularly highlight the same points: PowerEdge systems are built to last, Dell's support service resolves issues quickly, and the management tools are performant and seamlessly integrated. The XE7740 continues this tradition, and Dell has made significant progress in hardware management and security with this generation.
iDRAC 10
The XE7740 server ships with Dell's next-generation iDRAC 10, a major redesign of iDRAC 9, which was already performant and used by Dell customers for years. Integrated as a secure data center management module (DC-SCM) compliant with the OCP DC-MHS standard, iDRAC 10 is not merely a firmware update; it is new hardware. The controller integrates four cores clocked at 1 GHz with a 64-bit architecture and 2 GB of DDR4 memory (double the previous generation), delivering significantly superior performance and responsiveness for management operations.
In terms of security, iDRAC 10 introduces several notable improvements. The platform offers enhanced cryptographic support at all levels, including SHA-384 and SHA-512 authentication and quantum-resistant AES-256 encryption, as the industry prepares for post-quantum cryptographic threats. A dedicated integrated security enclave within the iDRAC 10 chip handles cyber-resilience functions, such as device-level attestation and Dell's custom root of trust. This hardware root of trust ensures that all firmware (BIOS, iDRAC, and component firmware) is cryptographically verified before execution, protecting against supply chain attacks and firmware tampering.
Secure component verification ensures that systems shipped by Dell comply with customer specifications in terms of components and configurations, ensuring their integrity from manufacturing to deployment. The latest iDRAC 10 firmware update also offers a redesigned, modular user interface, simplifying day-to-day administration.
OpenManage Enterprise
For managing large-scale IT fleets, Dell OpenManage Enterprise centralizes monitoring, firmware updates, and configuration management for all PowerEdge deployments. Among the important new features for AI-dedicated deployments, OME now offers direct visibility into GPU and accelerator statistics: power consumption, temperature, utilization, error counts, etc., without requiring vendor-specific tools. For enterprises managing dozens, or even hundreds, of XE7740 nodes in an inference cluster, this unified management platform represents a significant operational simplification.
Intel Xeon 6
At the heart of the XE7740 are two Intel Xeon 6 6787P processors, the flagship of the Xeon 6700P series. Based on the Granite Rapids architecture and using Intel 3 nm technology, the 6787P offers 86 processing cores (172 threads) per socket for a TDP of 350 W, with a base frequency of 2.0 GHz and a turbo frequency of 3.8 GHz.
What makes Granite Rapids particularly relevant for AI infrastructure is the combination of a large number of cores and its memory subsystem. Each 6787P processor offers eight DDR5 memory channels up to 6,400 MT/s. With a dual-socket XE7740 server equipped with 32 DIMM modules, the system can be configured for a total memory capacity of up to 4 TB.
Memory capacity and bandwidth are essential for AI workloads, particularly when using key-value cache offload. As complex language models increase context length, the key-value cache grows proportionally and can consume a significant amount of accelerator memory. Offloading part of the key-value cache to system memory or fast storage allows more efficient use of the accelerator's HBM for active computation, reducing time to first token (TTFT) for multi-turn conversations.
It is also worth mentioning the AMX tensor units of the Xeon 6, which handle a significant share of the processor-side work. These include preprocessing, tokenization, and hybrid inference tasks involving matrix operations. This proves particularly useful with inference frameworks like SGLang, which use the processor for radix tree KV cache management and scheduling without overhead.
Intel Gaudi 3 expansion cards: Competitive inference at scale
Intel's Gaudi 3 is the company's flagship AI accelerator, launched in the fourth quarter of 2024. Intel positions these accelerators very aggressively, rather than competing head-on with high-end data center training accelerators. The Gaudi 3 clearly targets the inference segment.
Inference of transformer-based models, in all current popular LLMs, is fundamentally memory-bound. During the decoding phase of autoregressive generation, the model generates tokens one by one, reading the model weights and KV cache entries for each token produced. The bottleneck is not in compute power, but in memory bandwidth, that is, the speed at which the accelerator can transfer data from HBM memory to the compute engines.
The Gaudi 3 carries 128 GB of HBM2e memory providing 3.7 TB/s of bandwidth. Its architecture is based on TSMC's 5 nm process and uses a dual-die design: two identical silicon dies connected by a high-bandwidth interconnect, presenting themselves as a single device to software. Compute power is organized into four deep learning cores (DCORE), each comprising 2 matrix multiplication engines (MME), 16 transactional compute units (TPC), and 24 MB of local SRAM cache. The 96 MB of integrated SRAM provides a total internal bandwidth of 12.8 TB/s. The accelerator also integrates 14 dedicated media decoders (H.265, H.264, JPEG, VP9), enabling fast image preprocessing for multimodal workloads.
A large portion of the cutting-edge open-source AI models released today are either natively trained in FP8 or are hybrid models combining FP8 (E4M3) and BF16 weights. The Gaudi 3 offers native FP8 acceleration for these models thanks to its 8 matrix multiplication engines and 64 tensor processing cores, delivering an FP8 compute power of 1.8 PFlops.
The Gaudi 3 also integrates RDMA over Converged Ethernet (RoCEv2) technology with 24 200 GbE ports on the OAM version, directly integrated into the chip. Although the PCIe expansion card used in the XE7740 does not expose all these ports in the same way, the various expansion card versions allow up to four cards to be connected for faster communication.

vLLM Online Serving Performance
To evaluate the inference capabilities of the Dell XE7740 server equipped with Intel Gaudi 3 accelerators, we compared the online serving performance of vLLM across a range of common models, covering different architectures, parameter counts, and precision formats. Each model was tested across three workload profiles, with the number of concurrent requests ranging from 1 to 128.
LLM inference consists of two distinct phases. The prefill phase processes all input tokens in parallel before any output token generation, making it a compute-intensive operation proportional to the number of input tokens. The decode phase then generates output tokens one by one (autoregressively). Each new token requires reading the entire model weights from memory, but the computation per token is relatively low, making it memory-bandwidth-bound.
These two phases stress fundamentally different parts of the accelerator, so we test three workload profiles that shift the balance between them:
- Equal (1024 input tokens/1024 output tokens) represents balanced chat interactions.
- Prefill Heavy (8192 inputs/1024 outputs) simulates retrieval-augmented generation or long-context summarization, in which the system must process large input contexts.
- Decode Heavy (1024 inputs/8192 outputs) represents long-form content generation where sustained memory bandwidth determines throughput.
In this section, we focus on two main metrics: total token throughput (in tokens per second), which reflects the system's overall capacity under load, and time to first token (TTFT), which measures the elapsed time between submitting a request and receiving the first generated token. Since the model must complete the prefill phase before emitting the first token, TTFT is directly related to the accelerator's compute power. Thus, a prefill-heavy scenario (combined with TTFT) is a particularly relevant indicator of the raw compute capabilities of Gaudi 3 accelerators, since the system must process all 8,192 input tokens before the user receives a response.
Conversely, the decode-heavy scenario tests the memory bandwidth of the accelerators, since the system must sustain high throughput for thousands of generated tokens. TTFT is crucial for interactive applications where users wait for a response before streaming begins. A system can achieve excellent throughput with large batch processing but remain slow if TTFT becomes too high; both metrics are therefore important for production deployments.
Note regarding FP8 precision results: although Intel Gaudi 3 accelerators include native FP8 compute acceleration (and FP8 should, in theory, offer higher throughput than BF16), the FP8 performance measured in our tests is lower than that obtained with BF16. This is not a hardware limitation, but rather a software maturity issue within Intel's vLLM build. The version we tested (vLLM 2.7.1 installer on Gaudi Docker 1.22.2) has not yet fully optimized its FP8 execution paths. Intel offers a new plugin-based vLLM version, currently in beta, which could resolve many of these performance issues.
Lama 3.1 8B Instruct
Llama 3.1 8B Instruct is a dense transformer model from Meta, meaning that every parameter is active for each generated token. With 8 billion parameters, it is among the most widely used open-source models. Models of this size are commonly used for everyday tasks such as summarizing short documents, drafting emails and messages, answering simple questions, and powering basic chatbot interactions where speed and cost-effectiveness take priority over reasoning complexity.
We tested this model with two configurations: TP1 (a single accelerator) and TP4 (all four Gaudi 3 accelerators). On TP1, the model reaches a total throughput of approximately 8,000 tok/s for 128 concurrent requests at equal workload, increasing from approximately 250 tok/s for a single request to a much higher throughput. The prefill-heavy scenario reveals interesting behavior: while TP1 caps at approximately 7,000 tok/s, TP4 exceeds 17,900 tok/s for 128 concurrent requests, thanks to the use of additional accelerators to process the large input context more efficiently.
In terms of latency for a single user, TP1 shows lower TTFT under low concurrency (67 ms versus 98 ms for TP4), reflecting the overhead of coordinating across four accelerators for a model that easily fits on one. However, as load increases, TP4 takes a clear advantage. With 128 concurrent requests, TP4 maintains TTFT at approximately 2 seconds for equal and decode workloads, while TP1 reaches 3.7 seconds and 6.6 seconds respectively. It is in the prefill-intensive scenario that the gap is most pronounced: TP1 reaches nearly 47 seconds of TTFT at 128 requests, while TP4 maintains it at approximately 11 seconds.
Lama 3.1 70B Instruct
Llama 3.1 70B Instruct is the largest dense model in Meta's Llama 3.1 family. With its 70 billion parameters, it offers significantly superior instruction-following and multilingual capabilities compared to the 8B variant. Models of this scale are perfectly suited to more intensive workloads, such as customer support agents, research assistants performing complex tasks, analysis of complex documents, and tasks requiring the maintenance of coherent context during extended interactions.
We tested this model with TP2 and TP4 configurations. The throughput difference between the two is considerable. With 128 concurrent requests, TP4 reaches approximately 3,600 tok/s at equal workload and peaks at nearly 4,600 tok/s in the prefill-intensive scenario. This represents approximately 4.4 and 4.6 times the throughput of TP2, which caps at approximately 816 tok/s and 1,005 tok/s respectively. Even with a decode-focused workload, throughput reaches approximately 1,960 tok/s on TP4, versus 593 tok/s on TP2.
Regarding TTFT (time to first token), TP2 struggles to handle the large prefill workload, reaching an impressive response time of 496 seconds for 128 concurrent requests, making it practically unusable for interactive applications. TP4 reduces this time to approximately 73 seconds. For equal and decode workloads, TP4 maintains TTFT at approximately 10 seconds for 128 requests, while TP2 reaches 50 and 29 seconds respectively. Under low concurrency, TP4 provides first-token responses in approximately 160 ms for the equal workload, versus 486 ms for TP2.
Qwen3 Coder 30B-A3B Instructions
Qwen3 Coder 30B-A3B is one of the most popular coding models for local inference deployments. It uses a Mixture of Experts (MoE) architecture. Unlike dense models, where every parameter participates in each iteration, MoE models route each token through a small subset of specialized expert networks. Qwen3 Coder retains a full 30-billion-parameter model at BF16 precision while activating only 3 billion parameters per generated token. This sparsely activated model offers the quality of a much larger network while requiring only a fraction of the compute power per token, making it extremely efficient on hardware that supports routing overhead. For end users, this model is perfectly suited to everyday coding assistance: standard code generation, function completion, code explanation, writing unit tests, and handling common development tasks that benefit from a specialized model without requiring complex reasoning.
We tested three configurations: TP1 BF16, TP1 FP8, and TP4 BF16. With 128 concurrent requests, TP4 BF16 stands out with approximately 14,300 tok/s in the prefill-intensive scenario, the highest throughput recorded for all models in our test suite. TP1 BF16 follows with approximately 6,900 tok/s in the same scenario, while TP1 FP8 lags with approximately 3,360 tok/s. At equal workload, the gap narrows slightly: TP4 reaches 6,073 tok/s, TP1 BF16 5,718 tok/s, and TP1 FP8 2,101 tok/s. As noted in the FP8 note above, the lower FP8 performance reflects the current state of Intel's vLLM build rather than a hardware bottleneck.
TTFT remains low thanks to the low activation frequency. TP4 BF16 shows a first-token latency of approximately 140 ms with a single user and maintains approximately 2.6 seconds with 128 concurrent requests under the same load conditions. TP1 BF16 is comparable under low concurrency (106 ms) but reaches 3.1 seconds at full load. The prefill-heavy scenario again clearly differentiates the configurations: TP4 reaches approximately 18 seconds with 128 requests, TP1 BF16 reaches 57 seconds, and TP1 FP8 extends to 72 seconds.
Qwen3 235B-A22B Thinking
The largest model in our test suite, Qwen3 235B-A22B Thinking, is a massive MoE reasoning model, with 235 billion total parameters and 22 billion active parameters per token. In addition to its impressive scale, this model incorporates chain-of-thought reasoning capabilities, allowing it to break down complex problems step by step before arriving at a solution, at the cost of a larger number of decoding tokens. It is thus particularly suited to the most demanding tasks: advanced code generation and debugging, mathematical problem solving, multi-step logical reasoning, and complex automated workflows where accuracy takes priority over raw speed. This model requires TP4 precision to operate and we tested it with BF16 and FP8 precisions.
BF16 significantly outperforms FP8 across the board. With 128 concurrent requests, BF16 reaches approximately 2,750 tok/s under heavy prefill workload and 2,500 tok/s under equal workload, while FP8 reaches approximately 1,784 tok/s and 516 tok/s respectively. The FP8 variant also experienced timeouts in the decode-intensive scenario at higher concurrency levels (more than 32 requests).
For TTFT, BF16 shows an initial response time of approximately 340 ms for a single request of identical length and reaches approximately 6.9 seconds with 128 concurrent requests. This behavior is rather responsive for a model of this scale. FP8 is approximately twice as slow, with an initial response time of 615 ms and a maximum of approximately 11.2 seconds at full load. The most demanding scenario is the one requiring heavy prefill: BF16 reaches 168 seconds and FP8 80 seconds with 128 requests.
Who is this for?
Demand for inference is not letting up. Whether deploying models internally to accelerate development, integrating AI into customer-facing products, or setting up automation pipelines running 24/7, compute needs continue to increase. And this demand comes with a well-known bottleneck: supply. Lead times for the most popular accelerators can reach several months, blocking projects that are already funded and staffed.
The XE7740, configured with Intel Gaudi 3, directly addresses this constraint. Gaudi 3 accelerators are available now and Intel provides validated deployment templates enabling teams to go from unboxing to commissioning in a few hours. Dell further simplifies access to the solution through trial programs that integrate XE7740 systems directly into your environment, allowing you to validate their performance with your real workloads, data, and infrastructure before any full deployment. This combination of immediate availability, rapid implementation, and low-risk evaluation makes the Gaudi 3 configuration particularly attractive for organizations that need inference capacity today and cannot afford to wait in allocation queues.
That said, the XE7740 is not a single-accelerator platform. Thanks to Dell's commitment to chip diversity, this same platform is available with a wide choice of options and all the popular accelerators on the market. The optimal choice depends entirely on the workload. Video processing pipelines, for example, are perfectly suited to NVIDIA L4 processors, and the XE7740 can be equipped with 16 L4 GPUs and 8 PCIe Gen5 x16 NIC slots for an uncompromising streaming and transcoding system. Enterprises running mixed AI workloads across their different departments can standardize their configuration on the XE7740 chassis and simply adapt the accelerator configuration for each deployment, simplifying fleet management and adapting compute power to the task.
Conclusion
The Dell PowerEdge XE7740 is designed to meet the demands of enterprise inference. Its dual-zone thermal architecture, structured PCIe topology, high-bandwidth memory architecture, and scalable network capacity form a system capable of supporting sustained production workloads. These design choices are not accidental; they reflect an infrastructure model where inference runs continuously, scales predictably, and integrates seamlessly with existing data center operations. In this evaluation, Intel Gaudi 3 demonstrates that the XE7740 delivers optimal inference performance today on dense and multi-expert (MoE) architectures, with predictable scaling behavior and high memory-bound throughput. Software optimizations will continue to improve, but the platform architecture already rests on solid foundations.
More importantly, the XE7740 establishes a sustainable model for enterprise AI infrastructure. Organizations can standardize their infrastructure through a consistent chassis, management stack, and deployment model, while evolving their acceleration strategy over time. As models grow, workloads diversify, and inference becomes more deeply integrated into business operations, the need for stable and adaptable infrastructure will only grow. The PowerEdge XE7740 is designed to meet this evolution. It offers the architectural balance, operational maturity, and expansion headroom needed for enterprise AI, from rapid adoption to long-term integration.
