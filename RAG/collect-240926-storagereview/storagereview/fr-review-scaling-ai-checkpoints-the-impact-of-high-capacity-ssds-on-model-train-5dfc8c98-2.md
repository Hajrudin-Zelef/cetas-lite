# fr-review-scaling-ai-checkpoints-the-impact-of-high-capacity-ssds-on-model-train-5dfc8c98

Checkpointing is essential to AI model training, as it ensures resilience, efficiency, and the ability to resume or refine training from saved states. However, the demands of modern AI workloads, with increasingly complex models and extensive training datasets, are pushing storage to its limits.
The role of checkpoints in AI workflows
Checkpointing in AI training is an essential process that involves periodically recording the complete state of the model during training. This state includes the model's weights and parameters, optimizer states, learning rate schedules, and training metadata. Checkpointing creates a complete snapshot of the training process at specific intervals, ensuring training continuity and recovery in the event of interruption.
Checkpoints are typically set at intervals based on iterations (for example, every thousand training steps). Modern LLM training, which can span weeks or months and consume enormous computational resources, relies heavily on these checkpoints as a safety net against potential failures. For example, training a model like the GPT-4 class can generate checkpoints ranging from several hundred gigabytes to several terabytes, depending on the model size and training configuration.
The primary purpose of checkpoints goes beyond simple backup functionality. It is an essential mechanism for training resilience, allowing training to resume from the last saved state rather than starting from scratch in the event of a system crash, power outage, or hardware issues. In addition, checkpoints are invaluable for model analysis, allowing researchers to examine the model's evolution at different stages of training and potentially revert to previous states if performance degradation is detected.
Write patterns during checkpointing are particularly interesting from a storage perspective. When a checkpoint is triggered, the system must write massive amounts of data in a burst pattern. This creates a distinctive I/O profile characterized by periods of relatively low storage activity during training computations, followed by intense high-bandwidth write operations during checkpointing. These write operations are typically sequential and can benefit considerably from storage systems optimized for high-bandwidth sequential writes.
Different parallelism strategies in distributed training can have a significant impact on checkpointing behavior. These parallelism strategies affect when checkpoints occur during training and which part of the model is checkpointed. In modern distributed training configurations, multiple GPUs may simultaneously write different parts of the same layer, creating complex I/O patterns. This parallel write capability is essential for efficiency, but it requires careful coordination and robust storage systems capable of handling simultaneous write operations while maintaining data consistency. The storage system must be able to efficiently handle these simultaneous writes, as any bottleneck in this process can lead to overall training delays.
Slow checkpointing can create significant bottlenecks during training, because the entire training process must be interrupted while the checkpoint is written to storage. For example, in a large-scale training configuration, if checkpointing takes 30 minutes every few hours, this can result in several hours of accumulated downtime over the entire training period. This has a direct impact on training efficiency and increases operational costs, especially in cloud environments where computing resources are billed by time.
Through faster checkpointing, teams can also afford to create checkpoints more frequently, thereby reducing the maximum potential data loss in the event of a failure. This enables more aggressive training approaches and better experimental iteration cycles. In addition, fast checkpoint loading times facilitate faster experimentation with different training configurations and model architectures, because researchers can more easily restore previous states to try alternative approaches.
The storage system's ability to efficiently handle these checkpointing operations becomes a critical factor in the overall training infrastructure. High-performance storage solutions capable of handling both the burst write patterns of checkpointing and the sustained read/write operations of training can have a significant impact on the total time and cost of training large language models. Thus, the performance characteristics of the storage subsystem, particularly in handling large sequential writes and maintaining high and consistent bandwidth, are crucial considerations in designing LLM training infrastructure.
For this report, we wanted to evaluate SSD performance for AI checkpointing, assessing the benefits of the latest Gen5 SSDs when checkpoint speed is critical, compared with the largest QLC SSDs on the market, which can store a large number of checkpoints if that is more beneficial for the model being trained.
Checkpoint performance – Comparative analysis with DLIO
To evaluate the real-world performance of Solidigm SSDs in AI training environments, we used the DLIO (Data and Learning Input/Output) test tool. Developed by Argonne National Laboratory, DLIO is specifically designed to test I/O patterns in deep learning workloads. It provides insight into how storage systems handle data backup, data ingestion, and the challenges associated with model training.
Working with DLIO, we sought to measure disk throughput, latency, and reliability in checkpoint-intensive scenarios. Although these tests were performed on the 61.44 TB D5-P5336, initial performance data showed that the 122 TB Solidigm D5-P5336 offers a similar performance profile. We also included results from a TLC-based D7-PS1010 to show the benefits of PCIe Gen5 in this test. We chose these two drives to show both angles on checkpointing, one being the fastest possible checkpoint time and the other storing the most checkpoints on a single SSD.
The platform chosen for this work was our Dell PowerEdge R760 running Ubuntu 22.04.02 LTS. We used version 2.0 of the DLIO benchmark dated August 13, 2024. Our system configuration is described below:
- 2 x Intel Xeon Gold 6430 (32 cores, 2.1 GHz)
- 16 x 64GB DDR5-4400
- 480 GB Dell BOSS SSD
- Gen5 JBOF serial cables
  - 7.68 TB Solidigm D7-PS1010 SSD
  - 61.44 TB Solidigm D5-P5336 SSD
To ensure that our comparative analysis reflects real-world scenarios, we based our tests on the LLAMA 3.1 405B model architecture, implementing checkpoints via torch.save() to capture model parameters, optimizer states, and layer states. Our configuration simulated an 8-GPU system, implementing a hybrid parallelism strategy with 4-way tensor parallelism and 2-way pipeline parallelism distributed across the eight GPUs. This configuration resulted in checkpoint sizes of 1,636 GB, representative of the training requirements of modern large-scale language models.
Our test process for the DLIO checkpoint workload consisted of filling each drive to a similar utilization level. For the 61.44 TB Solidigm D5-P5336, each pass included 33 checkpoint intervals, for a total of 54 TB. The smaller 7.68 TB D7-PS1010 comfortably accommodated three checkpoint intervals, with a total footprint of 4.9 TB. One additional checkpoint could fit on the D7-PS1010, although it increased its utilization slightly above what we wanted.
The DLIO checkpoint workload produced interesting results when we compared the 61.44 TB QLC Gen4 D5-P5336 with the 7.68 TB TLC Gen5 D7-PS1010. During the first pass, as the drives filled up, we observed a larger performance gap between the two SSD models. The faster Gen5 PS1010 completed each checkpoint in an average of 464 seconds, compared with 623 seconds for the Gen4 P5336. In the second and third passes, the gap narrowed to 579 and 587 seconds for the PS1010 and to 676 and 680 seconds for the P5336.
