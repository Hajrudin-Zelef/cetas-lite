# fr-review-dell-powerprotect-data-domain-all-flash-appliance-the-intel-powered-al-145f5125

<!-- source: https://www.storagereview.com/fr/review/dell-powerprotect-data-domain-all-flash-appliance-the-intel-powered-all-flash-foundation-for-cyber-resilience -->

Cyber resilience infrastructure occupies a central place in enterprise architecture, unlike primary storage. In the event of a system failure, data corruption, or ransomware blocking access to the environment, restoration ultimately depends on the backup platform. This reality has sustained strong demand for dedicated backup appliances, despite the rise of cloud alternatives. According to the IDC 4Q25 Purpose-Built Backup Appliance (PBBA) Tracker study, Dell dominates the market in terms of revenue, thanks to the performance of its Dell PowerProtect Data Domain range (based on the Intel® Xeon® processor) and its more than 15,000 active customer deployments worldwide.
We analyzed the Dell PowerProtect Data Domain DD9410 and DD9910 in detail, both equipped with the Intel Xeon Scalable processor, at the time of their launch. We then examined the Data-Less Head architecture, the DDOS software stack, the hardware root of trust and the secure boot chain, as well as the performance gains compared to the previous Data Domain DD9400/DD9900 generation. The Dell PowerProtect Data Domain DD9910F all-flash system builds on these same foundations without rethinking them. The software architecture, deduplication engine, Data Domain Boost (DD Boost) ecosystem, and security features remain identical across the entire range. Only the storage medium changes: the all-flash system replaces hard drives with flash memory across all its components, thus targeting workloads where this transition has the most direct operational impact.
The value of flash storage for a cyber resilience platform differs from that for primary storage. Flash changes the game in terms of restore and replication throughput, as well as the speed of integrity validation through data analysis in isolated data recovery chambers. These are the areas where enterprise business continuity SLAs are tightening the most, and they are at the heart of this analysis.
We examine the All-Flash appliance, starting with its hardware design, including how Intel's QAT technology enables hardware-accelerated compression, then addressing how flash technology concretely changes restore, replication, and cyber recovery workflows, how the appliance's data reduction shrinks the storage footprint and total cost of ownership, and where the appliance fits in Dell's current PowerProtect cyber resilience portfolio.
Inside the Data Domain DD9910F all-flash appliance
The PowerProtect Data Domain All-Flash system takes up the architectural model introduced with the current generation of PowerProtect Data Domain, combining a 2U controller with external storage shelves. Dell calls this design a "Data-Less Head" architecture, in which the controller handles compute, metadata services, and system orchestration, while backup data resides on external storage. The separation of the compute layer and the storage layer allows the system to scale while maintaining consistent performance across the Data Domain range.
The controller occupies a 2U chassis powered by two 5th Gen Intel Xeon Scalable processors, which handle the Data Domain file system and the integrated deduplication engine. Large DDR5 memory pools support the platform's metadata and data reduction workloads. Intel Quick Assist Technology (QAT) is integrated directly into the 5th Gen Xeon silicon, enabling hardware-accelerated compression within DDOS without consuming a PCIe slot or requiring a dedicated expansion card. By offloading compression to Intel QAT's integrated accelerators, the Intel Xeon processor platform keeps cores available for deduplication metadata processing and data path operations, which directly affect restore throughput and replication speed. Compute costs can increase significantly with throughput. PCIe Gen5 connectivity in the rear expansion slots provides the bandwidth headroom needed to support high-end high-density network configurations.
At the rear of the chassis, the system reveals the networking and expansion capabilities required for large-scale enterprise backup environments. Multiple PCIe slots support high-speed network adapters, including 10 GbE, 25 GbE, and 100 GbE configurations. These options allow the appliance to adapt network throughput to deployment needs, while dedicated connectivity links the controller to external storage shelves. Power is provided by two redundant power supplies configured to maintain operation in the event of a power supply failure.
The all-flash system's backup capacity is provided by the FS240 flash chassis, a 2U storage shelf equipped with enterprise SSDs. Each shelf offers high flash capacity in a form factor compatible with the controller chassis. The platform supports up to four FS240 shelves, allowing the system to scale from 272 TB to 1.1 PB of usable capacity depending on configuration. Each shelf includes redundant power and cooling components, in line with enterprise availability requirements.
In a 544 TB configuration, the platform consists of a 2U controller and two 2U flash shelves, for a total footprint of 6U. An equivalent Data Domain DD9910 deployment, based on hard drives, occupies approximately 10U of rack space. This difference partly explains Dell's claims regarding improved rack efficiency and reduced power consumption thanks to this all-flash design.
Flash changes the recovery equation
Restore operations, replication throughput, and integrity validation based on analysis in cyber recovery chambers depend heavily on read performance and rapid access to deduplication metadata. These are the use cases where performance is most critical. The All-Flash system's proprietary file system architecture, combined with performance optimization, ensures optimal disk performance.
Dell claims restore performance up to four times faster for its all-flash appliance than for the disk-based DD9910, at equivalent capacity. This advantage is due to overall system performance, thanks to flash storage technology and the Intel Xeon Scalable processor, which handles the intensive metadata processing required by restore operations.
Since both shelves share the same hardware controller, all gains are directly accessible in the all-flash configuration of the all-flash appliance. In the tests presented during the platform's unveiling, the restore workload included multiple data streams and successive backup generations with realistic change rates applied between cycles.
In the test scenario common to Dell and Intel, restore throughput rises rapidly, then stabilizes at approximately 60 TB per hour and remains at that level for the entire duration of the operation. The hard-drive-based DD9910 shows a more gradual ramp-up and lower sustained throughput under equivalent workloads. This difference is explained by how flash memory handles read-intensive operations compared to a hard drive, particularly when a restore workload requires rapid access to many deduplicated data segments in the storage pool. Data Domain systems store unique data segments only once and reference them via metadata pointers for subsequent backups. During a restore, the system must locate and reassemble these segments to reconstruct the requested data set. Since this process involves many read operations and metadata lookups, low-latency flash storage can significantly accelerate the reconstruction of large backup images.
Replication throughput also benefits from faster read speeds on the flash storage layer. Dell claims replication up to twice as fast for the All-Flash appliance compared to the DD9910 at equivalent capacity. In Data Domain environments, replication typically involves reading data from the source appliance and transferring it to a secondary system, often located at a disaster recovery site or in a cyber security digital vault.
Reduced replication time has operational implications that go beyond a simple throughput gain. In disaster recovery architectures, backup data is replicated to an isolated vault environment designed to protect against ransomware. This vault is exposed to production systems only during strictly controlled replication windows. Shorter replication windows reduce the period during which the vault is accessible to the production environment, thereby limiting the duration of potential exposure.
Dell also reports improvements in analytical validation workloads executed within cyber recovery vaults. In internal tests using the CyberSense analysis tool, the company found analytical performance up to 2.8 times faster on the All-Flash system than on the disk-based DD9910.
CyberSense analysis workflows examine backup data to verify its integrity and detect potential signs of corruption or ransomware activity before recovery operations are launched. Since the validation process involves analyzing large volumes of backup data, the improved read performance of flash media reduces the time needed to confirm that a recovery point is healthy before initiating a restore.
Power, space, and the operational case for flash
Improved performance is the most visible benefit of replacing disks with flash memory in a cyber resilience platform. However, efficiency gains are a strong argument for enterprises managing infrastructure at scale. Dell's internal tests, comparing the DD9910F (with Intel processor) to the DD9910 (disk-based, also with Intel processor) at equivalent capacity, indicate reduced power consumption of up to 80% and a rack footprint reduced by 40%. Taken individually, these figures seem like technical specifications. In the context of a multi-site data protection deployment, they open up an entirely different debate.
A large enterprise operating Data Domain appliances at a primary site, a disaster recovery site, and an isolated cybersecurity data center manages three distinct infrastructures. Power and cooling costs at each of these sites represent real expenses, and rack density affects a given building's capacity without expansion. Moving from approximately 10U to 6U per fully configured system, combined with significantly reduced power consumption, changes the infrastructure math for organizations with limited data center capacity or that actively manage their energy costs as part of their total cost of ownership calculation.
The data recovery vault use case deserves particular emphasis. By design, the infrastructure of these vaults is isolated and dedicated, often deployed in a dedicated space or colocation facility, where power consumption and space are billed directly. In this context, reducing the physical footprint and energy consumption of the infrastructure without compromising recovery performance represents a significant operational advantage, which grows as retention requirements increase and vault capacity expands.
Data reduction and effective capacity
Although the introduction of flash memory changes the performance of the All-Flash appliance, its underlying efficiency model still relies on the Data Domain deduplication engine. Dell now claims data reduction ratios of up to 75:1 for the current generation of PowerProtect Data Domain based on Intel Xeon, confirming the platform's long-standing commitment to energy efficiency.
This figure is not purely theoretical. In tests conducted by Prowess Consulting with a representative VMware backup workload, the Data Domain All-Flash system achieved data reduction of approximately 75:1, while competing systems required up to 3.8 times more physical capacity to protect the same data set. Over time, as additional backup copies accumulated and redundancy increased, the effective reduction ratio continued to improve, reaching 78:1 after seven days and exceeding 100:1 after two weeks in that test environment.
This behavior reflects how deduplication works in backup environments. The first backup copies contain a higher proportion of unique data, while subsequent backups introduce incremental changes that can be efficiently deduplicated against existing data sets. As a result, effective data reduction improves over time as retention periods lengthen.
From a practical standpoint, this reduction capability is essential for the All-Flash appliance to reconcile the economic benefits of flash with large-scale backup requirements. While the All-Flash appliance's gross usable capacity ranges from 272 TB to 1.1 PB, the Data Domain DD9910 disk-based storage system offers higher gross capacities, up to 2.1 PB. Effective capacity can be significantly higher in both cases, depending on data characteristics, change rates, and retention policies. Dell views this efficiency as a key factor in reducing overall infrastructure footprint, power consumption, and long-term storage costs.
Data Domain System Manager management and interface
Management of the All-Flash system is done via Data Domain System Manager, the same web interface used for the entire Data Domain range. Since DDOS 8.7 is identical across all products in the range, the management experience is the same for everyone, with no additional training required, and the All-Flash system integrates easily into existing workflows without requiring a new operational model.
The dashboard displays the information most frequently used by administrators without requiring navigation: file system capacity, used and available space, compression factor, last write date, active alerts, and licensed services status.
Real-time performance graphs are accessible with one click and cover CPU utilization, DD Boost throughput, active connections, file system operations, and network activity. In active environments simultaneously receiving backup and replication traffic, these graphs allow administrators to immediately verify whether the platform is operating according to expected parameters.
The Data Management section groups operational information below the dashboard. The file system view shows capacity, utilization, and compression at the system level, while M trees offer finer analysis. Each backup policy for a connected application creates a unique M tree, logically separating workloads within the system. Per-M-tree views display space utilization, daily write patterns, and pre- and post-compression data over configurable periods. This information is essential for determining whether a workload is compressing properly or consuming capacity faster than expected.
Replication, protocols, hardware, and administration each have dedicated sections, in an interface consistent with previous generations of Data Domain. The Replication section centralizes pair status, synchronization, and job status across connected systems, with scheduled and on-demand replication managed from a single screen. Protocol configuration (DD Boost, CIFS, NFS, and VTL) is grouped in a single section. DD Boost is the primary path for enterprise backup applications and displays active connections, client lists, plugin versions, and authentication settings in a single view.
The interface reflects a platform designed to be used by people who are not exclusively backup specialists. The backup administrator role has shrunk considerably over the past decade, as infrastructure teams have consolidated and generalist profiles have taken on broader responsibilities. DD System Manager has been optimized to remain accessible in this environment, with a logical structure, clear real-time reporting, and a fast process from alert to diagnosis.
Where the Data Domain All-Flash appliance fits in the PowerProtect portfolio
The current Data Domain range includes five hardware models. The Data Domain DD3410 is intended for small and medium-sized businesses, while the Data Domain DD6410 targets mid-sized enterprises. The Data Domain DD9410 and DD9910, meanwhile, meet the needs of larger environments, where capacity and throughput requirements are proportional. The All-Flash appliance and the All-Flash Ready Node enable flash technology to be integrated into the offering at different levels and for different deployment scenarios.
The All-Flash appliance targets large enterprise environments where restore SLAs are demanding and where the operational cost of slow restores or prolonged replication windows is significant. It complements the Data Domain DD9910 (disk-based system) without replacing it. Both systems share the same software stack and integration ecosystem, differing primarily in storage medium and the resulting performance and efficiency. Organizations with large data retention needs and control over capacity costs will likely continue to favor the Data Domain DD9910. Those prioritizing restore speed, vault analysis performance, and energy and space efficiency for high-end solutions may opt directly for the All-Flash appliance.
The All-Flash Ready Node is a separate product category intended for organizations deploying software or hyperconverged environments, in which Data Domain capabilities are delivered through a customer server platform rather than a dedicated appliance. It is not a direct alternative to the DD9910F.
For most large enterprise evaluations, the choice generally comes down to the Data Domain DD9910, equipped with Intel Xeon Scalable processors, and the all-flash system of equivalent capacity. The latter is more expensive to purchase. However, Dell highlights savings in power consumption, cooling, and rack footprint as significant advantages with respect to total cost of ownership over a multi-year deployment.
Conclusion
The Dell PowerProtect Data Domain All-Flash system, equipped with an Intel processor, clearly demonstrates the importance of flash memory in data protection. Backup ingestion is primarily sequential and throughput-dependent; flash memory does not fundamentally change the game there. On the other hand, it is during restoration that flash memory truly comes into its own, and the All-Flash system leverages it precisely at that level: restores up to 4 times faster, replication up to 2 times faster, and CyberSense analyses up to 2.8 times faster than the disk-based DD9910, at equivalent capacity. Each of these gains translates concretely into shorter restore times, reduced digital vault exposure periods, and faster integrity validation before data is returned to production.
Efficiency is the second part of the argument. A 6U all-flash form factor replacing approximately 10U of disk infrastructure, combined with reduced power consumption of up to 80%, changes the game for enterprises managing data protection across their primary, disaster recovery, and cyber vault sites. In the specific case of cyber vault deployments, where power consumption and rack space are billed directly and where isolation requirements make every rack unit crucial, these reductions translate into significant operational savings over multiple years. Data reduction also reinforces these economic benefits, with effective ratios reaching 75:1 and continuing to improve as retention increases.
What makes the All-Flash appliance strong is that it ensures optimal operational continuity. Its architecture, proven over years, defines Data Domain. The DDOS software stack, the DD Boost ecosystem, the security model, and the System Manager interface remain unchanged. For the more than 15,000 organizations already using Data Domain, the All-Flash appliance represents an obvious choice for their critical workloads.
For enterprises looking to integrate flash technology into their data protection strategy, the All-Flash appliance provides a direct answer. Dell did not rethink Data Domain to adapt it to flash technology. The company deployed it where the architecture gets the most out of it, achieved measurable gains in workflows where recovery SLAs are increasingly stringent, and maintained the stability of the operational model.
