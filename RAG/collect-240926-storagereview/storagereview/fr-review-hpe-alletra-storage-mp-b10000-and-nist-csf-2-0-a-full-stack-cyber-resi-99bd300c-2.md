# fr-review-hpe-alletra-storage-mp-b10000-and-nist-csf-2-0-a-full-stack-cyber-resi-99bd300c

HPE has designed a coordinated cyber resilience architecture built around the Alletra Storage MP B10000 storage system. This architecture extends the platform's native security capabilities with an integrated software suite including virtualization with Morpheus and VM Essentials, continuous data protection with Zerto, long-term backup retention with StoreOnce, and observability through integration with a vendor-independent security information and event management (SIEM) system. The overall architecture is designed to be fully compliant with the NIST 2.0 cybersecurity framework, with the B10000 serving as the operational hub for all framework functions. The goal of this design is to answer the two fundamental questions that every security expert asks about any infrastructure: is it still under our control, and is the data it contains still protected?
Enterprise storage is traditionally overlooked in broader security discussions. In most organizations, a chief information security officer (CISO) concerned about a misconfigured router in a branch office or an unpatched laptop on the corporate network pays little attention to data center storage arrays. These are protected by multiple layers of perimeter security, accessible to only a handful of administrators, and largely invisible to the rest of the IT department. On paper, they are one of the most secure assets in the building.
That assumption is no longer valid. Modern ransomware attacks have learned that the storage array is the most valuable target in the data center. Endpoints and servers can be reinstalled. Primary storage contains the data, and an attacker who gains administrative control over it can encrypt the data, delete the snapshots that would enable recovery, and destroy backups in a single coordinated operation. At that point, the organization is no longer experiencing a mere inconvenience; its survival is at stake. The tools available to attackers, including AI-assisted variants that adapt faster than signature-based defenses, are increasingly effective at hitting this target.
The stakes are no longer just operational. In the United States, the European Union, and the United Kingdom, regulators have transformed infrastructure security from a best practice into a legal obligation. Regulatory frameworks such as the European Digital Operational Resilience Act and the NIS2 directive require demonstrable controls for detection, recovery, and incident reporting, with liability extending to the executive level. The organizations subject to these obligations are precisely those that manage enterprise storage at scale: banks and financial services firms, hospitals and healthcare networks, energy and critical infrastructure providers, government agencies, and the cloud service providers that host all of these systems. For them, neglecting infrastructure security represents not only a business risk but also a compliance failure with serious consequences.
Cyber resilience sits at the crossroads between storage and security teams, which report to different managers, measure success differently, and rarely share the same operational language needed for coordination during an incident. Storage administrators master throughput, capacity, and recovery time objectives. Security teams, for their part, understand attack chains, attack vectors, and security posture management. Most organizations only discover this gap after being forced to. The architecture implemented by HPE is designed to bridge it, and the following sections analyze each NIST function to assess its effectiveness, constantly returning to the two essential questions of control and protection.
Key takeaways
- Full-stack resilience: HPE combines the Alletra MP B10000 storage system with VM Essentials, Zerto, StoreOnce, and a vendor-independent SIEM integration, mapping the coordinated stack to every NIST CSF 2.0 function.
- Fast block-level detection: The B10000's built-in ransomware engine, based on entropy and validated against more than 100 strains, detected a simulated encryption attempt in 4 to 5 minutes and automatically captured a forensic snapshot at the moment of detection.
- Immutability that withstands administrator compromise: Virtual Lock snapshots enforced by the array remain read-only for the entire retention period; even a compromised administrator account cannot delete them, and any attempt becomes a logged event visible to the SIEM.
- Tiered recovery: Four tiers (Zerto for the lowest RPO, VME snapshots, Virtual Lock promotion, and StoreOnce Catalyst for long-term retention) were tested in the lab, with clean restorations from Virtual Lock and Catalyst.
- Standards-compliant telemetry: Structured security syslog normalized to the Elastic Common Schema feeds Elastic Security, CrowdStrike Falcon, or any modern SIEM, supporting DORA/NIS2 evidence and automated SOAR playbooks.
Architecture overview
In order to provide a practical demonstration environment rather than a large-scale enterprise deployment, the HPE team set up a compact but functional backup and recovery infrastructure in its Fort Collins lab. At the heart of this environment is a three-node HPE VM Essentials (VME) cluster, running on HPE ProLiant DL325 Gen 11 servers, which provides the compute layer for the demonstration infrastructure. These hosts are interconnected via the local IP network, which also provides connectivity to the NAS layer used within the environment.
HPE Zerto virtual machines run within the VME cluster, configured similarly to how many organizations currently deploy Zerto in a traditional VMware environment. Although the underlying infrastructure uses HPE VME, the operational flow and disaster recovery capabilities remain familiar to administrators accustomed to VMware-based disaster recovery procedures.
A secondary Zerto environment connected to the WAN is available in the Bristol, UK lab. Firewall rules and network paths are configured to support replication from Fort Collins. This topology targets multi-site recovery: continuous replication and disaster recovery orchestration across geographically distinct sites. Thus, protected workloads in Fort Collins can fail over to Bristol in the event of primary site unavailability.
During this engagement, HPE ran Zerto replication locally in Fort Collins due to bandwidth and distance constraints, and because a second parallel VME cluster was not available to test the full inter-site flow. The Bristol infrastructure is documented here as configured and available, rather than as an inter-site failover performed during this session.
Storage connectivity is split between IP and Fibre Channel networks, depending on the workload. The Fibre Channel SAN infrastructure directly connects the VME hosts to the HPE Alletra MP B10000 platform, providing shared access to enterprise storage within the cluster. The B10000 also presents Catalyst via Fibre Channel to the StoreOnce Virtual Storage Appliance (VSA), which ensures application-consistent backups between the array and the backup target. Virtual Lock snapshots play a central role in ransomware resilience and platform immutability; their operation is detailed in the Protection and Recovery sections.
For its backup infrastructure, HPE deployed a StoreOnce Gen5 VSA solution running on a single lab server. The VSA solution offers the same essential StoreOnce capabilities as the dedicated appliance. Deduplication, backup target presentation, replication behavior, and integration with the HPE data protection suite remain fundamentally identical, whether deployed as a VSA or on dedicated hardware. The VSA solution is perfectly suited for labs, branch offices, test environments, and small-scale production use cases, making this demonstration compact and easy to deploy.
