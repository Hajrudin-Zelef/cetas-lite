# fr-review-the-token-efficient-path-for-long-context-inference-kv-cache-offload-t-bd60c176

At peak, offloading achieved a total service throughput up to 2.9x higher than the baseline on the DRAM tier (+188%) and 2.2x higher on the flash tier (+122%). Before the working set saturates VRAM, the performance of the three configurations is very close (within 1%). No memory eviction occurs, so offloading has nothing to recover. The gain only appears when memory pressure causes eviction, and it increases with server load.
The value of a larger storage tier becomes apparent when DRAM is saturated. At that load, the 512 GB RAM cache reaches saturation after approximately 45 minutes of runtime, and must then begin flushing the cache. The returned prefixes are incomplete and reloaded, which is precisely the recomputation the cache was supposed to avoid. The SSD tier, with its multi-terabyte headroom, never reaches this limit. Beyond this threshold, flash memory sustains a throughput of approximately 30,000 tokens per second, compared to approximately 17,000 for DRAM, an advantage of 75% for the SSD once RAM is saturated. In other words, after saturation, the DRAM tier delivered only 42% of its maximum throughput, while the SSD tier delivered 94%. This is the capacity scale on which the entire exercise rests: VRAM runs out first, the 512 GB DRAM tier runs out later, and a storage tier measured in terabytes rather than gigabytes practically never runs out, so it continues to provide context long after the faster tiers had to begin evicting it.
Note: the total throughput shown (several tens of thousands of tokens per second) does not correspond to the speed at which tokens are computed by the GPUs, but rather to the processing throughput of the offload tier. Total throughput counts each prefill token as input and each decode token as output for each request. Thanks to the cache, a successful request reloads the key-value (KV) pair of the prefix from DRAM or flash memory, thus avoiding a new prefill on the GPU.

Regarding output tokens, the three tiers again follow the same trend during cache filling, then diverge at the tipping point. The DRAM tier peaks near 300 tokens per second, an increase of 75% over the baseline, before gradually declining as it begins to flush caches; the flash tier holds around 250 tokens per second until the end of the run, an increase of 46% over the baseline. The performance of the RAM and SSD tiers remains broadly similar (within about 10%) until the DRAM tier saturates; after that, the gap widens.
Time to first token: what the user waits for
Throughput measures total token production. Time to first token measures what a user experiences: the wait between the moment they click "Send" and the moment they receive the first token. We plot it over the course of the run, at its median, as the working set grows and each tier fills.
As long as the caches are active, all three tiers return a first token in about half a second. Then their performance diverges, following the evolution of their throughput. The baseline collapses first: its response time exceeds 10 seconds from the start of the run, while the DRAM tier stays below the 1-second (TTFT) mark much longer, until its 512 GB are saturated around the 45th minute. Its latency then drops sharply when it begins to flush and recompute data. The SSD tier degrades most gradually and shows the shortest response time of the three over the second half of the run.
This reversal perfectly illustrates the tiering debate. DRAM is the fastest as long as the working set fits its category; flash memory becomes the fastest when it no longer does, because it retains contextual information that DRAM has begun to evict.
The problem of returning users
The latencies shown above concern exchanges within a continuous session. The case of a user resuming their session is different: a session is interrupted mid-conversation, remains inactive for about fifteen minutes, then resumes where it left off. We modeled this with a group of users who became inactive after about twenty exchanges and were reactivated 15 minutes later, a period sufficient for a busy server to process other requests through its caches. Eleven of these reactivations occurred within the measurement window of each tier. Since the workload is identical for all tiers, the same eleven sessions were reactivated in each run, allowing a direct comparison of resume times. The graph below shows the median time to first token for each tier during a normal exchange, compared to the resume time. The error bar indicates the longest resume time in the sample.
On average, the figures confirm the predictions of the mechanisms, and the tiers rank in the expected order. DRAM comes first: an inactive session resumes in 0.6 seconds, barely more than the 0.5 seconds of a normal session. The SSD ranks second with 0.8 seconds; we know that an NVMe read is slower than a DRAM read, and this is partly reflected in these results. The VRAM-only baseline is the slowest at 1.4 seconds; lacking data to offload, the prefix of the inactive session was evicted from GPU memory for active traffic, and the first resume must recompute it. At best, the total gap is only about one second.
The best-case scenario is not where the choice is made. The worst of the eleven reload times at each tier is as follows: 0.8 seconds on DRAM, 3.2 seconds on SSD, and 13.9 seconds on the baseline. The tolerance for a fourteen-second wait for a first token, or its impact on the service level objective (SLO), depends on the service. For any interaction, the second option prevails, and only the offload tiers keep this wait within a limit acceptable to the user. This delay also varies with context, because the cost of recomputation is proportional to the user's history: in the deeper contexts of the throughput test, the baseline wait time far exceeds 14 seconds, while DRAM and SSD result in only a limited reload.
Choosing and sizing the KV cache
Both offload tiers far outperform the VRAM-only baseline, and their performance is very close. The gap is small because the throughput gain comes solely from freeing up VRAM, and the same amount is freed whether the evicted cache is stored in DRAM or on SSD. Throughput is independent of the cache's original storage tier. Latency, on the other hand, is sensitive, because a DRAM read is faster than an NVMe read, but both options remain inexpensive compared to fully recomputing the same key-value pair.
The choice between the two solutions rests on a trade-off between service level objective (SLO) and cost, which depends on the operator's optimizations. Keeping the entire offloaded cache in RAM offers the best latency, but represents a significant investment for a relatively small latency gain compared to flash memory. Tiering constitutes a compromise: a smaller RAM tier absorbs latency-critical accesses, while an additional storage tier hosts less frequently accessed caches whose response time is not critical (a few hundred milliseconds).
One can also consider key-value caches in terms of their size. Sizing the storage tier depends on the maximum token throughput the system can support. The storage space required for key-value caches equals the throughput multiplied by the time-to-live (TTL) of the cache entries. Each token produced creates a key-value entry; the calculation therefore boils down to multiplying the token arrival rate by their retention duration. The two default TTL values in production are five minutes and one hour.
