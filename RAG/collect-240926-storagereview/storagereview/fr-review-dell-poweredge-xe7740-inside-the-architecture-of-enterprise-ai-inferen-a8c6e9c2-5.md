# fr-review-dell-poweredge-xe7740-inside-the-architecture-of-enterprise-ai-inferen-a8c6e9c2

Llama 3.1 70B Instruct is the largest dense model in Meta's Llama 3.1 family. With its 70 billion parameters, it offers significantly superior instruction-following and multilingual capabilities compared to the 8B variant. Models of this scale are perfectly suited to more intensive workloads, such as customer support agents, research assistants performing complex tasks, analysis of complex documents, and tasks requiring the maintenance of coherent context during extended interactions.
We tested this model with TP2 and TP4 configurations. The throughput difference between the two is considerable. With 128 concurrent requests, TP4 reaches approximately 3,600 tok/s at equal workload and peaks at nearly 4,600 tok/s in the prefill-intensive scenario. This represents approximately 4.4 and 4.6 times the throughput of TP2, which caps at approximately 816 tok/s and 1,005 tok/s respectively. Even with a decode-focused workload, throughput reaches approximately 1,960 tok/s on TP4, versus 593 tok/s on TP2.
Regarding TTFT (time to first token), TP2 struggles to handle the large prefill workload, reaching an impressive response time of 496 seconds for 128 concurrent requests, making it practically unusable for interactive applications. TP4 reduces this time to approximately 73 seconds. For equal and decode workloads, TP4 maintains TTFT at approximately 10 seconds for 128 requests, while TP2 reaches 50 and 29 seconds respectively. Under low concurrency, TP4 provides first-token responses in approximately 160 ms for the equal workload, versus 486 ms for TP2.
Qwen3 Coder 30B-A3B Instructions
Qwen3 Coder 30B-A3B is one of the most popular coding models for local inference deployments. It uses a Mixture of Experts (MoE) architecture. Unlike dense models, where every parameter participates in each iteration, MoE models route each token through a small subset of specialized expert networks. Qwen3 Coder retains a full 30-billion-parameter model at BF16 precision while activating only 3 billion parameters per generated token. This sparsely activated model offers the quality of a much larger network while requiring only a fraction of the compute power per token, making it extremely efficient on hardware that supports routing overhead. For end users, this model is perfectly suited to everyday coding assistance: standard code generation, function completion, code explanation, writing unit tests, and handling common development tasks that benefit from a specialized model without requiring complex reasoning.
We tested three configurations: TP1 BF16, TP1 FP8, and TP4 BF16. With 128 concurrent requests, TP4 BF16 stands out with approximately 14,300 tok/s in the prefill-intensive scenario, the highest throughput recorded for all models in our test suite. TP1 BF16 follows with approximately 6,900 tok/s in the same scenario, while TP1 FP8 lags with approximately 3,360 tok/s. At equal workload, the gap narrows slightly: TP4 reaches 6,073 tok/s, TP1 BF16 5,718 tok/s, and TP1 FP8 2,101 tok/s. As noted in the FP8 note above, the lower FP8 performance reflects the current state of Intel's vLLM build rather than a hardware bottleneck.
TTFT remains low thanks to the low activation frequency. TP4 BF16 shows a first-token latency of approximately 140 ms with a single user and maintains approximately 2.6 seconds with 128 concurrent requests under the same load conditions. TP1 BF16 is comparable under low concurrency (106 ms) but reaches 3.1 seconds at full load. The prefill-heavy scenario again clearly differentiates the configurations: TP4 reaches approximately 18 seconds with 128 requests, TP1 BF16 reaches 57 seconds, and TP1 FP8 extends to 72 seconds.
Qwen3 235B-A22B Thinking
The largest model in our test suite, Qwen3 235B-A22B Thinking, is a massive MoE reasoning model, with 235 billion total parameters and 22 billion active parameters per token. In addition to its impressive scale, this model incorporates chain-of-thought reasoning capabilities, allowing it to break down complex problems step by step before arriving at a solution, at the cost of a larger number of decoding tokens. It is thus particularly suited to the most demanding tasks: advanced code generation and debugging, mathematical problem solving, multi-step logical reasoning, and complex automated workflows where accuracy takes priority over raw speed. This model requires TP4 precision to operate and we tested it with BF16 and FP8 precisions.
BF16 significantly outperforms FP8 across the board. With 128 concurrent requests, BF16 reaches approximately 2,750 tok/s under heavy prefill workload and 2,500 tok/s under equal workload, while FP8 reaches approximately 1,784 tok/s and 516 tok/s respectively. The FP8 variant also experienced timeouts in the decode-intensive scenario at higher concurrency levels (more than 32 requests).
For TTFT, BF16 shows an initial response time of approximately 340 ms for a single request of identical length and reaches approximately 6.9 seconds with 128 concurrent requests. This behavior is rather responsive for a model of this scale. FP8 is approximately twice as slow, with an initial response time of 615 ms and a maximum of approximately 11.2 seconds at full load. The most demanding scenario is the one requiring heavy prefill: BF16 reaches 168 seconds and FP8 80 seconds with 128 requests.
Who is this for?
Demand for inference is not letting up. Whether deploying models internally to accelerate development, integrating AI into customer-facing products, or setting up automation pipelines running 24/7, compute needs continue to increase. And this demand comes with a well-known bottleneck: supply. Lead times for the most popular accelerators can reach several months, blocking projects that are already funded and staffed.
The XE7740, configured with Intel Gaudi 3, directly addresses this constraint. Gaudi 3 accelerators are available now and Intel provides validated deployment templates enabling teams to go from unboxing to commissioning in a few hours. Dell further simplifies access to the solution through trial programs that integrate XE7740 systems directly into your environment, allowing you to validate their performance with your real workloads, data, and infrastructure before any full deployment. This combination of immediate availability, rapid implementation, and low-risk evaluation makes the Gaudi 3 configuration particularly attractive for organizations that need inference capacity today and cannot afford to wait in allocation queues.
That said, the XE7740 is not a single-accelerator platform. Thanks to Dell's commitment to chip diversity, this same platform is available with a wide choice of options and all the popular accelerators on the market. The optimal choice depends entirely on the workload. Video processing pipelines, for example, are perfectly suited to NVIDIA L4 processors, and the XE7740 can be equipped with 16 L4 GPUs and 8 PCIe Gen5 x16 NIC slots for an uncompromising streaming and transcoding system. Enterprises running mixed AI workloads across their different departments can standardize their configuration on the XE7740 chassis and simply adapt the accelerator configuration for each deployment, simplifying fleet management and adapting compute power to the task.
