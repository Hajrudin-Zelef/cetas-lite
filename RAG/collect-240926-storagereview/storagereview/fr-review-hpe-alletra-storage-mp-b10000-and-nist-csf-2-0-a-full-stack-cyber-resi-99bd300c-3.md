# fr-review-hpe-alletra-storage-mp-b10000-and-nist-csf-2-0-a-full-stack-cyber-resi-99bd300c

The physical StoreOnce appliance has an advantage that goes beyond mere scalability and throughput, and that proves particularly relevant in the ransomware context analyzed in this article. A VSA runs as a virtual machine on a hypervisor. If an attacker compromises the virtualization layer, all workloads running on it, including a VSA backup target, become vulnerable. A physical StoreOnce appliance, by contrast, does not have this dependency. It runs on dedicated hardware, outside the hypervisor, which the attacker would have to traverse, thus ensuring that the backup of last resort relies on an uncompromised infrastructure. For production deployments where StoreOnce constitutes the long-term retention tier in a cyber resilience architecture, the physical appliance represents the most sensible choice, precisely for this reason.
The HPE Alletra MP system and the StoreOnce VSA were both integrated into the HPE Data Services Cloud console, providing centralized, cloud-based management and visibility of the entire environment. Through this console, the infrastructure can be monitored, managed, and integrated into HPE Data Services workflows from a single interface, bringing together storage, backup, and recovery operations.
Govern: Implementing your strategy
HPE does not write your governance plan. It is your organization, your insurer, and your legal department that define security policy. HPE provides the interfaces (APIs, command-line interfaces, Data Services Cloud console, and published security hardening guides) that allow you to implement that policy, attest to its compliance, and conduct audits of it.
This distinction is crucial, because most storage vendors quietly assume the opposite. They offer subjective default configurations presented as best practices and treat configuration drift as a support issue rather than a governance warning signal. The B10000 reverses this approach. Every administrative action is logged. Every security policy-relevant setting is accessible via the API. The security syslog stream is structured to feed the same SIEM where the organization's other governance evidence is already stored. When an auditor asks who changed the password policy on a specific date, the answer is accessible in the same way as for all other managed systems in the infrastructure.
Two governance disciplines deserve particular attention. The first is regulatory compliance. The B10000's audit logging and SIEM integration architecture supports the operational controls required by the European Digital Operational Resilience Act and the NIS2 directive, covering detection, response, and recovery activities, incident reporting workflows, and the visibility expected by management for management control. Centralizing audit and security telemetry in the SIEM makes these obligations concrete. The controls expected by the auditor are not superfluous additions. They are the same controls used daily by the security operations center (SOC) for platform operations.
The second point concerns executive oversight. Regulatory frameworks increasingly require executives to demonstrate consolidated and up-to-date visibility into information and communication technology (ICT) risks. A storage array that hides its audit history behind a vendor-only support portal cannot satisfy this requirement. A storage array that streams audit and security events directly into the customer's SIEM, however, can. The B10000 was designed to meet this second requirement.
The platform offers several concrete features. Documented security hardening guides define initial configuration. Role-based access control and dual authentication mechanisms ensure separation of duties. Multi-factor authentication is built in. DISA STIG (Defense Information Systems Agency Security Technical Implementation Guide) security hardening recommendations are published for environments that require them. The security syslog stream turns every governance-relevant action into a queryable event. The plan is the customer's responsibility. The instrumentation is the platform's responsibility.
Identify: Trust in the platform
Within the NIST CSF 2.0 framework, identification consists of understanding what the organization owns, where it comes from, and what risks it entails. For a storage platform, this translates into supply chain integrity, secure development practices, and verifiable initial configuration.
HPE's hardware and software supply chain is the foundation. Components come from audited circuits, firmware is signed, and platforms are shipped in tamper-evident packaging and seals that the receiving team can verify using shipping manifests. Supply chain assessment is a documented process used by the HPE Cybersecurity Center of Excellence to ensure that a B10000 platform leaving the factory one week has the same provenance as a B10000 platform leaving the factory another week.
Secure development practices extend this security chain to software. The B10000 operating system undergoes structured penetration testing, including internal red team exercises and third-party assessments, the results of which are integrated into the release cycle. Identification is not a one-time operation performed at installation. It is a continuous verification that the trusted platform of day one is still the one present on day 400. The B10000 provides the elements (signed firmware, hash-verified downloads, security hardening guides, secure development documentation, and audit log streams) that enable this verification. Using these elements is, again, solely the decision of the security team.
Protect: Maintaining posture
Protection of the B10000 relies on four mechanisms: immutable Virtual Lock snapshot scheduling, a deliberate separation between array snapshots and hypervisor snapshots, replication to a second site via the array's native Remote Copy and Zerto, and continuous drift detection via the audit log stream.
Immutable Virtual Lock snapshot scheduling
Virtual Lock snapshots are created according to a schedule defined at the array level and enforced directly by the B10000 storage platform. These snapshots remain immutable and read-only for the entire duration of their configured retention period, preventing any modification or deletion until expiration. Administrative actions performed on protected snapshots are recorded in the audit log. These logs can be forwarded to external SIEM platforms, providing visibility into policy violation attempts, including attempts to delete protected recovery points.
B10000 snapshots versus VME snapshots
In this environment, snapshots come from two different sources, and policy development depends on maintaining this distinction.
B10000 scheduled snapshots are immutable and read-only, and their retention is governed by the Virtual Lock mechanism. VME snapshots, created by HPE Morpheus VM Essentials at the hypervisor level, are modifiable and read-write accessible for virtual machine operational workflows.
If an attacker gains access to the hypervisor, VME snapshots become accessible. Virtual Lock snapshots, however, do not. The Virtual Lock schedule defines the minimum retention duration. VME snapshots complement the upper operational layer.
Replication: native at the array level and at the application level
A Virtual Lock snapshot on the primary site does not protect against site-level events. The architecture offers two distinct replication paths to address this. The first is native to the array: B10000 Remote Copy asynchronously replicates volumes to a second B10000 array located at another site. Combined with Virtual Lock snapshot schedules on the target, it creates an immutable and physically separate vault, independent of the hypervisor and any application software. The second, Zerto, operates at the application level with near-synchronous replication and continuous journaling, providing the granular low recovery point objective (RPO) recovery path described in the "Recover" section.
