## Full summary

Published 22 September 2026 by Matthieu Eugène, this BDM investigation (marked "members only" but with visible content) describes how US data-center projects advance under increasing opacity, to the detriment of local officials and residents. It opens in March 2025 in Mason County, north-eastern Kentucky, where Timothy Grosser and his son Andy are offered $10 million — thirty-five times the 1988 price — for their 250-acre farm. One visitor claims to represent a Fortune 100 company interested in industrial development but refuses to name the company or even himself, instead producing a non-disclosure agreement. "We refused to sign," Grosser tells NBC News. "I won't sell my farm at any price." Five months later, local officials announce at a public meeting that the county is being studied for a data center. Of the twenty owners approached, eighteen had already signed a promise of sale.

The same mechanism plays out 1,200 km away in Rapides Parish, Louisiana. In Boyce, a town of about a thousand residents, excavators clear a future data-center campus on 300 acres. Beekeeper Foster Hensel worries about his bills, his hives, and above all the lack of information. Negotiations between Texas developer **Applied Digital** and local officials were covered by NDAs. Former sheriff's deputy Shannon McManus heard rumors in late 2024 but never got answers: "The information should have been available before decisions were made, not after." The project is now named **Delta Forge 1**; the state economic-development agency announced it in May as a **$3.6 billion campus drawing 300 megawatts** in its first phase. The industrial development board that negotiated the deal approved it on 23 April in public session with little discussion.

**Orders of magnitude:** Data centers consumed about **4.4% of US electricity in 2023**, versus under 2% in 2018, per the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report commissioned by the Department of Energy, which projects 6.7% to 12% in 2028. Boyce's 300 MW first phase equals the consumption of about **200,000 US households on a summer day** (EIA data via NPR). Per the Congressional Research Service, a site is "hyperscale" if it hosts at least 5,000 servers, occupies at least 10,000 sq ft, and draws over 100 MW — enough for about 80,000 households.

**The mechanics of opacity:** Documents obtained by Gulf States Newsroom and Type Investigations show at least five state senators, the parish assessor and all development-board members signed NDAs; contracts used code names "Project Lightning" and "Project Pixel" and barred public disclosure. At least **54 such agreements** have been signed by Louisiana officials since early 2024. An NBC News review of 30+ projects across 14 states shows local officials signing NDAs and negotiating with apparent shell companies. Sociologist Eric Bonds counted NDAs in **25 of 31** Virginia projects. In Beaver Dam, Wisconsin, the city development agency signed in December 2023 with an unknown entity, **Balloonist LLC**, not Meta; Meta only confirmed its involvement in December 2025, two years later. In Texas, the same group negotiated tax abatements in Fort Worth and El Paso under **Winner LLC** and **Wurldwide LLC**, both registered in Delaware where owner identities are not public (Texas Tribune). Some agreements extend years beyond the initial proposal and include a FOIA clause requiring the community to restrict disclosure of public documents and to warn the company first.

**Justifications:** Companies cite three: (1) securities law — a listed company like Applied Digital treats a new campus as non-public privileged information; its CEO Wes Cummins tells NPR he'd "love to do without NDAs because it gives people an impression of shady dealings," but the agreement protects the local official from inadvertent insider-trading violations; (2) land prices — Visa's Neila Wilson said at a Bisnow conference that "the second the land seller knows who's moving in, the price rises exponentially"; (3) competition — Dan Diorio of the Data Center Coalition notes rivals knowing your target land can change the price and project. Environmental law professor Tracy Hester nuances this: data centers are no more secretive than other large private industry, echoing the 2010s shale-gas precedent. Legally, in several states NDAs are not enforceable against public-records law; in Pennsylvania, Schuylkill County commissioner Larry Padora was warned signing served no purpose but signed anyway — the only confidential info learned was the end client, Amazon.

**How many will actually be built:** Many projects exist only on paper. Developers file interconnection requests with several grid operators simultaneously for the same project, with no financial commitment, then build only at the best site. Wood Mackenzie estimates only about **28% of the 1,066 GW requested** in the US will materialize. Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro said in August that over a hundred data-center projects were under discussion, with only five holding operating permits. Economist Severin Borenstein (Berkeley) recalls the late-1990s precedent, when internet electricity forecasts vastly exceeded reality (usage multiplied 20× while consumption did not quite double). Residents now organize before permits are even filed. "Either you want your elected officials to court companies and seek development, or you don't," summarized Mason County prosecutor John Estill, an NDA signatory. "And if you do, NDAs are unfortunately part of the landscape."

## Key points

- US data-center projects advance under NDAs, code names and Delaware shell companies, hiding developer identity from officials and residents.
- Kentucky farmer Timothy Grosser refused a $10M offer for his 250-acre farm; 18 of 20 approached owners had already signed.
- Boyce, Louisiana: Applied Digital's "Delta Forge 1" campus — $3.6B, 300 MW first phase.
- Data centers used ~4.4% of US electricity in 2023 (under 2% in 2018), projected 6.7–12% by 2028.
- At least 54 NDA agreements signed by Louisiana officials since early 2024; NDAs in 25 of 31 Virginia projects studied.
- Shell companies (Balloonist LLC, Winner LLC, Wurldwide LLC) registered in Delaware concealed Meta's involvement for two years.
- Three justifications: securities law, land-price inflation, and competition; legal experts note NDAs often don't override public-records law.
- Only ~28% of the 1,066 GW requested in the US is expected to be built (Wood Mackenzie).
