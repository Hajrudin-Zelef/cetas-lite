# 3.5 The Sahel: record violence under the AES (February–September 2026)

### 3.5 The Sahel: record violence under the AES (February–September 2026)

- Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger — the Alliance/Confederation of Sahel States (AES) — faced jihadist violence on course for its highest recorded level in 2026, according to ACLED data reported in the press: 63 jihadist-linked events in 2016, 2,845 in 2025, and 2,145 already logged during the first part of 2026, putting the year on pace to exceed 3,000 events [secondary, ACLED via press] (https://theenglishchronicle.com/News/31954/; http://knowafrika123.blogspot.com/2026/09/sahel-violence-al-qaida-is-record-levels.html).
- The 2026 Global Peace Index was cited as reporting more than 9,000 deaths from security incidents across Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger in 2025, even as terrorism deaths fell globally [secondary] (https://theenglishchronicle.com/News/31954/).
- On 25 April 2026, fighters of the al-Qaeda-linked JNIM together with Tuareg separatists of the Azawad Liberation Front launched a major assault on Gao, Menaka and Kidal in northern Mali — described by one secondary outlet as the largest assault on Mali in nearly 15 years, with Kidal reportedly captured and Mali's Defence Minister Sadio Camara reportedly killed; wire corroboration was not found, so the battle details and the minister's death are [unverified] (http://panxmedia.blogspot.com/2026/05/alliance-of-sahel-states-confirms-joint.html).
- Niger's government confirmed the AES joint force conducted "intense air campaigns" in Malian territory in the hours after the 25 April attacks; the AES force was reported at 5,000 troops, increased to 15,000 in mid-April 2026 [secondary] (http://panxmedia.blogspot.com/2026/05/alliance-of-sahel-states-confirms-joint.html).
- A mid-2026 analytical piece described the Sahel as a single interconnected theater rather than three separate insurgencies: militant groups exploiting weak state control and porous borders, a blockade around Bamako, repeated attacks on Niamey's international airport, violence pushing south toward the Gulf of Guinea, and a Russian security partnership too limited to fill the vacuum left by departing Western forces [secondary analysis] (https://conflictalert.news/the-sahel-in-mid-2026-one-conflict-across-many-fronts/).
- On 30 July 2026, Nigerian President Tinubu said Sahel insecurity was "adding more fuel" to Nigeria's own crisis and noted non-Nigerian fighters in recent attacks and abductions, including the Oriire schoolchildren incident; on 6 August 2026, the AES states summoned Nigeria's diplomats and jointly rejected the remarks as "rapid, very simplistic and surprising" [secondary] (https://zambianobserver.com/burkina-faso-mali-and-niger-reject-tinubus-claim-that-sahel-terrorism-is-fuelling-nigerias-security-crisis/).
- Context: the three AES states completed their withdrawal from ECOWAS in January 2025 and reoriented security partnerships toward Russia, a reorientation whose battlefield results remained contested through 2026 [secondary] (https://conflictalert.news/the-sahel-in-mid-2026-one-conflict-across-many-fronts/).
- The mid-2026 analysis stressed a "Russian security paradox": Moscow's partnership was too limited to fill the vacuum left by departing Western forces, even as the juntas prioritized military sovereignty over Western partnerships [secondary] (https://conflictalert.news/the-sahel-in-mid-2026-one-conflict-across-many-fronts/).
- Pressure on capitals was a 2026 novelty: a blockade around Bamako and repeated attacks on Niamey's international airport showed violence reaching the seats of state authority, not just the rural north [secondary] (https://conflictalert.news/the-sahel-in-mid-2026-one-conflict-across-many-fronts/).
- The southward push toward the Gulf of Guinea states added a coastal-West-Africa dimension, with humanitarian needs deepening as the single-theater conflict expanded [secondary] (https://conflictalert.news/the-sahel-in-mid-2026-one-conflict-across-many-fronts/).
