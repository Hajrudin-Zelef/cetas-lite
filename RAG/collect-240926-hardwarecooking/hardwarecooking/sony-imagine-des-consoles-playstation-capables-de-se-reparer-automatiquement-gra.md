# sony-imagine-des-consoles-playstation-capables-de-se-reparer-automatiquement-gra

<!-- source: https://www.hardwarecooking.fr/sony-imagine-des-consoles-playstation-capables-de-se-reparer-automatiquement-grace-a-lia/ -->

**Sony** is exploring a new use of artificial intelligence that could transform the maintenance of consumer electronic devices. A patent recently filed by the Japanese manufacturer describes a system capable of detecting, diagnosing, and potentially automatically correcting certain software issues affecting its products, including PlayStation consoles.

Spotted by several observers specializing in technology patents, this project is not limited to the PlayStation ecosystem. Sony also plans to apply this technology to other categories of connected devices such as televisions, cameras, and headphones.

## An AI capable of creating its own diagnostic tools

According to the patent documents, the operation would rely on a cloud infrastructure responsible for monitoring connected equipment. When a software problem is detected, an AI model such as an LLM could analyze the situation and then automatically generate a diagnostic program tailored to the observed malfunction.

The goal would be to precisely identify the origin of the failure before proposing a solution. In some cases, the system could even automatically apply a software fix. If the problem proves more complex, the collected information would then be transmitted to an engineer to facilitate their analysis and reduce the time needed to resolve the bug.

Such an approach could allow Sony to limit manual interventions, while reducing the number of error messages and troubleshooting procedures imposed on users.

## PlayStation consoles that could fix themselves without human intervention

Applied to the PlayStation universe, this concept could offer a more seamless experience for players. A console facing a system bug, a compatibility issue, or a software error could potentially identify the cause of the malfunction itself and then download or generate the appropriate fix.

Ideally, the user would not even see the problem appear, as it would be resolved automatically in the background before it actually impacts the machine's operation.

This vision is part of a broader trend observed in the technology industry, where artificial intelligence is gradually being integrated into predictive maintenance, technical support, and software optimization processes.

## An ambitious idea that raises security questions

While the concept seems appealing on paper, it also raises several important questions. The patent mentions the possibility for the AI to generate code intended to fix certain problems, or even to intervene directly on a device's firmware.

However, entrusting an artificial intelligence model with the creation and deployment of software patches on a large scale carries obvious risks. Current generative models remain prone to hallucinations and can produce erroneous or unexpected code. In the context of a game console or a connected device, an error of this type could cause malfunctions far more serious than the initial problem.

Human validation and automated verification mechanisms would therefore likely remain indispensable before any concrete application of such technology.

## A patent far from becoming a commercial product

As is often the case in the technology industry, filing a patent in no way guarantees that a product will reach the market. Large companies register thousands of ideas each year, a large portion of which will never go beyond the conceptual stage.

Sony also has a particularly extensive history of unusual patents. Over the years, the manufacturer has notably imagined systems capable of diffusing odors during video games via a controller, as well as ultrasonic stimulation technologies designed to reproduce taste and olfactory sensations while watching a film.

In this case, the future of the project appears even more uncertain since the patent has reportedly already received a first objection from the examiner at the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). Even if Sony decides to pursue the procedure, there is no indication that this technology will one day see the light of day in a future PlayStation or in another product from the brand.

This initiative nonetheless shows just how far manufacturers are now exploring the potential of artificial intelligence well beyond simple content generation, with the ambition of creating devices capable of monitoring their own operating state and, ultimately, resolving some of their own problems.
