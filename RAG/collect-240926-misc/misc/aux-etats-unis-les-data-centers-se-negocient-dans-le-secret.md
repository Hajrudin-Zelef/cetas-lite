# aux-etats-unis-les-data-centers-se-negocient-dans-le-secret

<!-- source: https://www.blogdumoderateur.com/etats-unis-data-centers-secret/ -->

In March 2025, in Mason County, in northeastern Kentucky, Timothy Grosser and his son Andy received three men around a table. The offer made to them concerned the 250 acres (a little over 100 hectares) of the farm where they have lived and worked for nearly forty years: 10 million dollars, or thirty-five times the price paid in 1988. One of the visitors said he represented a Fortune 100 company interested in industrial development. He refused to specify which one, to name the company, or even to give his own name. Instead, he pulled out a non-disclosure agreement.

*“We refused to sign,”* Timothy Grosser told *NBC News*, which reported the scene. *“I won’t sell my farm at any price.”* Five months later, local officials announced at a public meeting that the county was being studied for the siting of a data center. Of the twenty landowners approached, eighteen had already signed a promise of sale.

## In Boyce, a 300-acre construction site and residents kept in the dark

The same mechanism is playing out 1,200 kilometers away, in Rapides Parish (the equivalent of a county), in Louisiana. In Boyce, a town of about a thousand residents, excavators are clearing the land for a future data center campus on 300 acres. Beekeeper Foster Hensel is worried about his bills and the environment around his hives, and above all about how little information is available. *“I quote a good author, Shakespeare: something is rotten in the state of Denmark,”* he tries to joke to *NPR*.

Mason McCallister, real estate agent, Bells (Texas)

“Everyone stays silent. If it were so good for the town, they would shout it from the rooftops.”


The negotiations between the developer, the Texas company Applied Digital, and local officials were covered by non-disclosure agreements. Shannon McManus, a former sheriff’s deputy, says he heard rumors in late 2024 without ever getting an answer. *“The information should have been available before the decisions were made, not after,”* he explains to the same outlet.

The project now has a name: Delta Forge 1. The state economic development agency announced it in May as a $3.6 billion campus expected to draw 300 megawatts from the power grid in its first phase alone. The industrial development board that negotiated the agreement had approved it on April 23, in a public session, with little discussion.

A few orders of magnitude

- Data centers consumed about 4.4% of U.S. electricity in 2023, compared with less than 2% in 2018, according to the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report commissioned by the Department of Energy. The same document projects a range of 6.7 to 12% in 2028.
- The 300 megawatts of the first phase of the Boyce campus correspond to the consumption of about 200,000 U.S. households on a summer day, according to data from the federal Energy Information Administration cited by *NPR*.
- According to the Congressional Research Service, a site is classified as hyperscale if it houses at least 5,000 servers, occupies at least 10,000 square feet, and draws more than 100 megawatts from the grid, enough to cover the needs of about 80,000 U.S. households.

## Project Lightning, Balloonist LLC: the mechanics of opacity

### Code names…

Documents obtained by the Gulf States Newsroom and Type Investigations through public records requests show how the file circulated. At least five state senators, the parish assessor, and all members of the development board had signed a non-disclosure agreement. These contracts designated the project under two code names, “Project Lightning” and “Project Pixel,” and prohibited the communication of information to the public. The same requests identified at least 54 such agreements signed by Louisiana officials since early 2024, across all projects combined.

A review of more than thirty projects in fourteen states conducted by *NBC News* shows that the practice is no local exception and that, in a majority of cases, local officials signed a non-disclosure agreement and negotiated with what appear to be shell companies. Eric Bonds, a sociologist at the University of Mary Washington, for his part tallied non-disclosure agreements in 25 of the 31 projects studied in Virginia, and estimates that count is lower than reality.

### …and shell companies

The use of shell companies completes the system. In Beaver Dam, Wisconsin, the city’s development agency signed an agreement in December 2023 not with Meta, but with an unknown entity, Balloonist LLC. The document mentioned only a *“project,”* without the words data center or the name of the group, and prohibited revealing even the existence of the discussions, as *Wisconsin Watch* documented. Meta only confirmed its involvement in December 2025, two years later. In Texas, the same group negotiated its tax abatements in Fort Worth and El Paso under the corporate names Winner LLC and Wurldwide LLC, both registered in Delaware, where the identity of the owners is not public, as the *Texas Tribune* established.

Finally, according to documents obtained by *NBC News*, these agreements can sometimes extend for years beyond the date of the initial proposal, and many include a clause requiring the community to restrict, as much as the law allows, the disclosure of administrative documents, as well as to notify the company first so that it has a reasonable opportunity to prevent disclosure. In other words, the private contract organizes the community’s response to requests for access to public documents.

The vocabulary of opacity

- **Non-disclosure agreement (NDA):** a contract prohibiting its signatory from divulging certain information. Signed by an elected official, it concerns a public file.
- **Code name:** a provisional designation for a project in administrative documents, which allows it to move forward without naming the company.
- **Shell company:** an entity created to carry out a real estate operation in place of its true beneficiary. The State of Delaware does not require the publication of owners' identities.
- **FOIA clause:** a stipulation requiring the local authority to limit the disclosure of documents and to alert the company before any disclosure. FOIA stands for the *Freedom of Information Act*, the U.S. law on access to administrative documents.

## "It gives people the impression of shady dealings," admits one CEO

Companies and development agencies offer three distinct justifications for these NDAs, which must be separated because they are not equivalent.

1. The first is securities law. A company like Applied Digital being publicly traded, a new campus project constitutes material nonpublic information. *"I would very much like to be able to do without NDAs, because it gives people the impression of shady dealings,"* its CEO Wes Cummins tells *NPR*. *"I understand that very well."* He explains that the agreement above all protects the local elected official, often unfamiliar with these rules, from an involuntary violation of insider trading regulations.
2. The second relates to land prices, and it is rarely formulated as directly as at an industry conference organized by *Bisnow* in May. *"The second the seller of the land knows who is going to set up there, the price rises exponentially,"* explained Neila Wilson, regional director of engineering and data center operations at Visa, adding that there was, from a commercial standpoint, intentionality there.
3. The third is competition, promoted by the sector's professional organization. *"If your competitors know that you are looking at a particular piece of land, it can change the price of the land, it can change how the project unfolds,"* Dan Diorio, vice president of public policy at the Data Center Coalition, tells *Spotlight PA*. Tracy Hester, a professor of environmental law at the University of Houston, offers a nuance to the *Texas Tribune*: according to him, data centers are no more secretive than any other large private industry, and the shale gas precedent of the 2010s followed the same trajectory before the sector conceded to publishing the chemicals used.

One little-known legal point remains: in several states, these agreements are not enforceable against the right of access to public documents. In Pennsylvania, Larry Padora, chairman of the board of commissioners of Schuylkill County, tells *Spotlight PA* that his legal counsel had warned him: *"Our counsel explained that NDAs are disclosable under the right of access. So having us sign them is pointless."* He signed anyway. The only confidential information learned in the meeting, he says, was the name of the data center's end customer: Amazon.

David Cuillier, director of the Brechner Freedom of Information Project, University of Florida

"Information is power. And these people are powerful, and they want to make a lot of money."


## How many of these projects will actually be built

A significant share of the data center projects identified exists only on paper. Developers file interconnection requests with several grid operators simultaneously, for the same project, without financial commitment, then build only at the best location. The firm Wood Mackenzie estimates that only about 28% of the 1,066 gigawatts requested in the United States will materialize.

At the state level, Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro indicated in August, for example, that more than a hundred data center projects were under discussion in his state, and that only five had the necessary permits to operate. He described the others as speculative, lacking a detailed plan, financing, access to electricity, or an identified customer.

Economist Severin Borenstein, of the University of California, Berkeley, recalls a precedent: in the late 1990s, electricity consumption forecasts linked to the Internet had largely exceeded reality, with usage multiplied by twenty while consumption did not quite double. The subject nevertheless remains difficult to measure precisely, including for the AI models that drive current demand.

Those that do succeed, however, now find themselves facing residents who have been warned, and often organized even before the permits are filed. *"Either you want your elected officials to court companies and seek development, or you don't,"* John Estill, the prosecutor of Mason County and a signatory of a non-disclosure agreement, summarized to *NBC News*. *"And if you do, then NDAs are unfortunately part of the landscape."*
