## The different types of analysts

A data analyst plays an essential role in any organization by collecting, organizing, and analyzing large datasets to inform strategic decisions. The analytics field has become more specialized, however, and depending on the type of needs, you’ll benefit from targeting the right analyst profile.

- **Data analyst**. The data analyst designs reports and conducts analyses to guide strategic decisions. A strong foundation in statistics, SQL, and visualization is essential.
- **Business analyst**. The business analyst relies on data tools to improve internal processes. Proficiency in SQL and project management tools is key.
- **Business intelligence analyst**. The BI analyst also aims to improve company performance and shares many responsibilities with the data analyst. Here too, a good knowledge of SQL, project management, and visualization tools is necessary.
- **Data engineer**. Data engineers build the infrastructure used to collect, store, and transform data so it can be used effectively. Knowledge of orchestration tools like Airflow and strong programming skills are useful.
- **Analytics engineer**. Falling between data engineer and data analyst, the analytics engineer builds data pipelines and conducts analyses to inform decisions. With tools like dbt, data modeling is more accessible than ever. Proficiency with an orchestration tool and excellent knowledge of SQL are fundamental.
- **Data scientist**. The data scientist has responsibilities close to those of the data analyst: producing analyses that move the business forward. A large part of the role, however, relies on predictive analysis and forecasting methods. An in-depth knowledge of inferential statistics, machine learning, and programming is essential.

Beyond the type of analyst, take your business model into account before writing the job description. While many skills and responsibilities are transferable from one role to another, differences exist depending on the industry in which you operate (healthcare, public sector, fintech, etc.).

## Responsibilities of a data analyst

Here are the key roles and responsibilities of a typical data analyst. They will vary depending on your company, your industry, and how your team is organized.

### Core responsibilities

The heart of a data analyst’s work rests on three pillars: automation, reporting, and data governance. Let’s take a closer look at them.

- **Automation**. The analyst should seek to automate repetitive tasks. On an individual level, this frees up time for other projects. At the company level, it reduces waste and opens the door to new revenue streams.
- **Reporting**. This is the core of the role: analysis and visualization. Reporting covers everything from tracking key indicators (KPIs) to visualizing insights and communicating them to stakeholders.
- **Data governance**. As they say, “garbage in, garbage out.” You can’t produce good analyses from bad data: reports degrade and insights become biased, leading to poor decisions. An essential role of the analyst is to identify and correct data quality issues. Other parts of a governance program include improving documentation, tagging and classifying data assets, and good metadata management practices.

### Other responsibilities

Other possible responsibilities include:

- Answering ad hoc questions about data;
- Leading coaching sessions for data users; and
- Reducing technical debt (that is, anything that doesn’t add value and clutters the analytics environment)

### Differences by industry

Beyond core responsibilities, analysts can develop specializations related to their industry. Gaining business context offers many advantages, including reducing ramp-up time and going deeper into key topics specific to the industry. The intrinsic requirements of the data analyst role, however, do not vary drastically from one industry to another.

In certain sectors like finance or healthcare, the analyst will work on more specific data, such as financial entries or patient records. In marketing or e-commerce, they will analyze customer behaviors and trends. In sales, the focus will be more on conversion rates between the stages of the funnel.

### Knowledge and Tools

Getting certified is an excellent first step, but it is not enough. An analyst must demonstrate their ability to assume the responsibilities of the position. How do you apply conditional formatting in a spreadsheet? What is the difference between a LEFT JOIN and a RIGHT JOIN in SQL? How do you determine the regression line in a linear model? These are examples of interview questions that one must know how to answer. Here are the main elements of an analyst's toolkit.

#### Statistics and Probability

A solid foundation in statistics and probability is essential: measures of central tendency and dispersion, linear and non-linear regression, sampling distributions, hypothesis testing, etc.

#### Spreadsheet (Excel/Google Sheets)

Even if ChatGPT might one day revolutionize everything, a large part of the work is still done in spreadsheets. Ubiquitous tools in companies, Excel and Google Sheets remain essential. Discover our Spreadsheet Fundamentals skill track to become a spreadsheet pro.

#### SQL

SQL is the essential tool of analytics. It is the language that allows querying a database to extract and analyze data. Technical interviews often include one or more SQL questions. For an introduction or a refresher, see our Introduction to SQL course.

#### Programming

For more advanced analyses, a scripting language usefully complements the other tools. Python and R are commonly used to perform statistical analyses on a large scale. For Python, explore the Data Analyst with Python career track on DataCamp. For R, see the Data Analyst with R track.

#### Visualization Tools

Analyzing data is important, but communicating insights is essential. An analyst relies on the main tools on the market, such as Microsoft Power BI, Tableau, Looker, etc., with varying levels of proficiency.
