# test-de-l-iphone-18-pro-le-meilleur-iphone-est-aussi-le-plus-prudent-frandroid

After my review of the iPhone 17 Pro, here is, one year later, that of the 18 Pro. And yet, I ended up spending the year with an Air, which I hadn't reviewed at the time. I was too drawn to novelty, but the Air's compromises eventually wore me down. Admittedly, I'm doing 1.7 charges per day right now. Basically, I charge my smartphone almost twice a day, which is no longer sustainable.

Anyway, back to the Pro. And the 18 Pro arrives with a 150-euro price increase, without a standard iPhone 18 alongside it, and with a photo feature that everyone understood was more impressive to announce than to use. That didn't stop us from testing it thoroughly.

A quick note before we begin: we're not here to do Apple bashing, but we're not going to indulge in blind praise with superlatives either. That's never been our style. The iPhone Pro excels in many areas and still stumbles in others, and we're going to explain which ones.

## Design: the same iPhone, better finished, and no heavier

You have to put a 17 Pro next to it to find a difference, and even then. The aluminum unibody frame, the full-width camera plateau, the 8.75 mm thickness, everything is identical down to the tenth. Last year's cases fit this one. So, with a few exceptions, the vast majority of cases are compatible.

What changes is the back: the glass is finally the same color as the aluminum, and the two-tone look of the 17 Pro, which sparked debate, is gone. Black is black. Glacier is a straightforward sky blue, Silver is finally silver. Apple listened to the criticism, and it shows.

Weight is the point where I want to be precise: both smartphones have put on some bulk. The 18 Pro weighs 211 grams, 7 more than the 17 Pro, and I didn't feel it. The 18 Pro Max weighs 249 grams, 18 more than its predecessor, and everyone who has held it this week says the same thing: it's the limit.

I didn't test the Max, but I've had an Air for a year and I know what a few extra grams do to a jacket pocket. At 249, we're entering Duo territory, which weighs 254 and, unlike this one, folds.

The Dynamic Island has shrunk by about 25%, because the Face ID infrared receiver has moved under the screen, into a less dense pixel area to the left of the clock. Face ID doesn't seem to have lost anything. The island now displays three Live Activities in compact format, or two in medium format, which is useful once a week and pleasant the rest of the time. But that's just 1% more screen.

A word on the color, because it's the first thing you see and the last thing reviews talk about. Apple decided that the star of the year would be Bordeaux, and all the communication aligned with it, just like Cosmic Orange last year.

Except that the real success of this generation is the other new addition: Glacier, the blue. A light blue, almost powdery in the shade, that wakes up in the sun without ever turning garish, and that echoes the Sierra Blue of the iPhone 13 Pro that many never got over seeing disappear. If I'm making a big deal of it, it's because I like it a lot.

I find that it doesn't scream "new iPhone" ostentatiously, it just looks pretty. It's my choice, and judging by the feedback since launch, I'm not the only one. Given the place color has taken in Apple's marketing, it's no surprise that the Glacier is harder to find than expected.

Very distinctive colors always have a downside: they date the device as soon as the next generation arrives. That's worth keeping in mind.

Finally, a point that still leaves me just as puzzled: the Camera Control button. Two years after its arrival, I noticed that its capacitive function was now disabled by default. That says a lot about this famous button presented as an innovation. For my part, I still haven't gotten into the habit of using it. It's there, but I forget about it. A sort of appendage I don't quite know what to do with.

## Display: the best of the year, with one small caveat

The iPhone 18 Pro reuses the 17 Pro's screen: 6.3 inches, 2,622 x 1,206 pixels, adaptive 120 Hz, Ceramic Shield 2 with anti-reflective treatment. Apple announces nothing new, and that's precisely why it needed to be measured.

Our X-Rite i1 Display Pro probe and Calman record a peak of 1,023 nits in SDR on a white test pattern, exactly the 1,000 nits promised for everyday use, and 2,740 nits in HDR, where Apple merely claims 1,600. The 3,000-nit figure isn't measurable in the lab, it only triggers in full sunlight with the brightness sensor, but at 2,740 on a test pattern, we're already at the level of the best Android screens, and a Dolby Vision video doesn't demand more.

For accuracy, in SDR, on a ColorChecker test pattern, the average Delta E 2000 comes in at 2.48, below the threshold of 3 at which the eye can distinguish a difference. In HDR, it drops to 1.25, which is excellent, and rare: most smartphone screens fall short in HDR, the iPhone does the opposite.

The only reservation is therefore the white point, measured at 6,710 K, a little above the 6,500 K reference. It's a slightly cool, somewhat bluish white, visible on grays more than on colors, and that True Tone corrects in daily use since we had disabled it for the measurement. Those who retouch photos will enable Reference mode, but chances are you'll never see the difference.

In terms of coverage, the panel covers 106.5% of BT.709, 99.5% of DCI-P3 and 72% of BT.2020. This means the screen is capable of displaying almost everything the widest Ultra HD content contains, which very few panels can do.

A useful clarification for understanding why the iPhone sometimes looks less "punchy" than a Galaxy in Vivid mode: iOS manages colors strictly, sRGB content stays within sRGB, and this gamut headroom is only called upon by content that declares it. The iPhone offers no option to change display modes, unlike Android. In short, Apple has the widest screen on the market and refuses to use it to flatter the eye. It's a choice, and it's certainly the right one.

Finally, we would have liked to see Apple adopt the privacy screen that Samsung has on the Galaxy S26 Ultra and that Xiaomi will introduce on its Xiaomi 18 Pro line. In this case, I think it's really one of the features that would be of great interest in Apple's privacy strategy. We might see it arrive on a future iPhone generation, even though, for now, no leak mentions the subject.

## Performance and heat: Apple has closed the case

The A20 Pro is the first iPhone chip etched in 2 nm, with six CPU cores, a GPU going from six to seven cores, two 16-core Neural Engines, 50% more memory bandwidth and still 12 GB of RAM.

The A20 Pro chip is the most powerful mobile chip we have tested to date.

These are numbers you don't feel when opening Safari, any recent iPhone is instantaneous, but you feel them when exporting a video, gaming for twenty minutes, or running a language model locally, where the gain approaches 60% more tokens per second. The 18 Pro is, to date, the best smartphone for on-device AI, which has a particular flavor when Siri AI isn't available in France.

The real subject is heat, and here Apple has finished the job it started last year. The vapor chamber has tripled in surface area and extends all the way to the camera block, the chip has been moved to the top of the motherboard and bonded to the chamber, the memory, until now stacked on top of it, has been relocated to the side, and a nano-etched copper plate isolates the screen. In extended benchmarking, the 18 Pro heats up as much as a 17 Pro, the heat concentrates under the camera block instead of rising into the screen, and it holds out longer before throttling. In daily use, intensive photography, navigation, video, it heats up less and cools down faster. And above all, it no longer becomes scorching hot while charging, which, as we will see, changes everything. Three years ago, heat was the iPhone's primary flaw. It no longer is one.

## AI: it runs locally

Apple has doubled the Neural Engine of the A20 Pro, going from 16 to 32 cores, and speaks of AI power doubled. This is the kind of promise that can be verified, provided you don't stop at a benchmark score. We therefore tested at three levels: a third-party language model in an app that anyone can install, the same model in Google's engine to compare with Android on equal footing, and the native Apple Intelligence features that people actually use.

Three levels, three different answers. I had the iPhone Air on hand, so it will serve as the reference: its A19 Pro shares the CPU and Neural Engine with the iPhone 17 Pro, so it plays the role of the previous generation.

So, before anyone jumps on me, I know its chassis isn't that of a Pro, without a vapor chamber and with a thickness that forgives nothing when it comes to heat. That's why all the tests in this section are short bursts, launched cold, and the Air was left to rest between each run: what we're measuring here is the chip itself, rather than the smartphone's ability to cool it. Sustained performance is the subject of the previous performance section, where the Air instead plays the role of the worst thermal case, and that's where you need to go to know what the vapor chamber changes.

First level, PocketPal, a llama.cpp app, with Qwen3 4B quantized in Q4_K_M and a prompt of over 2,000 tokens.

The iPhone 18 Pro reads the prompt in 4.4 seconds and generates at 32.4 tokens per second, the Air takes 7.1 seconds to read and generates at 16.6 tokens per second. Reading is 1.6 times faster, writing twice as fast. The ratio is instructive, because it's inverted relative to Apple's messaging: generating a token involves re-reading the model's 2.5 GB of weights from memory, and it's bandwidth that limits it.

The 50% extra bandwidth of the new memory architecture shows up here more than anywhere else. Besides, llama.cpp runs on the Metal GPU and doesn't use the Neural Engine. This test therefore sets aside the 32 cores and measures what a developer who has done nothing special for the iPhone gets, and that's already a lot.

Against Android, the comparison becomes a story of ecosystem before being a story of silicon. In the same PocketPal app, the Xiaomi 17 Ultra and its Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 cap out at 15 tokens per second, the Pixel 11 Pro Fold at 2.5: on Android, the app runs on CPU, and enabling the Xiaomi's Hexagon NPU divides the speed by three for lack of a compatible format. For an honest measurement, we went through Google AI Edge Gallery, Google's app, with Google's model, Gemma 4 E4B, on GPU everywhere: the 18 Pro decodes between 25 and 34 tokens per second depending on the run, the Air and the Xiaomi run at 19, the Pixel at 7.5. The Tensor G6 is excellent with Gemini Nano in Google apps, and average as soon as you leave the Google ecosystem, the Air and the Xiaomi are evenly matched, the 18 Pro is alone out front.

And on iPhone, a third-party app gets hardware acceleration without configuring anything. On Android, the same model can go six times faster or slower depending on the app, the setting (CPU, GPU, etc.) and the smartphone.

Third level, the native features, and there the narrative flips. We took the same photo, taken with the Air, and subjected it to the same edits on both iPhones: erasing a real estate sign on a balcony with Clean Up, removing the drain grate, straightening the perspective with the new Reframe, expanding the frame.

The result is identical down to the pixel, which is logical since it's the same Apple Intelligence model on both sides, and the 18 Pro is a bit faster, on the order of a few seconds on a reframe, nothing you'd notice without a stopwatch.

It's explained quite simply, in fact: Apple calibrates its features for the least powerful iPhone entitled to them. Incidentally, Reframe leaves empty corners, which the smartphone fills in by inventing. In our photo, the building on the left gained a window that doesn't exist. It's well aligned, well lit, and totally fake.

We continued the tests, and we won't deliver all of them here. But we wondered what the intelligence of a 4-billion-parameter model, the kind that fits in a smartphone, looks like? We submitted to Gemma 4 E4B, on all four devices, a small physics problem based on our own charging measurements, six values to calculate (the charging test is further down). The four smartphones gave different answers, with the same model and the same prompt, because GPU arithmetic doesn't accumulate in the same order.

None exceeded 4 out of 6, all succeeded at the yield, three out of four failed a subtraction. And when, by mistake, we left the answer key in the prompt, the Pixel calculated 18.25 W, saw that the expected answer was 18.2, and wrote that one should "stick to this value."

Like all the others, the chip in the 18 Pro is faster, but it isn't smarter. That's even the common thread of this section: the closer you get to the uses Apple intended, the less the chip matters; the further you move away, the more it crushes everything. The 32-core Neural Engine is an investment for tomorrow's applications, and for now, we're still far from exploiting it fully.

In short, with its A20 Pro chip, the iPhone 18 Pro remains the most gifted smartphone for on-device AI, even though Siri AI isn't available in France. That's probably where the shoe pinches.

### What's all this power for, then?

The question needs to be asked frankly, because I'm asking it myself: who, in real life, is going to run a language model in a third-party app on their smartphone? Almost no one.

Our tokens per second are the only way to measure the Neural Engine, but in everyday use, it mostly works elsewhere. What uses it is everything else, and everything else is invisible. Every photo passes through neural networks before even appearing on screen, for exposure fusion, denoising, subject separation, white balance. The keyboard uses it for correction, Photos for search, Voice Memos for transcription, the Camera for recognizing text or an object in the viewfinder, Face ID to unlock your phone, and Siri for pretty much everything it still does here.

These functions run locally, without a network, hundreds of times a day, and it's precisely for them that Apple doubled its Neural Engine. The problem is that none of them can be measured. You don't time the denoising of a photo, you don't compare keyboard correction between two chips, you don't know whether an iPhone 18 Pro finds "the blue house" in Photos faster than an Air because the search is done before you type. The gain probably exists, spread out in milliseconds across a thousand gestures, and it's impossible to demonstrate. That's the limit of this test, and of all tests: we measure power where it's visible, and it mostly serves where it isn't. Apple knows this, and that's probably why its marketing talks about "2x" rather than showing you where.

And for tomorrow, even if it isn't the subject of this test, I'm thinking of an operating system that no longer launches apps but builds them, a local agent that understands the need and constructs the tool, without sending anything to anyone. And there's nothing science-fiction about that, since it's what frontier models already do in the cloud.

What's blocking is fitting them into a pocket, and there, frankly, physics decides, whatever ingenuity you throw at it. A frontier-class model has hundreds of billions of parameters; even quantized, DeepSeek in its 671-billion version requires a Mac Studio with 512 GB of unified memory and more than 800 GB/s of bandwidth to run.

An iPhone 18 Pro has 12 GB and a fraction of that bandwidth, and we've seen what that bandwidth does to tokens per second. Context is the other wall: a coding agent that loads an entire repository far exceeds the memory cache a 16 GB card can hold, so for a smartphone chip, let's not even talk about it. What remains, then, is what we tested, the 4-billion-parameter models, and they're impressive for their size, but their size is the point.

My own experience is more modest and more telling: four high-school-level physics calculations, and Gemma 4 E4B fails two out of six (mental arithmetic is a weakness of all language models, but still…).

The good news is that the frontier is coming down: a 3-to-14-billion model today does what a 70-billion did twelve to eighteen months ago. The dream will therefore come, from below, and the iPhone that will have the memory and bandwidth to welcome it hasn't been released yet.

Let's start with what doesn't make waves, because it's rare: iOS 27 on the iPhone 18 Pro is a finished system. No blocking bug for a week, no stutter in animations, no lazy screen wake, no Wi-Fi dropping out, none of those little miseries that ordinarily accompany a new iPhone until version .1 or .2.

120 Hz is used everywhere, transitions are clean, the Camera app has integrated its Pro controls without weighing down the interface, and the Apple Intelligence features available here—Clean Up, Reframe, Writing Tools, Image Playground—run without a hitch. After two years of redesigns, Apple has delivered an iOS that asks for nothing more than to be used. That deserved to be said before what follows.

Because what follows is absence. These same users will nevertheless have Siri AI on Mac and on Vision Pro, and nothing on the smartphone they just paid 1,479 euros for. And what's missing is not a detail.

The second flaw is more down-to-earth and, in use, even more annoying: voice dictation has become a horror. ## Photo: the diaphragm is almost useless, the aperture is everything

First, what doesn't change: the three 48-megapixel sensors, main, ultra-wide and 4x telephoto, are those of the 17 Pro, and the 18-megapixel front camera too.

This table, above, mainly shows that Apple only evolves the hardware part of these cameras in small touches. Apple doesn't take risks, and that can be detrimental to it in certain aspects.

### Variable aperture, all that for this

The novelty is in the main lens: the mechanical six-blade diaphragm cut with a laser, driven by a motor, which offers four positions: Æ/1.48, Æ/1.8, Æ/2.8 and Æ/4.

We had explained the principle, and we asked ourselves a question: why did Samsung, Sony and Xiaomi abandon the idea? A week later, I have the answer. Because on a sensor of this size, closing the diaphragm is almost useless.

To realize this, one need only look at what auto mode gives us. Out of more than two hundred photos with the main sensor, the smartphone chose Æ/1.48 76% of the time, Æ/1.8 16%, Æ/4 8% of the time, and Æ/2.8 never. Moreover, it only stops down in very bright scenes or when it detects a group at several distances. And my photos were taken on sunny late-summer days.

Like any auto mode, it prefers to open as wide as possible. An amusing detail noted by our colleagues at *PetaPixel*: in auto, the diaphragm is not limited to the four stops, it adjusts continuously between Æ/1.48 and Æ/4. The four positions simplify the interface without marking the limit of the mechanism, and third-party applications could one day have access to it.

That's logical, but forcing Æ/4 in low light is a bad idea, the smartphone compensates by raising ISO on the same scene, and the photo becomes gray and grainy. In daylight, on a landscape, the sharpness difference between Æ/1.48 and Æ/4 exists when zooming in at 100%, and diffraction on such a small sensor erases half of it.

Yet sharpness is not the same depending on aperture, and the order is telling: from best to worst, Æ/2.8, Æ/1.8, Æ/4, then Æ/1.48. Wide open, the center holds but the edges soften, as on any lens pushed to its maximum aperture; stopped all the way down, diffraction nibbles away. The sharpest aperture is therefore Æ/2.8, the one auto mode never chose in our two hundred photos, and the least sharp is Æ/1.48, the one it chose three times out of four.

The ideal point is in the middle, where the 17 Pro already was, and the iPhone knows it: it prefers light to sharpness, and it's right, because no one looks at their photos zooming in at 100%.

The only real usefulness is group photos where you want the back row sharp, and Æ/1.8 already achieved that many times. The diaphragm is a gadget. A beautiful gadget, which makes six-pointed stars on streetlamps and allows one-second exposures with real light trails, but it remains a gadget.

The Æ/1.48 itself, on the other hand, has a very concrete effect. The 17 Pro was stuck at Æ/1.78, and moving to Æ/1.48 lets in about 45% more light. At night, you can see it in every photo: at equal shutter speed, the 18 Pro drops from 12,500 to 10,000 ISO, Night mode triggers less often and lasts less time when it does trigger, moving subjects are less blurry, and images have more detail, less noise, richer colors. It's the biggest progress in night photography on iPhone since Night mode itself, and it has nothing to do with the aperture variability. There's an irony in that: without the mechanism that allows it to close down, Apple probably would never have dared such a wide aperture. The gadget served as an alibi for the real innovation.

It must be said that what we were really expecting this year was something other than the variable aperture. For four generations, the rumor announced the move to a 1-inch sensor on the iPhone Pro, and for four generations, Apple has stuck with its 1/1.28 inch.

A 1-inch sensor is 116 mmÂ² of sensitive surface versus 72 on the iPhone, or 60% more light with every shutter press, without asking anything of the software. This translates into less noise at night, a wider dynamic range in daylight, and above all a natural depth of field that allows optical background blur, without Portrait mode, without approximate hair cutouts.

That's why Xiaomi, Vivo, Oppo and Huawei made the switch as early as 2022 with Sony's IMX989 then LYT-900: when you're chasing Apple in image processing, the most direct way to gain ground is to change the rules and bring in more photons.

And it's physics. A sensor of that size requires a longer lens, therefore a thicker module, a camera bump that protrudes, a weight that climbs, and a depth of field sometimes too shallow for a group photo.

Apple, which sells smartphones that fit in a pocket and photos that are sharp on the first try, has always refused that trade-off.

The variable aperture is its answer: rather than enlarging the sensor, allowing the same sensor to choose between more light and more sharpness. It's interesting, it's consistent with the house style, and it's exactly what the duel with the Xiaomi 17 Ultra, 1-inch sensor but fixed aperture, settled. The diaphragm doesn't compensate at all for the surface area, Apple has once again pushed back by a year what would really be of interest for this main camera.

### The new image processing: the real innovation

The main sensor hasn't changed, but what Apple does with it has. The 18 Pro inaugurates an entirely revamped computational processing chain for the 48-megapixel camera, which promises sharper and more detailed images. I observed it, and not just by zooming to 100%. Foliage, bricks, hair, everything that turned into fine mush on a 17 Pro keeps its structure here. The over-sharpening halos around contours, the iPhone signature for ten years, have almost disappeared, and the grain in low light is finer, more regular, closer to film grain than digital noise. The progress is a small step rather than a leap, but it's the step that was missing.

This new processing has a direct consequence on skin tones. Skin tones are more natural, especially in low light, where the iPhone tended to lean yellow or gray. Be careful not to mix up two things: this improvement comes from the processing chain and the extra light captured at Æ/1.48, it applies to all photos. What follows, the textures, is an additional aesthetic choice.

### Photographic Styles 3: a rendering system, still without a signature

First, we need to recall what Photographic Styles are, because many confuse them with filters. A filter is applied to a finished image and tints everything uniformly. A Style intervenes during processing, evaluates each photo, adjusts colors and warmth zone by zone, and leaves skin tones alone. It's Apple's answer to Fujifilm's film simulations, and since the 16 Pro, you can change Style after the fact in Photos, provided you shot in HEIF. Neither JPEG nor RAW are entitled to it, because the Style needs the HEIF reference HDR data to be recalculated.

The third generation, reserved for the 18 Pro, adds a second axis. Until now you chose a color, Standard, Amber, Gold, Rose Gold, Bright, Neutral, Cool Rose, plus the blacks and whites. Now you also choose a texture: Standard, Soft Skin, Glow or Film, each with an intensity slider, and a Grain toggle that can be added to any of them. Apple offers ready-made combinations, "Rose Gold + Glow," "Gold + Soft Skin," and you can compose your own. The texture is applied after capture, therefore invisible in the viewfinder, which is disorienting at first, and it can be modified or removed afterward in Photos, without loss.

Soft Skin is the most anticipated and the riskiest. It's nothing like a beauty mode: features remain intact, only the sharpness of micro-details of the skin decreases (pores, fine lines, small imperfections), without the uniform blur of social media filters. Even at maximum, the result remains successful.

Glow diffuses highlights, a bit like a *black mist* filter screwed onto a cinema lens, and saves cheeks blown out by head-on sun.

Film is the most subtle: it seeks out areas of high contrast, adds a Cinestill-style halation, and slightly reduces highlight dynamic range to mimic the limits of film stock. With Grain enabled, a street photo on the 18 Pro looks deceptively like a scanned negative. Except Grain has no slider. Apple doses the amount photo by photo, and that's it. Which is a shame because the other three textures have an adjustment, this one doesn't. That may change next year.

It's different from the styles you find on Instagram. A filter degrades a finished image, a Style drives processing at the source, from raw data, with a per-photo decision. Grain is calculated from real noise, instead of being overlaid. Halation appears where light would genuinely bleed on film. And all of this, you set it once, forget it, and every photo comes out with the same character, without retouching. This is what Android smartphones have been doing for a long time, with one big difference in my opinion.

That difference is the signature. When Xiaomi puts Leica in its smartphones, it goes far beyond a logo on the back: there's "Leica Authentic," with its deep shadows, its vignetting, its desaturated tones that you recognize from one photo to the next without reading the EXIF, and "Leica Vibrant" for those who want the opposite.

Vivo has Zeiss, Oppo has Hasselblad, Honor has Harcourt. A name, a history, a look that you choose the way you choose a film stock. Apple, on the other hand, gives you a toolbox. Seven colors, four textures, a grain, to combine. It's more flexible, it's more honest, and it's perfectly anonymous. No one will say "I shot in Amber + Glow" the way they say "I shot in Classic Chrome." The default rendering remains what it has always been, Apple's neutral and slightly clinical eye, the one that produces accurate photos and rarely photos you'd want to frame. A rendering system, however good it may be, is not an eye. After five years of Styles, the iPhone still doesn't have a look, and I think that's intentional.

A small aside: Apple's page indicated that texture and grain settings would arrive with iOS 27 on all iPhones compatible with the latest generation of Styles, meaning the 16, 17 series and the iPhone Air. The page has been modified, the feature is now reserved for the 18 Pro, 18 Pro Max, and the iPhone Duo. Apple ties textures to its new processing pipeline, which is defensible, but it's now an exclusive of the new generation.

### Low light: the aperture does the work the sensor doesn't

This is the situation where the 18 Pro justifies its variable aperture. At f/1.48 versus f/1.78 on the 17 Pro, the lens lets in 45% more light, and Apple announces low-light performance roughly 50% better, which is the same thing said differently.

And everything happens in the optics, even before software processing: at the same scene, the sensor receives more photons, so the iPhone can lower the ISO or shorten the exposure, and it's the second choice that matters. Dark scenes that required a long exposure and a motionless subject now work with a shorter shutter speed, meaning less motion blur on a child, a dog, a passerby.

And the results are good in dark scenes, reducing the reliance on long exposures in Night mode. DxOMark ranks the 18 Pro's low-light performance among the best, in both photo and video, with accurate exposure, wide dynamic range, and noise management that preserves detail rather than smoothing it away.

That's what we saw too: less Night mode, illuminated streets captured handheld, a rendering that stays a bit grainy in textures because Apple prefers grain to mush. There's one reservation, also physical: at f/1.48, sharpness is lower than at f/1.8 or f/2.8, the wide-open lens being softer at the edges.

### Ultra wide-angle: nothing new, nothing to complain about

The 48-megapixel module is the same one from the 17 Pro, identical, and that's just fine. It serves two purposes, landscape and macro, and it does both without unpleasant surprises: colors are aligned with the main sensor, dynamic range holds up, the edges don't collapse, macro locks in at a few centimeters.

It's not the best ultra wide-angle on the market in low light, where the smaller sensor and fixed aperture make themselves felt, but Apple stopped selling it as anything other than a complement a long time ago. It's also the module that professional photographers find the most neglected on the phone, and they're not wrong: it's the only one of the three that benefited from neither new optics nor the new processing.

### Zoom: the module Apple left behind

This is where the 18 Pro loses ground. A lot of ground.

DxOMark places the iPhone 18 Pro in second place worldwide with 172 points, just three points behind the Huawei Pura 80 Ultra. A ranking it owes to its record scores in video and low light, but not to its zoom, which remains its weak link.

The telephoto is the same 48-megapixel 4x, 100mm equivalent, inherited from the 17 Pro, with its promise of "8x optical quality" that is in reality just a crop from the sensor.

On the other side, Chinese smartphones have been fielding 200-megapixel periscopes for two generations, focal lengths from 75 to 120mm with true continuous zoom, and a second long lens on the Ultra models. The Xiaomi 17 Ultra, which we used for comparison, offers a 75-100mm 200-megapixel telephoto, the Galaxy S26 Ultra pushes even further.

As expected, details at long-range telephoto are behind compared to competing smartphones.

At 4x, the iPhone remains very good. Beyond that, it relies on processing and it shows. The 8x "optical quality" is a crop from the center of the telephoto's 48-megapixel sensor, so a 12-megapixel image that processing is asked to fill in: details become flat areas, textures are invented, edges are jagged, foliage turns into mush. On the same scenes, the Xiaomi 17 Ultra at 8x crops into a 200-megapixel sensor and retains substance, legible letters on a sign, relief on a facade, where the iPhone delivers a painting. You don't see it on the phone screen, you see it as soon as you open the photo on a computer screen. If you like photographing concerts, matches, animals, or your children at the back of a gymnasium, this is the wrong smartphone to buy, and it's nothing new: this is the third year that Apple has left this module in this state while the competition makes it their number one selling point.

### Video: the only category where the iPhone has no rival

In video, the 18 Pro does what iPhones have been doing for ten years, better, and the aperture finds its true purpose here.

The rule of cinema calls for a 1/48 s shutter at 24 frames per second, which, in full sunlight and at fixed aperture, forces you to raise the shutter speed and chop up motion, or to screw on an ND filter.

The 18 Pro closes down to ƒ/4: three stops less, without an accessory, with natural motion blur. To understand why this matters, we need to go back to what a camera does and a smartphone cannot.

An exposure is set with three parameters: sensitivity, shutter speed, and aperture. A smartphone with a fixed aperture has only two. In full daylight, its sensitivity is already at minimum, so it only has shutter speed left to avoid blowing out the image, and it raises it to 1/1000 s or 1/2000 s.

The result is that choppy, stroboscopic look of smartphone videos in full sunlight, where each frame is too sharp for the eye to connect the motion. A videographer wants the opposite: a 1/48 s shutter at 24 frames per second, the 180-degree rule, the one that gives cinema its motion blur. On a camera, you stop down the aperture and the matter is settled. On a smartphone, you screw an ND filter onto the lens, an accessory that costs a lot and often falls off.

The 18 Pro is the first iPhone, and the only smartphone sold here with a true third parameter: Samsung had tried it on the Galaxy S9 and S10 before giving up, Xiaomi on its 13 and 14 Ultra before abandoning it, and only Huawei maintains it on its Pura Ultra, which aren't sold here.

In full midday sun, ƒ/4 isn't always enough to hold 1/48 s and an ND remains useful, but in everything else, in shade, at the end of the day, in bright interiors, the aperture does the job alone. And in 4K, the loss of sharpness at ƒ/4 that we measured in photo is no longer visible, which makes the entire range usable. It's in video that the variable aperture justifies its motor.

Around it, everything is in place: Dolby Vision up to 4K 120 frames per second on the main sensor, sensor-shift stabilization, ProRes, Log, and above all a consistency between the three modules that no one matches, same exposure, same color science, same rendering when switching from one focal length to another mid-shot.

And this applies as much to the main sensor as to the ultra-wide, with stabilization that stays smooth even while running and skin tone rendering that I find improved in low light.

So, yes, an Android smartphone sometimes films just as well on a single shot. But I haven't found one that films as well on ten shots in a row. Which explains why the iPhone is the overwhelming favorite for video.

### Manual mode, the surprise

The other new feature, more discreet and more useful, is the Pro controls in the native app, after nineteen years of refusal. Incidentally, it's a blow to many third-party apps that built their business on it.

Aperture, shutter speed from 1/6000 to one second, white balance, manual focus, histogram, in a customizable drawer that you have to enable yourself, otherwise you never see it; Apple clearly wanted the general public not to stumble upon it by accident. On the other hand, no manual ISO, which is strange, and a Camera Control button that controls neither aperture nor shutter speed, which is absurd. Asked about ISO, Apple responds that every photo is a fusion of many exposures and that an ISO setting wouldn't make sense in that context. That's understandable for photo. In video, where there is no fusion and where you want to be at base ISO to get maximum dynamic range in Log, the excuse doesn't hold up.

But manual white balance and focus with a dial, behind a window or in the dark, are two features I'd been waiting years for and that will put half of third-party photo apps out of business. Focus tracking from a double tap, which holds a subject even if it leaves and returns to the frame, works remarkably well, including on a cat. It's at the level of a recent mirrorless camera, and on one point it does even better: when the subject leaves the frame, the iPhone doesn't refocus on what remains, it waits. Two caveats: tracking resets with each video recording stop, you have to re-tap the subject for each shot, and it's incompatible with burst mode. Cinematic mode is applied after the fact to a normal video, with mixed results.

And Apple Reference Image, the signature of photos straight from the sensor, is the good idea of the year that we don't have in Europe at launch.

## Battery life: a Max battery in a Pro

The 18 Pro's battery is 4,056 mAh, according to the European energy label, versus 3,988 on the 17 Pro. Sixty-eight milliamp-hours, which is to say nothing. And yet Apple claims that the 18 Pro lasts as long as a 17 Pro Max, and the European label, measured according to an identical protocol from one year to the next, gives 52 hours of endurance versus 47 for the 17 Pro, five more with the same battery. The entire gain comes from the 2 nm chip, the display and the modem.

In use, it's a day-and-a-half smartphone, two days if the day is quiet, with high brightness, CarPlay, Bluetooth accessories and lots of photos. The 17 Pro finished its busy days below 20%, the 18 Pro finishes them around 35. The progress comes from efficiency rather than a chemistry revolution, and it shows. This is, moreover, the whole strength of the iPhone: it's efficiency, and it makes little sense to compare battery capacity with that of a Chinese smartphone.

And there are two important French specifics. Our models keep a nano-SIM tray, and therefore a slightly smaller battery than the eSIM-only versions sold in the United States or Japan, two hours less video on the spec sheet.

In return, Apple's C2 modem equips both sizes here, while the American Pro Max keeps a Qualcomm. I measured no difference in throughput between the C2 and the 17 Pro's modem, but a network reconnection that seems faster when coming out of airplane mode, a gain I couldn't quantify over a week of testing. If you were hesitating to buy your Pro Max in New York, that's an argument for staying here.

## Network and communication: hello C2

The iPhone 18 Pro carries over Apple's in-house wireless chip N1 introduced last year: Wi-Fi 7 (802.11be) on 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz in 2×2 MIMO, Bluetooth 6 and Thread, the smart home protocol that lets the phone talk directly to Matter accessories without going through a hub. Nothing new here, but nothing to envy in the best of Android either.

The real novelty is cellular, and it's historic for a Pro: the modem is now an Apple C2, no longer a Qualcomm. After the C1 in the iPhone 16e and the C1X in the iPhone Air, this is the first time Apple has entrusted its high-end models to its own chip, everywhere in the world with one exception, the 18 Pro Max sold in the United States, which keeps a Qualcomm, no doubt to minimally honor the contract binding the two companies until March 2027.

Apple promises significantly faster uploads than on the C1X and 15% less energy, and finally supports 5G mmWave, which only concerns Americans. Here, we get 5G sub-6 GHz in 4×4 MIMO and Gigabit LTE, which is exactly the 17 Pro's spec sheet on paper.

During my test on the Orange network in Nantes, I noticed no difference in signal acquisition or throughput compared with the iPhone 16 Pro and its Qualcomm, including on a train, and hotspot sharing lasted a workday without interruption. The gain, if it exists, is hidden in battery life rather than in throughput, which is consistent with what Apple is highlighting.

The iPhone 18 Pro accepts a nano-SIM and an eSIM in France, or two eSIMs, where American models abandoned the tray two years ago. In China, the iPhone 18 Pro even has two physical SIMs.

The rest is in place: second-generation Ultra Wideband chip for precise location of AirTags and your loved ones in Find My, NFC with reader mode and Express Cards, dual-frequency GPS, and satellite connection for emergency SOS and Find My, included for two years.

The USB-C port remains USB 3 at 10 Gbit/s with DisplayPort 4K HDR output, essential for offloading ProRes or recording in ProRes RAW to an external SSD.

## Charging: a pleasant surprise

Apple raised its promise by a notch this year: the iPhone 18 Pro and 18 Pro Max recover 50% battery in about 15 minutes, versus around twenty minutes on the previous generation.

Here, with a 120 W charger and a wattmeter, the iPhone 18 Pro went from 1% to 50% in 19 minutes, 80% in 35 minutes, 98% in 63 minutes. The peak capped at 39 W, held for six minutes, before dropping in stages: 30 to 33 W until 17 minutes, 22 to 25 W until the half-hour mark, 18 to 20 W until 36 minutes, then under 10 W for the end. Over the whole session, 19.3 Wh came out of the outlet, an average of 18 W actual.

To reach maximum speed, you need a charger compatible with USB Power Delivery 3.1 and Adjustable Voltage Supply (AVS), and the power displayed on the adapter isn't enough, to the point that some much more powerful MacBook chargers can't manage it. To date, the only charger on the market that ticks these boxes is Apple's 40 W dynamic adapter, capable of going up to 60 W.

Our wattmeter confirms it unambiguously: the USB voltage stayed at 14.5 V from start to finish, the classic 15 V PD profile, whereas the

fast phase with the official charger runs around 18 V. The iPhone 18 Pro never negotiated AVS with our 120 W brick, it settled for the previous 40 W.

In other words, if you plug the 18 Pro back into your 17 Pro's charger, or into almost any GaN charger bought in the last three years, you keep last year's charging. Apple's 15 minutes exist, but they're sold separately for 45 euros.

## Price: 150 euros more

1,479 euros for 256 GB, 1,729 for 512, 2,229 for 1 TB, 2,979 for 2 TB. That's 150 euros more than last year at every tier, an increase that the price of memory partly explains, and which is concentrated on the large capacities, the jump from 512 GB to 1 TB now costs 500 euros.

Apple lifted the price of all its older iPhones the same evening, so the gap between an 18 Pro and a 17 remains the same as last year. It's the 2026 market, and Samsung does the same.
