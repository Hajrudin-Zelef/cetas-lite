# test-de-l-iphone-18-pro-le-meilleur-iphone-est-aussi-le-plus-prudent-frandroid

Glow diffuses highlights, a bit like a *black mist* filter screwed onto a cinema lens, and saves cheeks blown out by head-on sun.

Film is the most subtle: it seeks out areas of high contrast, adds a Cinestill-style halation, and slightly reduces highlight dynamic range to mimic the limits of film stock. With Grain enabled, a street photo on the 18 Pro looks deceptively like a scanned negative. Except Grain has no slider. Apple doses the amount photo by photo, and that's it. Which is a shame because the other three textures have an adjustment, this one doesn't. That may change next year.

It's different from the styles you find on Instagram. A filter degrades a finished image, a Style drives processing at the source, from raw data, with a per-photo decision. Grain is calculated from real noise, instead of being overlaid. Halation appears where light would genuinely bleed on film. And all of this, you set it once, forget it, and every photo comes out with the same character, without retouching. This is what Android smartphones have been doing for a long time, with one big difference in my opinion.

That difference is the signature. When Xiaomi puts Leica in its smartphones, it goes far beyond a logo on the back: there's "Leica Authentic," with its deep shadows, its vignetting, its desaturated tones that you recognize from one photo to the next without reading the EXIF, and "Leica Vibrant" for those who want the opposite.

Vivo has Zeiss, Oppo has Hasselblad, Honor has Harcourt. A name, a history, a look that you choose the way you choose a film stock. Apple, on the other hand, gives you a toolbox. Seven colors, four textures, a grain, to combine. It's more flexible, it's more honest, and it's perfectly anonymous. No one will say "I shot in Amber + Glow" the way they say "I shot in Classic Chrome." The default rendering remains what it has always been, Apple's neutral and slightly clinical eye, the one that produces accurate photos and rarely photos you'd want to frame. A rendering system, however good it may be, is not an eye. After five years of Styles, the iPhone still doesn't have a look, and I think that's intentional.

A small aside: Apple's page indicated that texture and grain settings would arrive with iOS 27 on all iPhones compatible with the latest generation of Styles, meaning the 16, 17 series and the iPhone Air. The page has been modified, the feature is now reserved for the 18 Pro, 18 Pro Max, and the iPhone Duo. Apple ties textures to its new processing pipeline, which is defensible, but it's now an exclusive of the new generation.

### Low light: the aperture does the work the sensor doesn't

This is the situation where the 18 Pro justifies its variable aperture. At f/1.48 versus f/1.78 on the 17 Pro, the lens lets in 45% more light, and Apple announces low-light performance roughly 50% better, which is the same thing said differently.

And everything happens in the optics, even before software processing: at the same scene, the sensor receives more photons, so the iPhone can lower the ISO or shorten the exposure, and it's the second choice that matters. Dark scenes that required a long exposure and a motionless subject now work with a shorter shutter speed, meaning less motion blur on a child, a dog, a passerby.

And the results are good in dark scenes, reducing the reliance on long exposures in Night mode. DxOMark ranks the 18 Pro's low-light performance among the best, in both photo and video, with accurate exposure, wide dynamic range, and noise management that preserves detail rather than smoothing it away.

That's what we saw too: less Night mode, illuminated streets captured handheld, a rendering that stays a bit grainy in textures because Apple prefers grain to mush. There's one reservation, also physical: at f/1.48, sharpness is lower than at f/1.8 or f/2.8, the wide-open lens being softer at the edges.

### Ultra wide-angle: nothing new, nothing to complain about

The 48-megapixel module is the same one from the 17 Pro, identical, and that's just fine. It serves two purposes, landscape and macro, and it does both without unpleasant surprises: colors are aligned with the main sensor, dynamic range holds up, the edges don't collapse, macro locks in at a few centimeters.

It's not the best ultra wide-angle on the market in low light, where the smaller sensor and fixed aperture make themselves felt, but Apple stopped selling it as anything other than a complement a long time ago. It's also the module that professional photographers find the most neglected on the phone, and they're not wrong: it's the only one of the three that benefited from neither new optics nor the new processing.

### Zoom: the module Apple left behind

This is where the 18 Pro loses ground. A lot of ground.

DxOMark places the iPhone 18 Pro in second place worldwide with 172 points, just three points behind the Huawei Pura 80 Ultra. A ranking it owes to its record scores in video and low light, but not to its zoom, which remains its weak link.

The telephoto is the same 48-megapixel 4x, 100mm equivalent, inherited from the 17 Pro, with its promise of "8x optical quality" that is in reality just a crop from the sensor.

On the other side, Chinese smartphones have been fielding 200-megapixel periscopes for two generations, focal lengths from 75 to 120mm with true continuous zoom, and a second long lens on the Ultra models. The Xiaomi 17 Ultra, which we used for comparison, offers a 75-100mm 200-megapixel telephoto, the Galaxy S26 Ultra pushes even further.

As expected, details at long-range telephoto are behind compared to competing smartphones.

At 4x, the iPhone remains very good. Beyond that, it relies on processing and it shows. The 8x "optical quality" is a crop from the center of the telephoto's 48-megapixel sensor, so a 12-megapixel image that processing is asked to fill in: details become flat areas, textures are invented, edges are jagged, foliage turns into mush. On the same scenes, the Xiaomi 17 Ultra at 8x crops into a 200-megapixel sensor and retains substance, legible letters on a sign, relief on a facade, where the iPhone delivers a painting. You don't see it on the phone screen, you see it as soon as you open the photo on a computer screen. If you like photographing concerts, matches, animals, or your children at the back of a gymnasium, this is the wrong smartphone to buy, and it's nothing new: this is the third year that Apple has left this module in this state while the competition makes it their number one selling point.

### Video: the only category where the iPhone has no rival

In video, the 18 Pro does what iPhones have been doing for ten years, better, and the aperture finds its true purpose here.

The rule of cinema calls for a 1/48 s shutter at 24 frames per second, which, in full sunlight and at fixed aperture, forces you to raise the shutter speed and chop up motion, or to screw on an ND filter.

The 18 Pro closes down to ƒ/4: three stops less, without an accessory, with natural motion blur. To understand why this matters, we need to go back to what a camera does and a smartphone cannot.

An exposure is set with three parameters: sensitivity, shutter speed, and aperture. A smartphone with a fixed aperture has only two. In full daylight, its sensitivity is already at minimum, so it only has shutter speed left to avoid blowing out the image, and it raises it to 1/1000 s or 1/2000 s.

The result is that choppy, stroboscopic look of smartphone videos in full sunlight, where each frame is too sharp for the eye to connect the motion. A videographer wants the opposite: a 1/48 s shutter at 24 frames per second, the 180-degree rule, the one that gives cinema its motion blur. On a camera, you stop down the aperture and the matter is settled. On a smartphone, you screw an ND filter onto the lens, an accessory that costs a lot and often falls off.
