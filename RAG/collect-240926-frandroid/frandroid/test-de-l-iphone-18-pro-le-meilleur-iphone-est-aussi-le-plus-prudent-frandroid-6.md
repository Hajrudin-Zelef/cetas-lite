# test-de-l-iphone-18-pro-le-meilleur-iphone-est-aussi-le-plus-prudent-frandroid

The 18 Pro is the first iPhone, and the only smartphone sold here with a true third parameter: Samsung had tried it on the Galaxy S9 and S10 before giving up, Xiaomi on its 13 and 14 Ultra before abandoning it, and only Huawei maintains it on its Pura Ultra, which aren't sold here.

In full midday sun, ƒ/4 isn't always enough to hold 1/48 s and an ND remains useful, but in everything else, in shade, at the end of the day, in bright interiors, the aperture does the job alone. And in 4K, the loss of sharpness at ƒ/4 that we measured in photo is no longer visible, which makes the entire range usable. It's in video that the variable aperture justifies its motor.

Around it, everything is in place: Dolby Vision up to 4K 120 frames per second on the main sensor, sensor-shift stabilization, ProRes, Log, and above all a consistency between the three modules that no one matches, same exposure, same color science, same rendering when switching from one focal length to another mid-shot.

And this applies as much to the main sensor as to the ultra-wide, with stabilization that stays smooth even while running and skin tone rendering that I find improved in low light.

So, yes, an Android smartphone sometimes films just as well on a single shot. But I haven't found one that films as well on ten shots in a row. Which explains why the iPhone is the overwhelming favorite for video.

### Manual mode, the surprise

The other new feature, more discreet and more useful, is the Pro controls in the native app, after nineteen years of refusal. Incidentally, it's a blow to many third-party apps that built their business on it.

Aperture, shutter speed from 1/6000 to one second, white balance, manual focus, histogram, in a customizable drawer that you have to enable yourself, otherwise you never see it; Apple clearly wanted the general public not to stumble upon it by accident. On the other hand, no manual ISO, which is strange, and a Camera Control button that controls neither aperture nor shutter speed, which is absurd. Asked about ISO, Apple responds that every photo is a fusion of many exposures and that an ISO setting wouldn't make sense in that context. That's understandable for photo. In video, where there is no fusion and where you want to be at base ISO to get maximum dynamic range in Log, the excuse doesn't hold up.

But manual white balance and focus with a dial, behind a window or in the dark, are two features I'd been waiting years for and that will put half of third-party photo apps out of business. Focus tracking from a double tap, which holds a subject even if it leaves and returns to the frame, works remarkably well, including on a cat. It's at the level of a recent mirrorless camera, and on one point it does even better: when the subject leaves the frame, the iPhone doesn't refocus on what remains, it waits. Two caveats: tracking resets with each video recording stop, you have to re-tap the subject for each shot, and it's incompatible with burst mode. Cinematic mode is applied after the fact to a normal video, with mixed results.

And Apple Reference Image, the signature of photos straight from the sensor, is the good idea of the year that we don't have in Europe at launch.

## Battery life: a Max battery in a Pro

The 18 Pro's battery is 4,056 mAh, according to the European energy label, versus 3,988 on the 17 Pro. Sixty-eight milliamp-hours, which is to say nothing. And yet Apple claims that the 18 Pro lasts as long as a 17 Pro Max, and the European label, measured according to an identical protocol from one year to the next, gives 52 hours of endurance versus 47 for the 17 Pro, five more with the same battery. The entire gain comes from the 2 nm chip, the display and the modem.

In use, it's a day-and-a-half smartphone, two days if the day is quiet, with high brightness, CarPlay, Bluetooth accessories and lots of photos. The 17 Pro finished its busy days below 20%, the 18 Pro finishes them around 35. The progress comes from efficiency rather than a chemistry revolution, and it shows. This is, moreover, the whole strength of the iPhone: it's efficiency, and it makes little sense to compare battery capacity with that of a Chinese smartphone.

And there are two important French specifics. Our models keep a nano-SIM tray, and therefore a slightly smaller battery than the eSIM-only versions sold in the United States or Japan, two hours less video on the spec sheet.

In return, Apple's C2 modem equips both sizes here, while the American Pro Max keeps a Qualcomm. I measured no difference in throughput between the C2 and the 17 Pro's modem, but a network reconnection that seems faster when coming out of airplane mode, a gain I couldn't quantify over a week of testing. If you were hesitating to buy your Pro Max in New York, that's an argument for staying here.

## Network and communication: hello C2

The iPhone 18 Pro carries over Apple's in-house wireless chip N1 introduced last year: Wi-Fi 7 (802.11be) on 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz in 2×2 MIMO, Bluetooth 6 and Thread, the smart home protocol that lets the phone talk directly to Matter accessories without going through a hub. Nothing new here, but nothing to envy in the best of Android either.

The real novelty is cellular, and it's historic for a Pro: the modem is now an Apple C2, no longer a Qualcomm. After the C1 in the iPhone 16e and the C1X in the iPhone Air, this is the first time Apple has entrusted its high-end models to its own chip, everywhere in the world with one exception, the 18 Pro Max sold in the United States, which keeps a Qualcomm, no doubt to minimally honor the contract binding the two companies until March 2027.

Apple promises significantly faster uploads than on the C1X and 15% less energy, and finally supports 5G mmWave, which only concerns Americans. Here, we get 5G sub-6 GHz in 4×4 MIMO and Gigabit LTE, which is exactly the 17 Pro's spec sheet on paper.

During my test on the Orange network in Nantes, I noticed no difference in signal acquisition or throughput compared with the iPhone 16 Pro and its Qualcomm, including on a train, and hotspot sharing lasted a workday without interruption. The gain, if it exists, is hidden in battery life rather than in throughput, which is consistent with what Apple is highlighting.

The iPhone 18 Pro accepts a nano-SIM and an eSIM in France, or two eSIMs, where American models abandoned the tray two years ago. In China, the iPhone 18 Pro even has two physical SIMs.

The rest is in place: second-generation Ultra Wideband chip for precise location of AirTags and your loved ones in Find My, NFC with reader mode and Express Cards, dual-frequency GPS, and satellite connection for emergency SOS and Find My, included for two years.

The USB-C port remains USB 3 at 10 Gbit/s with DisplayPort 4K HDR output, essential for offloading ProRes or recording in ProRes RAW to an external SSD.

## Charging: a pleasant surprise

Apple raised its promise by a notch this year: the iPhone 18 Pro and 18 Pro Max recover 50% battery in about 15 minutes, versus around twenty minutes on the previous generation.

Here, with a 120 W charger and a wattmeter, the iPhone 18 Pro went from 1% to 50% in 19 minutes, 80% in 35 minutes, 98% in 63 minutes. The peak capped at 39 W, held for six minutes, before dropping in stages: 30 to 33 W until 17 minutes, 22 to 25 W until the half-hour mark, 18 to 20 W until 36 minutes, then under 10 W for the end. Over the whole session, 19.3 Wh came out of the outlet, an average of 18 W actual.

To reach maximum speed, you need a charger compatible with USB Power Delivery 3.1 and Adjustable Voltage Supply (AVS), and the power displayed on the adapter isn't enough, to the point that some much more powerful MacBook chargers can't manage it. To date, the only charger on the market that ticks these boxes is Apple's 40 W dynamic adapter, capable of going up to 60 W.
