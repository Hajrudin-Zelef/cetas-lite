# Europe wants to ban social media and AI chatbots for under-15s

## Key takeaways AI-generated summary

1. The European Commission's Kids Act bans accounts on TikTok, Instagram or YouTube before age 13, with mandatory parental consent until age 15
2. AI chatbots would be closed to under-15s, and designs fostering emotional attachment in a minor would be outright banned
3. The text also targets infinite scrolling, artificial notifications and recommendation algorithms, with fines of up to 6% of global turnover

No social media before age 13, no personal account before age 15. That's how Ursula von der Leyen summed up the Kids Act before the European Parliament, before the Commission detailed on Thursday a text that targets social media, video platforms, online games and chatbots at the same time.

The principle is tiered access based on age. Before age 13, no account possible on TikTok, Instagram or YouTube, and 3-12 year-olds should make do with services designed for children that parents control from start to finish.

Between 13 and 15, parental consent will be required to open an introductory account, a sort of restricted mini-account where contacts are limited. The draft that leaked to Euronews mentioned a cap of one hour of screen time per day. Full autonomy only comes at 15.

The Commission also lumps in AI chatbots, those virtual companions with which some teens spend entire evenings chatting. They too would be closed to under-15s. As for designs that foster emotional attachment in a minor, they would be outright banned.

On the design side, the text takes aim at infinite scrolling and the reward mechanisms that make users addicted. Artificial notifications also get the axe. Recommendation algorithms, for their part, will have to stop locking minors into spirals of ever more extreme content.

All of this obviously requires age verification. The Commission promises a European app or national solutions, without excessive collection of personal data. Platforms will have to prove they are playing by the rules and pay a supervision fee that will fund the checks. In the event of foul play, the fine can climb up to 6% of global turnover.
