# A researcher uses ChatGPT to develop a drug against schizophrenia

## Key takeaways AI-generated summary

1. Douglas Yao claims that ChatGPT designed the molecule PAC-3310, which he then manufactured in a laboratory set up in his garage
2. Tests show activity on the M4 receptor in laboratory cells and a reduction in excessive movement in hyperactive mice, without trials on patients
3. The mouse trials were not conducted blindly, a limitation acknowledged by the authors, and the efficacy of PAC-3310 in humans remains to be demonstrated

Schizophrenia is a psychiatric illness that can cause hallucinations and disrupt thinking. Douglas Yao, a researcher who uses computer science to study biology, is attempting to develop a new drug to treat it, with the help of ChatGPT.

His project focuses on a molecule called PAC-3310, in other words a chemical substance whose effects on the brain he wants to study. It could become the basis of a drug if the research succeeds, but the results presented concern only cells and mice. No patients for now.

On September 8, Yao showed a vial claiming that ChatGPT had designed this molecule and that he had then manufactured it in a laboratory set up in his garage. The artificial intelligence would therefore have proposed the chemical structure of the product, while its manufacture required laboratory work carried out by the researcher.

Yao created the company Pace Pharmaceuticals to develop this type of research. Its website presents a report on PAC-3310, which allows one to see what was actually tested behind the announcement. The garage narrative inevitably attracts attention, but what we would most like to know is whether this substance can help patients.

To understand the avenue being explored, we must return to how the brain works: its cells exchange chemical messages received by proteins called receptors. PAC-3310 seeks to activate one of them, named M4, while acting as little as possible on the others, in the hope of limiting side effects.

In their trials, the researchers observed activity on this receptor in cells cultured in the laboratory. In mice made artificially hyperactive, the substance reduced excessive movement. This result serves to explore a treatment avenue, but a mouse that moves less does not demonstrate that a patient will have fewer hallucinations.

The mouse trials were not conducted blindly: the people in charge of the tests knew which treatment each animal received. This can influence the evaluation, a limitation that the authors indicate in their report.

Drugs that act on this family of receptors already exist. In the United States, Cobenfy was authorized in September 2024 against schizophrenia in adults, after trials in patients. PAC-3310 still has to prove itself: its efficacy in humans and its safety are not established by the experiments presented.

The project deserves attention. But a demonstration with ChatGPT unfortunately does not replace the trials necessary to offer a drug to patients.
